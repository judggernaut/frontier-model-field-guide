Neural networks from the ground up: weighted sums that bend

Every model in this guide, from a spam filter to a frontier LLM, is built from one tiny part repeated billions of times: multiply some numbers by weights, add them up, and bend the result. This chapter builds that part, stacks it into layers, runs numbers through it by hand, counts what "a 70B model" actually counts, and ends with what the famous universal approximation theorem does and does not promise. The next chapter, how networks learn, covers how the weights get their values.

Should I go for a run?

One artificial neuron, told as a decision you make most mornings.

You wake up and know three things, each written as a number: how sunny it is, how much free time you have, and the chance of rain. A neuron's job is to turn a handful of numbers like these into one number: go, or not.

Each input gets a weight that says how much it matters and in which direction. Rain counts against you, strongly. The bias is a constant nudge that does not depend on the inputs at all: your general mood before you even look outside.

Add up the weighted inputs and the bias: 1.8 + 1.5 − 2.1 − 0.5 = 0.7. That single number, the weighted sum, is the neuron's raw opinion. So far it is nothing more than a spreadsheet formula.

Last, the activation function bends the sum. The most common one, ReLU, keeps positive numbers and turns negatives into zero. On a grey morning (sunshine 0.3, rain 1.0) the sum is −1.4 and the neuron outputs 0: stay in. That bend looks trivial. The next section shows it is the whole trick.

That is the whole neuron: inputs times weights, plus a bias, then a bend. The weights and the bias are the parameters. You picked them here from common sense. A neural network starts with random ones and adjusts them, billions at a time, until its outputs match the data.

A single neuron

Plain wordsA tiny decision maker

A neuron is a very small decision maker. It takes a few numbers in, decides how much each one matters, adds them up with a constant nudge, and gives one number out. The "how much each one matters" numbers are called weights, the constant nudge is the bias, and the final squish or bend is the activation function.

The name comes from a loose analogy with brain cells, which also combine many incoming signals and either fire or stay quiet. The analogy stops there. An artificial neuron is two lines of arithmetic, and nobody designs them by hand: a network starts with random weights and the training process tunes them.

For leadersWhy "neuron" is a misleading unit of analysis

It is tempting to imagine each neuron in an LLM as a little expert with a job: a "French neuron" or a "sarcasm neuron". Some units do behave that way. Most do not. Interpretability research finds that individual units usually respond to several unrelated things at once (they are polysemantic), because a network can store more concepts than it has units by spreading each concept across many of them. Anthropic's "Toy Models of Superposition" (Elhage et al., 2022) studies this in small models, and "Towards Monosemanticity" (Bricken et al., 2023) recovers cleaner features with sparse dictionary learning on top of the units.

Practical consequence: claims like "we found the neuron for X and turned it off" deserve scrutiny. Ask whether the intervention was checked for side effects on unrelated behaviour, and whether the unit is a feature found by a method such as a sparse autoencoder or a raw neuron.

Under the hoodThe formula

A neuron with inputs \(\mathbf{x} \in \mathbb{R}^n\), weights \(\mathbf{w} \in \mathbb{R}^n\), bias \(b\), and activation \(\phi\) computes

\[ y = \phi\Big(\sum_{i=1}^{n} w_i x_i + b\Big) = \phi(\mathbf{w}^\top \mathbf{x} + b) \]\(z = \mathbf{w}^\top\mathbf{x} + b\) is the pre-activation. With \(\phi\) = identity this is linear regression; with \(\phi\) = sigmoid it is logistic regression. A neuron is a regression model with a bend at the end.

Geometrically, \(\mathbf{w}^\top\mathbf{x} + b = 0\) is a hyperplane (a line, in two dimensions). The neuron measures signed distance from that hyperplane, scaled by \(\|\mathbf{w}\|\), and the activation decides what to do with it. A ReLU neuron is "off" on one side of its hyperplane and grows linearly on the other.

Why the bend matters: non-linear activations

Plain wordsConverting units twice is still converting units

Convert miles to kilometres, then kilometres to metres. Two steps, but you could have done it in one: multiply by 1,609. Chaining "multiply and add" steps always collapses into a single "multiply and add" step. A stack of a hundred neurons with no bend is exactly as powerful as one neuron. It can only draw straight lines.

The activation function breaks that. ReLU's corner at zero means a neuron is off for some inputs and on for others. Combine many of those corners and you can trace curves, bumps, and any shape you like. Without the bend, depth buys nothing.

For leadersA settled choice, mostly

Activation functions were a lively research topic for a decade. For LLMs the question is largely settled: GELU (BERT, GPT-2) and the gated variant SwiGLU (PaLM, LLaMA and most open models since) are standard. Switching between modern choices gives small, real gains in quality per unit of compute (Shazeer, 2020, reported SwiGLU among the best of several gated variants on transformer language modelling) and is not where a frontier program finds its next big win.

What still matters: some activations interact with numerical precision and quantization (large activation outliers make 8-bit and 4-bit inference harder), and gated variants add a third weight matrix to each MLP block, which changes parameter counts and memory planning.

Watch out
A proposal that claims a big quality jump from a new activation function alone, measured on one small model, usually shrinks or vanishes at scale. Ask for the comparison at matched compute across at least two model sizes, with several seeds at the smallest size.

Reading a handwritten 7

How layers build complex features out of simple ones. Simplified, but true to what trained image networks do.

To the network, a handwritten 7 is a grid of brightness numbers: 56 of them here, about 150,000 for a small colour photo. Nothing in the input says "line" or "corner". Whatever structure the network uses, it has to build itself.

The first layer's units each respond to something tiny and local, such as "bright above, dark below" (a horizontal edge) or a slanted edge. Nobody told them to. When researchers visualise what trained image networks respond to, the first layer is reliably full of edge and colour detectors (Zeiler and Fergus, 2014).

The next layer's inputs are the first layer's outputs. A unit there can fire for "edges in a row near the top" or "a bar meeting a slant at a sharp corner". It is the same weighted-sum-and-bend neuron as before; only its inputs got more meaningful.

The last layer weighs the parts: a top bar plus a sharp corner and no closed loop looks like a 7, not a 9. Each layer reuses the features below it, which is why depth is so efficient: a corner detector built once serves every digit that has a corner.

Language models show a similar ordering. Probing studies of BERT found that information about parts of speech peaks in earlier layers, sentence structure in the middle, and harder semantic relations like coreference later (Tenney et al., 2019). The boundaries are blurry, but the build-up from simple to complex is real.

Layers: from simple features to complex ones

Plain wordsAn assembly line for ideas

A layer is a row of neurons that all look at the same inputs. The outputs of one layer become the inputs of the next, like stations on an assembly line. The first station handles raw material (pixels, or pieces of words). Each later station works with what the previous one made, so the "parts" get bigger and more meaningful as you go: edges, then shapes, then objects.

The layers in the middle are called hidden layers because you never see their outputs directly. You only see the input you gave and the answer at the end.

For leadersDepth, width, and where to read more

Two knobs shape a network: depth (how many layers) and width (how many units per layer). Depth lets the network compose features, reusing simple parts to build complex ones. Width gives each layer more features to work with. Frontier LLMs have tens to over a hundred layers and widths in the thousands to tens of thousands. The ratio is a design choice that scaling-law work has found to matter much less than total size, within a broad sensible range (Kaplan et al., 2020).

For how a transformer specifically arranges its layers (attention blocks and MLP blocks), see architecture and inside an LLM. For the different model families built from these parts, see the model family tree.

Under the hoodA layer is a matrix multiply

Stack the weight vectors of a layer's \(m\) neurons as rows of a matrix \(W \in \mathbb{R}^{m \times n}\). Then the whole layer is

\[ \mathbf{h}^{(\ell)} = \phi\big(W^{(\ell)}\,\mathbf{h}^{(\ell-1)} + \mathbf{b}^{(\ell)}\big), \qquad \mathbf{h}^{(0)} = \mathbf{x} \]\(\phi\) is applied to each element. A network of \(L\) layers is the composition \(f(\mathbf{x}) = f_L \circ \cdots \circ f_1(\mathbf{x})\). The last layer usually skips the activation (regression) or applies softmax (classification, including next-token prediction).

This is why GPUs matter: a layer is one matrix multiply plus an element-wise function, and matrix multiplies are what GPU hardware is built for. Process a batch of inputs at once and the matrix-vector product becomes a matrix-matrix product, which uses the hardware far more efficiently.

Why depth helps, formally: some functions that a deep network represents with a modest number of units need exponentially many units in a shallow one. Telgarsky (2016) constructs such functions for ReLU networks. Depth is not just a convenience; it changes what is cheap to represent.

Following the numbers through a tiny network

A forward pass by hand: estimating a delivery time from distance and number of stops. The weights are made up; training would set them.

Two inputs (2 km away, 1 stop), a hidden layer of two ReLU neurons, and one output neuron with no bend. Every connection has a weight, and every neuron after the inputs has a bias. Green weights push up, orange ones push down.

Hidden unit 1 takes a weighted sum of both inputs plus its bias: 1.0 × 2 + 0.5 × 1 − 1.0 = 1.5. That is positive, so ReLU passes it through unchanged. Think of h₁ as a rough "how much driving" signal.

Hidden unit 2: −1.0 × 2 + 1.0 × 1 + 0.5 = −0.5. Negative, so ReLU outputs 0 and the unit is switched off for this input. It might switch on for a short trip with many stops. Different inputs switch on different units; that is how the network carves up the input space.

The output neuron weighs the hidden units: 20 × 1.5 − 10 × 0 + 5 = 35 minutes. It has no ReLU, so it can output any number. Input to output, left to right, one layer at a time: that is a forward pass. It is the only thing a network does when you use it.

Six weights plus three biases: this network has 9 parameters. A frontier model is the same arithmetic with billions of parameters and dozens of layers. "A 70B model" means 70 billion of these numbers.

The forward pass

Plain wordsNumbers flow left to right

Running a network is called a forward pass. You put numbers in on the left, each layer does its weighted sums and bends, and the answer comes out on the right. No thinking happens elsewhere; the forward pass is the whole computation. When you chat with an LLM, every single word it writes is the result of one forward pass through the full network.

For leadersWhy forward passes set the serving bill

Because each generated token needs a full forward pass, serving cost scales with parameter count times tokens generated. A rule of thumb from Kaplan et al. (2020): a forward pass costs about 2 floating-point operations per parameter per token (one multiply and one add), ignoring the attention term that grows with context length. A 70B dense model therefore needs roughly 140 billion operations for every token it writes. Training costs about three times that per token, since the backward pass (next chapter) costs about twice the forward pass.

That arithmetic drives the business case for distillation, mixture-of-experts, and quantization, covered in inference.

Under the hoodThe same pass in code

The story's network, written as matrices: \(W_1 = \begin{pmatrix}1.0 & 0.5\\ -1.0 & 1.0\end{pmatrix}\), \(\mathbf{b}_1 = (-1.0, 0.5)\), \(\mathbf{w}_2 = (20, -10)\), \(b_2 = 5\).

import numpy as np

x = np.array([2.0, 1.0])                  # distance in km, number of stops
W1 = np.array([[1.0, 0.5],                # hidden unit 1's weights
               [-1.0, 1.0]])              # hidden unit 2's weights
b1 = np.array([-1.0, 0.5])
w2 = np.array([20.0, -10.0])              # output weights
b2 = 5.0

def relu(z):
    return np.maximum(z, 0.0)             # keep positives, zero out negatives

z = W1 @ x + b1                           # both hidden weighted sums in one matrix multiply
h = relu(z)                               # the bend
y = w2 @ h + b2                           # output layer: weighted sum, no bend
print(f"z = {z[0]:.1f}, {z[1]:.1f}")      # z = 1.5, -0.5
print(f"h = {h[0]:.1f}, {h[1]:.1f}")      # h = 1.5, 0.0
print(f"y = {y:.1f} minutes")             # y = 35.0 minutes
print(W1.size + b1.size + w2.size + 1)    # 9 parameters
fn relu(z: f64) -> f64 {
    z.max(0.0) // keep positives, zero out negatives
}

fn main() {
    let x = [2.0, 1.0]; // distance in km, number of stops
    let w1 = [[1.0, 0.5], // hidden unit 1's weights
              [-1.0, 1.0]]; // hidden unit 2's weights
    let b1 = [-1.0, 0.5];
    let w2 = [20.0, -10.0]; // output weights
    let b2 = 5.0;

    // both hidden weighted sums (a matrix multiply, written out)
    let z: Vec<f64> = (0..2).map(|i| w1[i][0] * x[0] + w1[i][1] * x[1] + b1[i]).collect();
    let h: Vec<f64> = z.iter().map(|&v| relu(v)).collect(); // the bend
    let y = w2[0] * h[0] + w2[1] * h[1] + b2; // output layer: weighted sum, no bend
    println!("z = {:.1}, {:.1}", z[0], z[1]); // z = 1.5, -0.5
    println!("h = {:.1}, {:.1}", h[0], h[1]); // h = 1.5, 0.0
    println!("y = {:.1} minutes", y); // y = 35.0 minutes
    println!("{}", 4 + b1.len() + w2.len() + 1); // 9 parameters
}

Parameters: what "a 70B model" counts

Plain wordsKnobs on a giant mixing desk

Picture a sound engineer's mixing desk with a knob for every connection in the network. Each knob is a parameter: a weight or a bias. "A 70B model" has 70 billion knobs. Training is the process of turning all of them, a tiny bit at a time, until the network's outputs sound right.

More knobs means the network can capture more patterns, the same way a bigger desk can mix a more complex song. It also means more memory to store the knobs and more arithmetic every time the network runs.

For leadersWhat the number does and does not tell you

Parameter count is the single most quoted number about a model, and it answers three practical questions well:

  • Memory. Each parameter takes 2 bytes at 16-bit precision. A 70B model needs about 140 GB just to hold its weights, before any working memory, which is why it does not fit on one 80 GB GPU without quantization.
  • Serving compute. About 2 operations per parameter per generated token for a dense model.
  • Training compute. About 6 operations per parameter per training token (Kaplan et al., 2020).

It answers capability badly on its own. Chinchilla (Hoffmann et al., 2022), at 70B parameters trained on 1.4 trillion tokens, beat the 280B-parameter Gopher trained on 300 billion tokens. Data volume and quality, training recipe, and post-training all matter as much as size; see scaling laws.

Mixture-of-experts models complicate the count. Only some "expert" sub-networks run for each token, so they quote total parameters (memory) and active parameters (compute per token) separately. Mixtral 8x7B (Mistral AI, 2024) has about 47B total and about 13B active.

Principal asks
Is that total or active parameters? What precision will it serve at, and what does the weight memory come to on our hardware? How many training tokens per parameter, and how does that compare with the compute-optimal ratio? If two models differ in size, were they trained on the same data and compute, or is size standing in for everything else that differs?
Under the hoodCounting a real transformer

For a decoder-only transformer, almost every parameter lives in weight matrices inside the repeated blocks. Using the published Llama 2 70B shape (Touvron et al., 2023): width 8,192, 80 layers, MLP width 28,672, 64 query heads sharing 8 key/value heads (grouped-query attention), and a 32,000-token vocabulary:

def count_params(d, layers, ffn, vocab, heads, kv_heads):
    head_dim = d // heads
    attn = 2 * d * d + 2 * d * kv_heads * head_dim   # Q and output full size; K and V shrunk by grouped-query attention
    mlp = 3 * d * ffn                                # SwiGLU has three matrices: gate, up, down
    norms = 2 * d                                    # two RMSNorm scale vectors per layer
    embed = 2 * vocab * d                            # input embedding plus a separate output head
    return layers * (attn + mlp + norms) + embed + d # + the final norm

n = count_params(d=8192, layers=80, ffn=28672, vocab=32000, heads=64, kv_heads=8)
print(f"{n:,}")                                                            # 68,976,648,192
print(f"{n / 1e9:.1f}B parameters, {2 * n / 1e9:.0f} GB at 2 bytes each")  # 69.0B parameters, 138 GB at 2 bytes each
fn count_params(d: u64, layers: u64, ffn: u64, vocab: u64, heads: u64, kv_heads: u64) -> u64 {
    let head_dim = d / heads;
    let attn = 2 * d * d + 2 * d * kv_heads * head_dim; // Q and output full size; K and V shrunk by grouped-query attention
    let mlp = 3 * d * ffn; // SwiGLU has three matrices: gate, up, down
    let norms = 2 * d; // two RMSNorm scale vectors per layer
    let embed = 2 * vocab * d; // input embedding plus a separate output head
    layers * (attn + mlp + norms) + embed + d // + the final norm
}

/// Formats 68976648192 as "68,976,648,192", like Python's f"{n:,}".
fn with_commas(n: u64) -> String {
    let s = n.to_string();
    let mut out = String::new();
    for (i, c) in s.chars().enumerate() {
        if i > 0 && (s.len() - i) % 3 == 0 {
            out.push(',');
        }
        out.push(c);
    }
    out
}

fn main() {
    let n = count_params(8192, 80, 28672, 32000, 64, 8);
    println!("{}", with_commas(n)); // 68,976,648,192
    let nf = n as f64;
    println!("{:.1}B parameters, {:.0} GB at 2 bytes each", nf / 1e9, 2.0 * nf / 1e9); // 69.0B parameters, 138 GB at 2 bytes each
}

The breakdown is worth remembering: the MLP blocks hold about 82% of the parameters (56.4B), attention about 17.5% (12.1B), and embeddings plus norms under 1%. Biases are absent: most modern LLMs drop them from linear layers because they add little. "70B" is a rounded marketing number for 68.98 billion.

Tracing a day of electricity demand with hinges

Why a network with enough ReLU units can draw any curve. The demand curve is illustrative; the fits are real least-squares fits.

A grid operator wants a network that takes the hour of the day and outputs expected demand: low overnight, a morning peak, a midday plateau, a big evening peak. One input, one output, one hidden layer of ReLU units. How many units does it take?

A single ReLU unit is a hinge: flat, then a straight ramp starting at its threshold (here about 5:00). Scaled and shifted by the output weight and bias, the best one hinge can do is a crude "demand rises during the day". Average miss: 2.9 GW.

Add a second hinge with a negative output weight and the ramp bends back down at about 8:30. Every extra unit adds one more corner where the slope can change. Average miss: 2.2 GW.

With six hinges the network can follow the night low, both peaks, and the plateau, as a sequence of straight segments. It looks like a child's drawing of the curve, but it already has the right shape. Average miss: 0.9 GW.

Twenty hinges and the fit is within 0.1 GW almost everywhere. Keep adding units and the error keeps falling toward zero. That is the universal approximation idea in one picture: enough bends can trace any reasonable curve. It says nothing about whether training will find those bends, or what the network does at hours it never saw.

Universal approximation: what it promises and what it does not

Plain wordsEnough bends can draw anything

The universal approximation theorem says that a network with one hidden layer and enough units can imitate any smooth-enough curve as closely as you like. The story above is the whole idea: each unit adds a bend, and enough bends can trace any shape.

It is a statement that a good setting of the knobs exists. It does not say how many knobs you need (possibly an astronomical number), whether training will find the right setting, or whether the network will give sensible answers for inputs unlike its training data. Those three gaps are where all the real work in machine learning happens.

For leadersAn argument you will hear misused

"Neural networks are universal approximators, so the model can learn this" is a common and empty argument. The theorem guarantees representation only. Whether a capability is learned depends on three things it is silent about: whether the data contains the signal, whether optimization finds it (next chapter), and whether what was learned generalizes beyond the training examples.

The widget below makes the third gap visible. Switch to "20 noisy points" and add units: error on the training points keeps falling while error against the true curve stops improving and gets worse. Fitting the data you have is not the same as learning the pattern that produced it.

Watch out
Universal approximation is an existence theorem. It is often cited to dismiss a concern ("a big enough model can represent that"). The useful questions are about data, optimization, and generalization, and the theorem answers none of them.
Under the hoodThe statement and its fine print

For any continuous \(f\) on a compact set \(K \subset \mathbb{R}^n\) and any \(\varepsilon > 0\), there exist \(N\) and parameters \(v_j, b_j \in \mathbb{R}\), \(\mathbf{w}_j \in \mathbb{R}^n\) such that

\[ \sup_{\mathbf{x}\in K}\; \Big|\, f(\mathbf{x}) - \sum_{j=1}^{N} v_j\, \phi(\mathbf{w}_j^\top\mathbf{x} + b_j) \Big| < \varepsilon \]Proved for sigmoid activations by Cybenko (1989) and Hornik, Stinchcombe and White (1989). Leshno et al. (1993) showed it holds exactly when \(\phi\) is not a polynomial, which covers ReLU, tanh and GELU.

The fine print:

  • No bound on \(N\). For general continuous functions the number of units can grow exponentially with input dimension. Barron (1993) showed that for functions whose Fourier transform has a bounded first moment, squared error falls like \(1/N\) regardless of dimension, which is one reason smooth high-dimensional targets are tractable.
  • In one dimension, ReLU networks are piecewise-linear functions. \(g(x) = a + \sum_j v_j \max(0, x - t_j)\) has a kink at each \(t_j\). Linear interpolation of a twice-differentiable \(f\) with kink spacing \(h\) has error at most \(h^2 \max|f''|/8\), so error falls like \(1/N^2\) when the kinks are well placed.
  • Depth changes the economics. Telgarsky (2016) gives functions that a deep ReLU network computes with few units but any shallow one needs exponentially many units to approximate.
  • Nothing about learning or generalization. The theorem is silent on whether gradient descent reaches a good \(\{v_j, \mathbf{w}_j, b_j\}\), and on behaviour off the training data.

Eval lens
The two error cards are the whole story of evaluation in miniature. "Error on training data" is what the optimizer sees and drives down; "error against true curve" is what you care about, and it can only be measured on data the model was not fitted to. Key idea
A neural network is weighted sums with bends, stacked. The weights are the parameters; the bends make depth worth having; the forward pass is the entire computation. Everything else in this guide, from attention to RLHF, is about how to arrange those parts and how to set the weights.
