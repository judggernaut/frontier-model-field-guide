DPO and offline preference methods: RLHF without the RL loop

Direct Preference Optimization (Rafailov et al. 2023) showed that the KL-regularised RLHF objective from the previous chapter can be optimized with a simple classification loss on preference pairs. No reward model at training time, no sampling, no value function, no PPO. It became the default post-training method for open models within a year, and it spawned a family of variants. Understanding exactly what DPO optimizes, and where that differs from RLHF in practice, is one of the most useful pieces of judgment in post-training.

This one, not that one

How DPO learns straight from pairs, without hiring a judge.

RLHF turns preference pairs into a reward model, then samples answers, scores them, and runs PPO with four networks in memory. It works, but it is slow and fragile.

DPO shows the policy each pair directly. Training looks like ordinary supervised learning: a fixed dataset, one forward and backward pass per pair, and only the policy plus a frozen copy of its starting point in memory.

Every pair carries one instruction: nudge the picked answer up and the rejected answer down, measured against how the model wrote before training. That comparison to the old self is the leash, built straight into the loss.

DPO's trick is that the model's own shift in probability, relative to its old self, works as a reward score. The loss only looks at the gap between the picked and rejected shifts, which is why the paper's subtitle is "your language model is secretly a reward model". Numbers are illustrative.

Each pair's push is weighted by how wrong the model currently is about it. A pair the model ranks backwards gets a strong correction; one it already ranks clearly right gets a small one. β controls how fast that weight falls as the margin grows.

The idea

Plain wordsSkip the middleman

RLHF works in two stages: first train a judge on people's picks, then train the student against the judge. DPO skips the judge. It shows the student each pair directly and says: make the picked answer a bit more likely and the rejected answer a bit less likely, compared with how you used to write. The comparison to how it used to write is the leash, built straight into the lesson.

For leadersWhy it spread, and what you give up

DPO trains like supervised learning: fixed dataset, one forward and backward pass per pair, two models in memory (the policy and a frozen reference, whose log-probabilities can even be precomputed). It is cheaper, far more stable, and easier to debug than PPO. Llama 3 (Meta 2024), Zephyr, and Tülu are well-known examples of post-training pipelines that relied on DPO.

What you give up is the online loop. PPO learns from the model's own fresh samples, scored on the fly. Plain DPO learns from a fixed set of pairs, often generated by other models. The policy is never corrected on its own new mistakes, and its ceiling is set by the pairs you collected. Much of the work since 2023 is about recovering the benefits of online data while keeping DPO's simplicity.

Principal asks
Where did the pairs come from: this model's samples, an older model, or another lab's model? How were they labeled, and what is the label agreement? Did the chosen responses' log-likelihood go up or down during training? Is the win rate length-controlled? Did you compare against one round of online or iterative DPO?

The derivation, step by step

Under the hoodFrom the RLHF objective to a classification loss

Step 1. Start from the RLHF objective for a fixed prompt \(x\):

\[ \max_{\pi}\; \mathbb{E}_{y \sim \pi}\big[r(x,y)\big] - \beta\, \mathbb{D}_{\mathrm{KL}}\big(\pi(\cdot\mid x)\,\|\,\pi_{\text{ref}}(\cdot\mid x)\big) \]

Step 2. Rewrite it as a single KL divergence. Define \(\pi^*(y\mid x) = \pi_{\text{ref}}(y\mid x)\, e^{r(x,y)/\beta} / Z(x)\). Expanding the KL and collecting terms:

\[ \mathbb{E}_{y\sim\pi}\big[r\big] - \beta\,\mathbb{E}_{y\sim\pi}\Big[\log \tfrac{\pi(y\mid x)}{\pi_{\text{ref}}(y\mid x)}\Big] = -\beta\, \mathbb{E}_{y\sim\pi}\Big[\log \tfrac{\pi(y\mid x)}{\pi_{\text{ref}}(y\mid x)\, e^{r(x,y)/\beta}}\Big] = -\beta\, \mathbb{D}_{\mathrm{KL}}\big(\pi \,\|\, \pi^*\big) + \beta \log Z(x) \]\(Z(x)\) does not depend on \(\pi\), and a KL divergence is minimized (at zero) exactly when the two distributions match. So the optimal policy is \(\pi^*\).

Step 3. Invert: express the reward through the optimal policy. Take logs of the definition of \(\pi^*\) and solve for \(r\):

\[ r(x, y) = \beta \log \frac{\pi^*(y\mid x)}{\pi_{\text{ref}}(y\mid x)} + \beta \log Z(x) \]

Step 4. Substitute into Bradley–Terry. Preferences depend only on reward differences between two responses to the same prompt, so the intractable \(\beta \log Z(x)\) cancels:

\[ P(y_w \succ y_l \mid x) = \sigma\Big(\beta \log \frac{\pi^*(y_w\mid x)}{\pi_{\text{ref}}(y_w\mid x)} - \beta \log \frac{\pi^*(y_l\mid x)}{\pi_{\text{ref}}(y_l\mid x)}\Big) \]

Step 5. Parameterize \(\pi^*\) with the model \(\pi_\theta\) and maximize likelihood of the observed preferences:

\[ \mathcal{L}_{\text{DPO}}(\theta) = -\,\mathbb{E}_{(x, y_w, y_l)}\Big[\log \sigma\Big(\beta \log \frac{\pi_\theta(y_w\mid x)}{\pi_{\text{ref}}(y_w\mid x)} - \beta \log \frac{\pi_\theta(y_l\mid x)}{\pi_{\text{ref}}(y_l\mid x)}\Big)\Big] \]Fitting this loss is fitting a reward model whose form is fixed to \(\hat r_\theta(x,y) = \beta \log \frac{\pi_\theta(y\mid x)}{\pi_{\text{ref}}(y\mid x)}\), the implicit reward. The policy and the reward model are the same network. "Your language model is secretly a reward model" is the paper's subtitle.

Note what the derivation assumes: the Bradley–Terry model is right, the optimum over all policies is reachable, and the data covers the responses the policy will produce. Each assumption fails in practice in a specific way, covered below.

What the gradient does

For leadersWhat β means in DPO

β plays the same role as in RLHF: the strength of the leash to the reference. In DPO it also sets how far the margin must grow before a pair stops contributing. Typical values sit between 0.01 and 0.5, and results are sensitive to it, so a sweep over β is part of any serious DPO experiment. If a team reports one DPO run at one β, the result tells you about that β, not about DPO.

Under the hoodA weighted push and pull
\[ \nabla_\theta \mathcal{L}_{\text{DPO}} = -\beta\, \mathbb{E}\Big[\underbrace{\sigma\big(\hat r_\theta(x, y_l) - \hat r_\theta(x, y_w)\big)}_{\text{high when the model ranks the pair wrongly}}\Big(\nabla_\theta \log \pi_\theta(y_w\mid x) - \nabla_\theta \log \pi_\theta(y_l\mid x)\Big)\Big] \]Raise the chosen response, lower the rejected one, weighted by how wrong the implicit reward currently is. Pairs already ranked correctly by a wide margin get small weight but never zero.

The gradient only constrains the difference between the two log-likelihoods. Nothing in it says the chosen response's probability must rise. That single fact explains the most important DPO failure mode.

import torch.nn.functional as F

def dpo_loss(pi_logp_w, pi_logp_l, ref_logp_w, ref_logp_l, beta=0.1):
    """Each argument: summed log-prob of the full response under the policy or the frozen
       reference, shape [batch]. Sum only over response tokens, never the prompt."""
    h = (pi_logp_w - ref_logp_w) - (pi_logp_l - ref_logp_l)   # log-ratio margin in nats
    loss = F.softplus(-beta * h).mean()                        # == -log sigmoid(beta * h)
    reward_acc = (h > 0).float().mean()                        # "implicit reward accuracy"
    return loss, reward_acc

# two pairs: the first is ranked correctly (h = 2.0), the second wrongly (h = -2.5)
# loss = 0.712; per-pair gradient weights sigma(-beta*h) = 0.45 and 0.562

Failure modes

The race where both runners slow down

Likelihood displacement: how DPO can widen the gap while making both answers less likely.

Two runners start side by side: the chosen answer and the rejected answer. A runner further right is an answer the model is more likely to write.

DPO's loss is a coach who only measures the gap between the two runners. It never checks where either one is on the track.

In a healthy run, the chosen runner moves ahead and the rejected runner falls back. The gap grows and both moves are the ones you wanted.

The trap: both runners fall back, the rejected one faster. The gap grows just the same, so the loss falls and reward accuracy looks perfect, yet the model is now less likely to write the answer people picked.

Probability has to go somewhere. It flows to answers that were in neither pair, which can be harmless or can be a new kind of bad output. Pal et al. (2024) and Razin et al. (2024) documented this; it is worst when the two answers are very similar.

So watch where the runners are, not just the gap: the absolute log-likelihood of chosen answers, response length, and a held-out win rate. The default dashboard metrics, reward accuracy and margins, look the same in a healthy run and a broken one.

Plain wordsWinning the comparison by making both worse

Suppose you tell a student "write more like essay A than essay B". The student can do that by improving toward A. Or by drifting away from both, as long as it drifts further from B. Both satisfy the instruction. DPO only checks the gap between the two, so sometimes it takes the second route, and the probability goes to answers that neither essay resembles.

For leadersThe four ways DPO disappoints

Likelihood displacement

Chosen and rejected log-likelihoods both fall during training, while the margin and the "reward accuracy" metric look excellent. Pal et al. (2024, "Smaug", which proposed DPO-Positive) and Razin et al. (2024) documented this; it is worst when chosen and rejected responses are similar, which is exactly what careful pairwise labeling tends to produce.

Length exploitation

If chosen responses are longer on average, increasing length is an easy way to widen the margin. DPO-trained models often get wordier. Length-normalized variants (SimPO) and explicit length regularization address it.

Off-policy data

Pairs sampled from a different model teach the policy about someone else's mistakes. Several 2024 studies (for example Tajwar et al. 2024) found on-policy or iteratively refreshed data clearly outperforms static off-policy pairs.

Overfitting deterministic preferences

If a pair is always preferred one way, Bradley–Terry implies an infinite reward gap, and DPO keeps pushing the rejected response's probability toward zero regardless of β. The leash stops working. This was the motivation for IPO.

Watch out
Implicit reward accuracy and margins on the training set are the metrics DPO dashboards show by default, and they are the least informative ones. They rise in healthy runs and in runs suffering likelihood displacement alike. Track the absolute log-likelihood of chosen responses, average response length, and a held-out win rate.

The family

Under the hoodFive variants and the problem each fixes

IPO (Azar et al. 2023) replaces the log-sigmoid with a squared loss around a finite target margin, so the policy stops pushing once a pair is separated by enough. With \(h = \log \frac{\pi_\theta(y_w)\,\pi_{\text{ref}}(y_l)}{\pi_\theta(y_l)\,\pi_{\text{ref}}(y_w)}\):

\[ \mathcal{L}_{\text{IPO}} = \mathbb{E}\Big[\big(h - \tfrac{1}{2\tau}\big)^2\Big] \]

KTO (Ethayarajh et al. 2024) needs no pairs at all, only responses labeled desirable or undesirable, which is the shape of thumbs-up and thumbs-down product data. It applies a prospect-theory value function to the implicit reward relative to a reference point \(z_0\) (an estimate of the KL between policy and reference), with separate weights for gains and losses:

\[ v(x,y) = \begin{cases} \lambda_D\, \sigma\big(\beta(r_\theta(x,y) - z_0)\big) & y \text{ desirable} \\ \lambda_U\, \sigma\big(\beta(z_0 - r_\theta(x,y))\big) & y \text{ undesirable} \end{cases}, \qquad \mathcal{L}_{\text{KTO}} = \mathbb{E}\big[\lambda_y - v(x,y)\big] \]Here \(r_\theta(x,y) = \log\frac{\pi_\theta(y\mid x)}{\pi_\text{ref}(y\mid x)}\) is the unscaled log-ratio (β appears once, outside), and \(z_0\) is the policy-to-reference KL in the same units.

ORPO (Hong et al. 2024) drops the reference model and merges SFT and preference learning into one stage: the usual next-token loss on the chosen response plus an odds-ratio penalty, where \(\mathrm{odds}_\theta(y\mid x) = \frac{P_\theta(y\mid x)}{1 - P_\theta(y\mid x)}\) and \(P_\theta\) is the length-normalized sequence likelihood:

\[ \mathcal{L}_{\text{ORPO}} = \mathcal{L}_{\text{SFT}}(y_w) - \lambda\, \log \sigma\Big(\log \frac{\mathrm{odds}_\theta(y_w\mid x)}{\mathrm{odds}_\theta(y_l\mid x)}\Big) \]

SimPO (Meng et al. 2024) also drops the reference model, uses the length-normalized average log-probability as the implicit reward (removing the incentive to grow length), and requires a target margin \(\gamma\):

\[ \mathcal{L}_{\text{SimPO}} = -\,\mathbb{E}\Big[\log \sigma\Big(\tfrac{\beta}{|y_w|}\log \pi_\theta(y_w\mid x) - \tfrac{\beta}{|y_l|}\log \pi_\theta(y_l\mid x) - \gamma\Big)\Big] \]

Online and iterative DPO keeps the DPO loss but refreshes the data: sample responses from the current policy, label pairs with a reward model or an LLM judge, train a round, repeat. Guo et al. (2024) found that online AI feedback outperformed both offline DPO-style training and RLHF baselines in their human evaluations. In practice, iterative DPO with a strong judge is now a common middle ground between offline DPO and full RL.

Eval lens
DPO results are easy to overstate. Ask for: win rate against the starting model using a judge validated against humans (LLM as judge), length-controlled (AlpacaEval 2's length-controlled win rate, Dubois et al. 2024, is the best-known public example); a β sweep, not one run; absolute chosen log-likelihood over training; and a capability regression suite. If the only evidence is training-set reward accuracy, there is no evidence yet.
