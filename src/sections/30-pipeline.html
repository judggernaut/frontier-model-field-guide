The training pipeline, end to end

A frontier model is not trained once. It is assembled in stages, and each stage changes different things about the model. If you know what each stage buys, you can usually guess where a problem came from before anyone opens a log.

DataCrawl, filter, dedup, mix
PretrainingNext-token prediction on trillions of tokens
Mid-trainingAnnealing, long context, domain boosts
SFTDemonstrations of the format and behaviour
Preference and RLRLHF, DPO, RL on verifiable rewards
SafetyRefusals, policy, red-team fixes

Plain wordsSchool, then apprenticeship, then coaching

Pretraining is like reading a whole library. The model learns language, facts, and how ideas connect, but it has no manners and does not know it is supposed to help anyone. It just continues whatever text you hand it.

Supervised fine-tuning (SFT) is an apprenticeship. The model watches thousands of examples of a good assistant answering questions and learns to copy that style.

Preference tuning and reinforcement learning (RL) are coaching. The model tries answers, someone or something scores them, and the model shifts toward the answers that score well. This is where it learns to be helpful, careful, and, for reasoning models, to think through a problem step by step.

Most raw knowledge comes from the library stage. Most of the personality and the polish comes from the later stages.

Compute shares are illustrative. They vary by lab and by year, and the RL share has been rising since reasoning models arrived in late 2024.

Two things matter for planning. First, pretraining decisions are the most expensive to reverse: a tokenizer or a data mixture is baked into a run that may cost tens of millions of dollars. Post-training is cheap enough to iterate weekly. Second, post-training mostly elicits capability rather than creating it. If the base model cannot do something even with good prompting and many samples, no amount of SFT will make it reliable. RL on verifiable tasks is the partial exception, which is why it gets so much attention (see GRPO and RL with verifiable rewards).

Principal asks
  • Is this a capability problem (pretraining) or a behaviour problem (post-training)? What evidence separates the two?
  • Does the base model show the skill at pass@k? If not, why do we expect fine-tuning to create it?
  • Which eval would have caught this one stage earlier, where it is cheaper to fix?
  • What did this stage cost in compute and in people, and what is the next most valuable use of that budget?
Under the hoodObjectives by stage

Each stage optimizes a different objective, which is why each stage fails differently.

StageObjectiveData
Pretraining\(\min_\theta -\sum_t \log p_\theta(x_t \mid x_{<t})\) over all tokensTrillions of web, code, book, and synthetic tokens
Mid-trainingSame loss, different data: high-quality and long documents, often with the learning rate decaying to zeroBillions to hundreds of billions of curated tokens
SFTSame loss, but only on response tokens: \(-\sum_{t \in \text{response}} \log p_\theta(y_t \mid x, y_{<t})\)Thousands to millions of prompt and response pairs
RLHF / RL\(\max_\theta \mathbb{E}_{y \sim \pi_\theta}[r(x,y)] - \beta\,\mathrm{KL}(\pi_\theta \,\|\, \pi_{\text{ref}})\)Prompts plus a reward model or a verifier
DPO and relativesA classification loss on preferred versus rejected responsesPreference pairs

The KL term in the RL objective keeps the policy close to the SFT model. Without it, the policy drifts toward whatever the reward model mistakenly loves. Chapters SFT, reward models, RLHF, and DPO unpack each line.

Where evals sit

Evaluation is not a gate at the end. Every stage has its own checks, and the cheapest bug is the one caught at the stage that caused it.

Eval lens
When a metric moves, first ask which stage changed. A base-model benchmark that drops after RL is a forgetting problem, not a pretraining problem. A judge win rate that rises with response length is probably a reward or judge bias, not a capability gain (see LLM as judge).
