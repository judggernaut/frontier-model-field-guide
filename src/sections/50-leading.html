Connecting training and evaluation

Parts II and III describe two machines. One makes models. The other tells you whether they got better. This chapter is about running both as one system, and about the judgment calls that separate a senior reviewer from someone who reads results off a dashboard.

Plain wordsThe scale and the diet

Training is the diet. Evaluation is the bathroom scale. If the scale is wrong, every diet looks like it works or none does. If you only ever weigh yourself on one scale, you start eating whatever moves that particular scale, even if it does nothing for your health. Teams that train models face the same trap. Whatever you measure becomes what you optimise, so you need scales you trust, more than one of them, and some you never look at until the end.

For leadersEvals are the objective function of the organisation

In a training organisation, the eval suite is the de facto objective function. People will optimise whatever gets reported in the weekly review. That gives the eval owner unusual leverage and unusual responsibility.

  • Goodhart is the default. Every metric that drives decisions will be gamed, usually without bad intent. Training data drifts toward benchmark-like content, prompts drift toward judge preferences, and RL policies find verifier loopholes. Plan for it: rotate suites, keep private holdouts, and audit the largest gains first.
  • Separate the scales. Keep three tiers: development evals the training team can see every day, decision evals reviewed at milestones, and sealed holdouts that only an independent owner runs before launch. If the sealed set disagrees with the development set, believe the sealed set.
  • Pre-register the decision. Write down, before the run, which evals decide success and what delta counts. Post hoc selection of the metric that moved is the most common way a team fools itself.
  • Price the evals. Evaluation is a real fraction of compute and headcount. A suite that cannot detect the effect sizes your training changes produce (see power and sample size) is pure cost. Fund fewer, better-powered evals over many thin ones.
Under the hoodWhy gains shrink when they reach the holdout

Suppose you try \(m\) training variants and pick the one with the best development score. Even if every variant has the same true quality, the winner's observed score is biased upward by the expected maximum of \(m\) noise draws. With per-variant standard error \(\sigma\), that bias is roughly

\[ \mathbb{E}\big[\max_{i\le m} \varepsilon_i\big] \approx \sigma\sqrt{2\ln m} \]For \(m=20\) variants and \(\sigma = 1\) point, the "best" variant looks about 2.4 points better than it is. This is the winner's curse, and it is why a fresh holdout almost always shows a smaller gain than the development set.
import numpy as np

rng = np.random.default_rng(0)
m, sigma, trials = 20, 1.0, 20000
best_dev = rng.normal(0, sigma, size=(trials, m)).max(axis=1)   # all variants truly equal
print(round(best_dev.mean(), 2))                 # ~1.87 (the asymptotic formula overstates at small m)
print(round(sigma * np.sqrt(2 * np.log(m)), 2))  # 2.45

The practical fix is the same as in any selection problem: hold out a set you never select on, and report the selected model's score on it.

Is this result real? Seeds and run-to-run noise

Training runs are noisy. The same recipe with a different random seed lands at a different score. Before you believe a gain from a training change, you need to know how large that seed noise is compared with the gain.

Plain wordsOne run is one coin flip

If you train the same model twice, you get two slightly different results, like flipping a coin twice. A claimed one-point gain from comparing one run against one other run can easily be luck. The widget shows how many repeat runs you need before a gain of a given size is believable.

For leadersWhat to require before you believe an ablation
  • Ask for the seed variance first. No one can interpret a delta without it. If the team does not know it, the first deliverable is three seeds of the baseline.
  • Compute-matched comparisons. A new method that also used 30 percent more compute has not been compared with the baseline. Insist on equal FLOPs, equal tokens, or an explicit compute-versus-quality curve.
  • Tuned baselines. The new method usually gets more tuning effort than the baseline. Ask how many hyperparameter trials each arm got.
  • Small-scale results transfer imperfectly. Many gains at 1B parameters vanish at 70B, and some appear only at scale. Ask whether the trend holds across at least two or three model sizes before it drives a large run.
  • Kill criteria. Every research bet should state in advance what result would stop it. Without one, projects drift for quarters.
Under the hoodThe arithmetic behind the widget

With \(n\) seeds per arm, seed standard deviation \(s\), and true gain \(\delta\), the difference in arm means has standard error \(s\sqrt{2/n}\). A two-sided test at \(\alpha = 0.05\) has power approximately

\[ \text{power}(n) \approx \Phi\!\left(\frac{\delta}{s\sqrt{2/n}} - 1.96\right), \qquad n_{80} = \left\lceil 2\left(\frac{(1.96 + 0.84)\,s}{\delta}\right)^2 \right\rceil \]\(\Phi\) is the standard normal CDF. With very few seeds a t-distribution is more honest and needs slightly more seeds than this normal approximation.

The chance a single seed versus a single seed shows the wrong sign is \(\Phi\big(-\delta/(s\sqrt{2})\big)\). When the gain equals the seed standard deviation, that is about 24 percent: one comparison in four points the wrong way.

For leadersCommunicating uncertainty upward

Executives need a decision, not a lecture on intervals. A pattern that works:

  1. Lead with the decision and your confidence. "Recommend shipping. We are confident it is not worse and fairly confident it is better on coding."
  2. Give the number with its range in plain words. "Coding pass rate up about 3 points, plausibly between 1 and 5."
  3. Name the main risk and how you are watching it. "Long-context retrieval slipped slightly. We are monitoring it in the staged rollout with a rollback trigger."
  4. Say what would change your mind. This builds trust when something does change.
Principal asks
  • What would we have to believe for this result to be wrong?
  • If we had half the eval budget, which evals would we keep, and why?
  • Which of our evals has disagreed with production most often, and what did we change because of it?
  • What is the smallest effect our suite can reliably detect, and is that smaller than the effects we are trying to ship?
  • Who owns the sealed holdout, and when did someone last look at failures on it by hand?
