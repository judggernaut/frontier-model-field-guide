How to use this guide

This guide follows one idea from end to end: you cannot improve what you cannot measure, and you cannot trust a measurement you do not understand. Part I gives you the statistics. Part II turns that into model evaluation. Part III explains how models are trained, always connecting back to how you would measure whether the training worked. Part IV is about leading that work.

Three layers in every chapter

Plain words

An analogy and the core idea, with no jargon and no formulas. Read this first, even if you think you know the topic.

For leaders

What decision the idea informs, what it costs, how it fails, and the questions a senior reviewer asks. This is the layer for staff, principal, and partner conversations.

Under the hood

The math, the derivations, and short runnable code. Enough to check someone's work, not just follow it.

Use the All / Plain / Leader / Deep switch in the top bar to read a single layer across the whole guide. A good first pass is Plain for everything, then Leader, then Deep for the chapters that matter to your work.

Plain wordsWhat a layer looks like

Imagine tasting soup to decide if it needs salt. One spoonful tells you something, but not everything. Evaluation is tasting the model. Training is cooking. This guide teaches both, and how to tell when one spoonful is lying to you.

For leadersWhy both halves matter at senior levels

At senior levels in a frontier lab, decisions rarely turn on a single technique. They turn on whether a result is real, what it cost, and what it will break. Training choices (data, objective, RL method) determine what can go wrong. Evaluation choices determine whether you notice. Leaders who understand both can push back on a launch, scope a research bet, or kill a project with evidence instead of instinct.

Under the hoodHow the math and code are shown

Formulas are typeset, for example the cross-entropy loss that almost every chapter in Part III builds on:

\[ \mathcal{L}(\theta) = -\frac{1}{T}\sum_{t=1}^{T} \log p_\theta(x_t \mid x_{<t}) \]The average negative log probability the model assigns to each correct next token \(x_t\), given everything before it.
import math

def cross_entropy(probs_of_correct_token):
    # average surprise, in nats, at the token that actually came next
    return -sum(math.log(p) for p in probs_of_correct_token) / len(probs_of_correct_token)

print(round(cross_entropy([0.9, 0.5, 0.25]), 3))  # 0.732
print(round(math.exp(0.732), 2))                  # perplexity 2.08
