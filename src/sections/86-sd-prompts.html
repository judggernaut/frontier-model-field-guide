Reported design prompts, worked with the method

The previous four chapters teach one machine and one method. These are the prompts candidates actually reported from Anthropic and OpenAI system-design rounds in 2026, each worked in the same order: the questions that change the design, the lower bound or the key number, the design, and the follow-ups that were asked. The first three are the ones most often repeated.

Plain wordsStart from what physics allows

The candidate who wrote up the checkpoint-distribution round said the interviewer seemed to care less about the exact design and more about whether they could say "the fastest this could possibly go is about thirteen minutes, and here is why", then show how close their design gets. That is the habit to build: before drawing boxes, find the bottleneck that no design can avoid, compute it, and measure every idea against it.

For leadersWhat the reports have in common
  • They are infrastructure problems with one hard constraint: a shared 10 Gb/s link, eight GPUs that a large model needs all at once, a GPU fleet whose cache decides cost.
  • The interviewers push on failure and fairness: slow peers, corrupted chunks, starvation, rollback.
  • Candidates who failed name the same two causes: over-engineering ("Kafka, a hash ring and three cache layers for a problem that needed half that") and spending the clock on intermediate designs instead of stating the bound and going to the good answer.

1 · Distribute a 500 GB checkpoint to 1,000 GPU workers

Reported as an Anthropic senior SWE technical screen, a Staff onsite ("model rollout") and a Sr Staff "model downloader" round. A central repository with limited egress; 100–1,000 workers, later 10,000; each worker's uploads and downloads share one 10 Gb/s link (about 1.25 GB/s); every worker must receive and verify the whole model before the new version serves.

Worked problem

Lower bound. Every worker must download S = 500 GB. The source sends each byte at least once, so T ≥ S ÷ Bsrc. All other copies come from peers, so on average each worker also uploads about S. With a shared link, each worker moves about 2S through 1.25 GB/s:

\[ T \;\gtrsim\; \max\!\Big(\frac{S}{B_{\text{src}}},\ \frac{2S}{b}\Big) = \max\!\Big(\frac{500}{B_{\text{src}}},\ \frac{1000\ \text{GB}}{1.25\ \text{GB/s}}\Big) \approx 800\ \text{s} \approx 13\ \text{min} \]S model bytes, Bsrc source egress, b per-worker link shared by upload and download. The bound does not grow with the number of workers.

Candidates, measured against it. Everyone pulls from the source: N·S ÷ Bsrc, 400,000 s at 1.25 GB/s for 1,000 workers. A k-ary tree: each internal node uploads k·S, so time grows with k and the leaves' upload capacity is wasted. A chunked chain (pipeline): each node receives a chunk and forwards it, so each link carries 2S; time ≈ 2S ÷ b + N × chunk time, about 800 s + 1,000 × 0.1 s for 64 MB chunks, but one slow node stalls everyone behind it. Chunked peer-to-peer (BitTorrent-style, rarest-first) reaches the bound and routes around slow nodes.

Design. Split the checkpoint into 64–256 MB chunks with a manifest of SHA-256 hashes signed at the source. A tracker (or coordinator) keeps chunk ownership; each worker asks for the rarest chunks it lacks from a few peers with spare upload, verifies the hash before advertising the chunk, and caps concurrent uploads. Prefer peers in the same rack to keep traffic off the spine.

Follow-ups that were asked. Corrupted chunk: hash mismatch, discard, fetch from another peer, blacklist repeat offenders. Slow or failed worker: per-chunk timeouts, re-request elsewhere; a worker that dies just restarts from its verified chunks on disk. No central coordinator: gossip chunk bitmaps among a random peer set (each node keeps 20–50 neighbours). Activation: workers report "verified v42"; the controller flips serving to v42 in waves (canary 1%, then 10%, then all) and keeps v41 on disk for rollback. 10,000 workers: the bound is unchanged; the tracker becomes the bottleneck, so shard it or go to gossip.

The same arithmetic, inside one cluster with RDMA, is weight sync for RL; see weight sync and the 1T MoE problem in §13.

2 · Eight GPUs, a large model and a small model

Reported as an Anthropic system-design follow-up and a standalone "GPU inference scheduler" question. A pool of 8 GPUs; a small-model batch needs 1 GPU, a large-model batch needs all 8 at once; both batches take the same time t; requests for both arrive continuously.

Worked problem

Capacity. In GPU-time, a large batch costs 8t and a small batch t. With arrival rates λL and λS batches per second the pool is stable only if

\[ \frac{8\,\lambda_L t + \lambda_S t}{8} \;<\; 1 \]Utilisation of the 8-GPU pool. Above 1 no scheduler helps: shed load, batch harder, or buy GPUs.

The real problem is the drain. To start a large batch you must wait until all 8 GPUs are free. If small jobs keep arriving and you keep admitting them, the large request starves. If you stop admitting the moment a large request arrives, GPUs go idle one by one while the last small jobs finish: up to 7 × t of wasted GPU-time per large batch.

Design. Two queues. When a large request is waiting, compute a reservation time: when the current small jobs will have drained. Keep admitting small jobs only if they will finish by then (backfilling, as in EASY backfill on HPC schedulers); since all batches take t, that means "stop admitting once the reservation is set" unless a GPU is idle and t fits. To bound wasted drain, batch large requests: let them accumulate up to a size or a maximum wait derived from their SLA, then drain once for several large batches back to back. Under heavy mixed load, a fixed time-division schedule (for example, every period P the pool runs large for a slot, small otherwise) gives predictable latency for both. Priority by age prevents starvation either way.

Follow-ups that were asked. How to represent "all eight atomically" (a gang reservation, never partial allocation, which deadlocks two large jobs holding four GPUs each). When to stop admitting small jobs (when the predicted drain plus the large SLA budget says so). What signals to use (queue depths, request age, SLAs, predicted remaining time). At larger scale this is gang scheduling with preemption in Kueue or Slurm; see control plane.

3 · An inference system for document review

Reported as an Anthropic system-design round. The interviewer drilled into how to measure cache hit rate, which batching strategy to use, and how to load-balance before the GPUs, then moved to the eight-GPU question above.

Worked problem

Ask first. Document length and count; how many questions per document; latency target (interactive or batch overnight); one model or several; is the instruction or rubric shared across requests.

Why caching dominates. Each review sends a long shared prefix (system prompt, rubric, then the document) and a short question. Prefill is compute-bound and grows with the prefix; if the prefix's KV blocks are already cached, prefill costs only the new tokens. Hit rate is cached prompt tokens ÷ total prompt tokens, read from the engine's prefix-cache counters, measured per replica and overall, and broken down by prefix level (rubric only, rubric plus document).

Routing decides the hit rate. Round-robin spreads one document's questions across replicas and each one prefills it again. Route by a hash of the document (consistent hashing so losing a replica moves only its keys), with a load guard: if the target's queue is too long, spill to the next replica on the ring. This is the sticky routing of the rollout service.

Batching. Continuous batching with chunked prefill inside each engine (decode tokens first, prompt chunks fill the remaining token budget). Separate interactive and bulk traffic: bulk can wait for larger batches and run on the same fleet at low priority, or on dedicated replicas at higher memory utilisation. Cost scales with uncached prefill tokens plus generated tokens, so the metric to report upward is cost per reviewed document at the latency SLO.

Shorter reports

Collaborative prompt playground (Anthropic, several reports)

Users edit prompts together, run them against models, compare outputs, and keep versions. Key decisions: the document model for concurrent edits (operational transform or a CRDT, or simpler: per-prompt locks with last-writer-wins on fields); version history stored as immutable revisions with deltas for large prompts; runs as asynchronous jobs with streamed output, results keyed by (prompt revision, model, parameters) so they can be cached and compared; rate limits and quotas per workspace. One onsite follow-up asked how to store large prompt revisions efficiently.

1:1 messaging at 100M DAU (Anthropic Staff backend screen)

Web-only, direct messages, offline delivery required. Gateway and app servers holding WebSockets; a presence service mapping user to server; persist then acknowledge then route; per-conversation sequence numbers so clients fetch what they missed; idempotency keys for deduplication; partition the message store by conversation ID. Follow-ups: ordering, routing between app servers (pub/sub per server or per user channel), replication lag, traffic spikes. A Sr Staff candidate who started with a single queue felt the interviewer wanted pub/sub channels.

Secure sandboxed cloud IDE (OpenAI)

Long-lived workspaces on short-lived sandboxes; arbitrary, possibly hostile code. Containers sharing a kernel are not enough: microVMs (Firecracker) with cgroup limits and default-deny egress. Workspace files on fast local disk, snapshotted to object storage at least every minute; idle sandboxes suspended. Terminal output ephemeral; Run and Build are tracked jobs with logs streamed live and persisted for reconnects. The same isolation ladder (runc, gVisor, microVM) is in sandboxes and rewards.

ChatGPT, simplified (OpenAI)

The interviewer said to skip large-scale architecture and persistence. Follow-ups: why Server-Sent Events rather than WebSockets for streaming (one-way, plain HTTP, works through proxies, automatic reconnect), and how the client keeps rendering responsive for very long conversations (virtualised list, render only visible messages, incremental markdown).

Model rollout and LLM-inference phone screen (Anthropic Safeguards, Staff)

An inference question that had appeared in earlier reports, followed by an onsite where the candidate built an agent loop by hand. Prepare: the generate loop with KV cache, batching, sampling parameters, then a tool-calling loop (call the model, parse a tool call, run it, append the observation, stop on a final answer or a turn cap) with validation and timeouts.

Matmul sharding with a roofline model (Anthropic infra onsite)

Given a spreadsheet tool, analyse a feed-forward matmul under different shardings. Arithmetic intensity is FLOPs ÷ bytes moved; a kernel is compute-bound when intensity exceeds peak FLOP/s ÷ memory bandwidth (about 989 ÷ 3.35 ≈ 295 FLOP/byte on an H100 in BF16). For the FFN [B, d] × [d, 4d] then [B, 4d] × [4d, d]: splitting the first weight by columns (the 4d dimension) needs no communication if the input is replicated, and splitting the second by rows then needs one all-reduce of the [B, d] partial sums (the Megatron pattern); splitting the contracting d dimension of the first weight instead needs an all-reduce of a [B, 4d] tensor, four times the bytes. Compare each communication time (bytes ÷ link bandwidth) with the compute time; the constants are in numbers.

Watch out
Two candidates wrote at length about failing by over-engineering after preparing with chatbots: the models proposed hyperscale designs that were hard to explain in 45 minutes and agreed too readily with whatever the candidate suggested. One fixed it by building both designs in code and keeping the simpler one. State the bound, pick the simplest design that reaches it, and add complexity only when the interviewer's numbers force it.
