Contamination and benchmark integrity: is the model reasoning or remembering?

A benchmark score means something only if the model has not seen the answers. Frontier models are trained on a large fraction of the public internet, and public benchmarks live on the public internet. This chapter covers how test items leak into training data, how to detect it, how to design benchmarks that resist it, and how to report results so a reader can judge for themselves.

Two students and a leaked answer key

How changing the numbers on an exam separates memorising from understanding.

Last year's final exam was posted online with its answers. Student A memorises the answer key. Student B learns how to solve the problems. A model trained on internet text can end up like Student A for popular benchmarks, usually by accident.

On the leaked exam, both score in the nineties. From the score alone you cannot tell who understands the material. A benchmark number on a public test set has exactly this problem.

Now change the surface of every question: new names, new numbers, options shuffled. The skill needed is identical, so an honest score should barely move. This is a perturbation test.

Student A falls from 95 to 55. Student B goes from 93 to 91. The size of the drop is the fingerprint: a big gap between original and perturbed versions says the original score was partly memory.

Small drops happen by chance, because the perturbed questions are a fresh sample. On 200 questions the noise on a gap is roughly ±5 points, so B's 2-point drop means nothing and A's 40 is overwhelming. Always put an interval on the gap before calling something contaminated.

Perturbation is one detective. If you own the training data, search it for the test items. Benchmark authors can plant a unique canary string so leaks are findable. And questions written after the model's data cutoff cannot have been memorised.

Plain wordsThe exam that was posted online

A teacher gives the same final exam every year. One year a student posts the questions and answers online. Next year's class scores much higher. Did they get smarter, or did they read the answers?

The test for this is simple: change the exam a little. Rename the people in the word problems, swap the numbers, reorder the options. A student who understands the material does about as well as before. A student who memorised the answer key falls apart.

Language models are trained on huge piles of internet text, so some of them have "read the answer key" for popular benchmarks, often by accident. This is called contamination.

For leadersWhy contamination is an organisational risk, not a footnote

Contamination inflates exactly the numbers that go into launch announcements, investor decks, and model comparison tables. It also corrupts internal decisions: a training change that "improves" a contaminated benchmark may just be memorising better. The damage is reputational when outsiders find it, and strategic when it misdirects research.

What to fund. At minimum, a decontamination pass in the data pipeline and a private held-out set for every capability you gate launches on. For high-stakes capabilities, a continuously refreshed benchmark built from material created after the training cutoff.

Principal asks
  • Was this benchmark decontaminated against the training corpus, at what n-gram length, and how many items were removed?
  • What does the model score on a perturbed or post-cutoff version of the same task?
  • Is the headline number on the public set or on a private held-out set? Where do they disagree?
  • How long until this benchmark is saturated, and what replaces it?

How items leak

Direct inclusion

The benchmark file, or a web page reproducing it, is scraped into pretraining data.

Paraphrase and discussion

Blog posts, forum threads, and solution write-ups restate the items in different words. Exact-match filters miss these.

Fine-tuning data

Synthetic or human-written SFT data generated by people or models that had seen the benchmark.

Selection pressure

Even with no leaked text, choosing checkpoints, prompts, or hyperparameters by benchmark score fits the model to the test set. This is overfitting through decisions, not data.

Detection

Plain wordsSearch the library, then test the student

There are two ways to check. If you own the training data, search it for the exam questions. If you do not, test the student: reword the questions and see whether the score holds up.

Under the hoodOverlap, canaries, membership signals, perturbation

N-gram overlap

The standard data-side check. Tokenise each benchmark item and each training document, and flag items that share long n-grams with the corpus. Thirteen-gram overlap was used for decontamination in the GPT-3 paper (Brown et al., 2020); other labs use 8 to 50 tokens or a fraction-of-item threshold. Short n-grams flag common phrases; long ones miss paraphrases.

import hashlib, re

def ngrams(text: str, n: int = 13) -> set[str]:
    toks = re.findall(r"\w+", text.lower())
    return {" ".join(toks[i:i + n]) for i in range(len(toks) - n + 1)}


def contamination_overlap(task_text: str, model_output: str, n: int = 13) -> float:
    """Fraction of the task's n-grams reproduced verbatim in the output.
    High overlap on a task the model should have had to reason about is a
    memorization signal, not proof, but it tells you where to look."""
    t, o = ngrams(task_text, n), ngrams(model_output, n)
    return len(t & o) / len(t) if t else 0.0

At corpus scale the same idea runs over hashed n-grams in a Bloom filter or suffix array, so trillions of training tokens can be checked against every benchmark in one pass.

Embedding similarity

To catch paraphrases, embed benchmark items and training chunks and flag nearest neighbours above a similarity threshold, then have a person or a strong model confirm. This finds rewritten solutions and translated copies that n-grams miss, at the cost of false positives on genuinely similar but independent text.

Canary strings

Embed a unique random token (a GUID) in every file of a private or public eval set, with a request that it never be trained on. Two uses: filter it out of your own corpora, and probe released models. If a model completes the canary when given its prefix, the set is in that model's training data. BIG-bench popularised this practice.

Membership-inference signals (outsider tests)

Without training data access, you can still look for signs a specific text was trained on:

  • Verbatim completion. Give the first half of an item; check whether the model reproduces the second half, including its exact numbers or answer options.
  • Likelihood gap. Seen text tends to get unusually low perplexity compared with paraphrases of the same content. The Min-K% Prob method (Shi et al., 2023) averages the lowest-probability tokens, which separates seen from unseen text better than average perplexity.
  • Ordering artefacts. If a model assigns higher likelihood to a benchmark's examples in their published order than in shuffled orders, it has probably seen the file. Since the examples are exchangeable, a clean model has no reason to prefer one order (Oren et al., 2023).

These are evidence, not proof. Famous text is low-perplexity whether or not it was in a benchmark.

Perturbation tests

The strongest practical test available to an outsider. Rename variables, change constants, reorder independent clauses, shuffle options, translate and back-translate. A model that reasons keeps roughly the same score. A model that memorised falls off a cliff. GSM-Symbolic (Mirzadeh et al., 2024) applied this to grade-school maths and found measurable drops for many models when only names and numbers changed.

def perturbation_check(run_fn, task, perturb_fn, trials: int = 5) -> dict:
    """Rename variables, change constants, reorder independent clauses.
    A model that reasons stays at roughly the same score.
    A model that memorized falls off a cliff."""
    base = run_fn(task)
    perturbed = [run_fn(perturb_fn(task, seed=i)) for i in range(trials)]
    mean_p = sum(perturbed) / trials
    return {"base": base, "perturbed_mean": mean_p, "drop": base - mean_p}

The drop alone is not the contamination signal, because perturbations can also make items genuinely harder. The clean comparison is a difference in differences: the suspect model's drop minus the drop of a control model that could not have seen the items (or a model trained before the benchmark existed).

\[ \widehat{\Delta}_{\text{contam}} = \big(\bar a^{\,S}_{\text{orig}} - \bar a^{\,S}_{\text{pert}}\big) - \big(\bar a^{\,C}_{\text{orig}} - \bar a^{\,C}_{\text{pert}}\big), \qquad \operatorname{SE} = \frac{s_d}{\sqrt{n}} \]\(S\) is the suspect model, \(C\) the clean control, and \(\bar a\) is accuracy on original or perturbed items. Compute the per-item difference \(d_i\) on the same \(n\) items so the comparison is paired; \(s_d\) is its standard deviation. This is the same pairing logic as in eval statistics.
Contamination shows up as a gap
A suspect model and a clean control of equal ability answer the same items, once in original wording and once perturbed. Raise the share of items the suspect saw in training and watch its original score climb while the perturbed score stays put. Then shrink the item count and see when the signal disappears into noise.
Key idea
Set seen share to 0 and extra difficulty to 8 points. Both models drop by about the same amount, so the difference in differences is near zero. A raw drop on perturbed items is not evidence of contamination by itself. The control model is what turns a drop into a measurement.

Designing benchmarks that resist contamination

Plain wordsWrite new exams faster than they can leak

The surest fix is an exam made after the student finished studying. If the questions did not exist when the model was trained, it cannot have memorised them. The other fix is to keep some exams locked in a drawer and never post them.

Saturation

Every public benchmark degrades as a signal over time, through contamination, through selection pressure, and through simple saturation as top models approach the ceiling. Near the ceiling, remaining errors are often label noise, so further gains mean nothing. Plan the replacement before the current benchmark hits about 90% for frontier models. Assume a public benchmark's useful life is measured in model generations, not years.

Under the hoodWhy saturation destroys resolving power

Near the ceiling, two things happen at once. The headroom to show improvement shrinks, and a growing share of the remaining "errors" are items with wrong or ambiguous gold labels. If a fraction \(e\) of items have bad labels, the maximum measurable accuracy is about \(1-e\), and differences between strong models are compressed into the last few points where label noise dominates. This is why audits of popular benchmarks that find a few percent of mislabelled items matter so much for frontier comparisons.

The number of tasks needed to separate two models also grows as they converge, because fewer tasks are discordant (see sample size). A saturated benchmark is both noisier and less informative per task.

Reporting norms

For leadersWhat a credible benchmark claim includes
  1. Decontamination method: overlap threshold, n-gram length, how many items were flagged and removed, and the score on the clean subset next to the full set.
  2. A contamination-resistant companion number: private, perturbed, or post-cutoff results alongside the public one.
  3. Interval and n on every score, as in every other chapter of Part II.
  4. Evaluation settings: prompt, few-shot examples, sampling parameters, number of attempts. Small prompt changes move benchmark scores by several points.
  5. Selection disclosure: whether the checkpoint or prompt was chosen using this benchmark.
Eval lens
When a public benchmark and a private or post-cutoff equivalent disagree, believe the one the model could not have seen. A consistent gap between the two, tracked across releases, is itself a useful metric of how much of a model's public performance is memory.
