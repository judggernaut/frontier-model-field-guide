Fast and slow: reasoning models and thinking modes

Some questions you answer on the spot. Others you only get right by working through them. Since late 2024, frontier models have been built and sold with the same split: an instant mode that answers straight away, and a thinking mode that writes out a long chain of intermediate steps first. This chapter explains why writing steps helps a next-token predictor at all, how reasoning models are trained to do it, what thinking budgets and routers control, what the extra tokens cost, when thinking does not help, and why "think harder" and "turn up the temperature" are two completely separate knobs.

17 × 24: in your head, then on paper

Fast and slow thinking, and what it means for a model that writes one token at a time.

Kahneman opens Thinking, Fast and Slow with this sum. A rough size pops into your head instantly, but the exact answer does not. The quick guess feels fine and is wrong.

Writing the working breaks one hard step into three easy ones, and each line can be checked before the next. It costs time and attention. That trade is the whole story of reasoning models.

A model does a fixed amount of work for each token it produces. Answering at once gives the problem one token's worth of work. Writing the working first gives it many, and every new token can read the lines already written, like a scratchpad.

Every thinking token is generated one at a time and billed like any other output token. A hundredfold increase in tokens is a hundredfold increase in latency and cost for that answer.

The takeaway: reasoning is a way of buying computation at answer time. It pays off when the problem decomposes into steps and the answer can be checked. It is wasted, and sometimes harmful, on everything else.

Two speeds of thinking

Plain wordsFast intuition, slow work

Psychologist Daniel Kahneman popularised a simple picture of the mind with two characters. System 1 is fast and automatic: you read a face, finish a familiar phrase, or know that 2 + 2 is 4 without trying. System 2 is slow and deliberate: you park in a tight space, fill in a tax form, or work out 17 × 24 on paper.

Language models now come in both flavours. An instant answer is like System 1: one quick pass, no visible working. A thinking mode is like System 2: the model writes out steps, checks them, and sometimes backtracks before it commits to an answer. The analogy is useful for intuition. It is only an analogy.

For leadersA product split, and a vocabulary to use with care

"System 1 and System 2" has become shorthand across the industry. Vendors use it for thinking modes, and at least one company has named a whole product class "System One models" (see System 1 decision models). The shorthand helps when you are deciding which requests deserve expensive deliberation. It misleads when it suggests the model has two different minds inside.

  • Same weights, different spend. In most products the instant and thinking modes are the same network, or two networks from one family, used with very different token budgets. The quality gap comes from extra computation and from training that teaches the model how to use it.
  • The decision you own is allocation: which traffic gets thinking, how much, and who decides (the user, your code, or a router).
  • The risk is anthropomorphism in reviews. "The model reflected and realised its mistake" describes text the model wrote. Whether that text reflects how the answer was computed is a separate and open question.
Under the hoodWhere the analogy holds and where it breaks

Kahneman himself presented the two systems as a shorthand, characters in a story about the mind, not two separate brain modules. Dual-process theory remains debated in psychology, and several studies cited in the book (notably on priming) later failed to replicate. Use the analogy for the trade-off between speed and deliberation, and nothing more.

For a transformer the mapping is concrete. Every output token costs one forward pass: a fixed stack of \(L\) layers, roughly \(2N\) floating-point operations for a model with \(N\) parameters. The amount of serial computation behind an answer therefore scales with the number of tokens generated before it:

\[ C_{\text{answer}} \approx 2N\,\big(T_{\text{think}} + T_{\text{ans}}\big), \qquad \text{serial depth} \approx L\,\big(T_{\text{think}} + T_{\text{ans}}\big) \]\(N\) is parameter count, \(L\) the number of layers, \(T_{\text{think}}\) the tokens written before the answer and \(T_{\text{ans}}\) the answer tokens. An instant answer has \(T_{\text{think}} = 0\).

So "System 2" in a language model is many System 1 steps chained through a scratchpad. Nothing in the architecture changes between modes; the model only runs longer, and training decides what it writes while it runs.

Chain of thought: showing the work

Plain wordsAsking for the steps

In 2022 researchers found that if you show a model a few worked examples with the steps written out, or simply add "Let's think step by step" to the question, it gets far more maths word problems right. The model writes its working first, then the answer. This is called chain of thought.

Why should writing help a machine that only predicts the next word? Two reasons. Each word it writes is another round of computation. And everything it writes stays on the page, so later words can build on earlier ones, the way you build on the lines of a long division.

For leadersWhat the evidence says
  • Few-shot chain of thought. Wei et al. (2022) prompted a 540-billion-parameter model with eight worked examples and reached state-of-the-art accuracy on GSM8K, a set of grade-school maths word problems. The effect appeared only in sufficiently large models.
  • Zero-shot chain of thought. Kojima et al. (2022) added "Let's think step by step" with no examples. On text-davinci-002, MultiArith accuracy went from 17.7% to 78.7% and GSM8K from 10.4% to 40.7%.
  • Where it helps. Sprague et al. (2024), a meta-analysis of over 100 papers plus experiments on 20 datasets and 14 models, found the gains concentrated on maths and symbolic logic. On MMLU, answering directly matched chain of thought except on questions whose text or answer contained an equals sign.
Principal asks
When someone reports a gain from "adding reasoning", ask: on which task types, measured how, at what token cost? Does the gain survive on the slices without symbolic computation? Was the comparison at matched decoding settings, or did the reasoning arm also get more samples?
Under the hoodWhy intermediate tokens add power

A single forward pass is a fixed-depth circuit. Problems that need many dependent steps (carry the digit, then add the next column) can exceed what a fixed depth computes reliably. Writing intermediate tokens turns the model into a loop: the output of pass \(t\) becomes input to pass \(t+1\), and the context window acts as working memory.

Theory backs this up. Merrill and Sabharwal (2024, ICLR) showed that a decoder-only transformer allowed a linear number of intermediate steps can recognise all regular languages, which is believed impossible without them, and that with polynomially many steps it recognises exactly the problems solvable in polynomial time. The number of thinking tokens is a computational budget in a precise sense.

Two caveats matter for evaluation. The written chain is a generated text, so it can be unfaithful: the stated reasons need not be the computation that produced the answer. And chain of thought improves accuracy on average while adding a new failure surface, since an early wrong step propagates.

Reasoning models: trained to think before answering

Plain wordsFrom a prompt trick to a trained habit

Chain-of-thought prompting asks a model to show its work. A reasoning model is trained to do it on its own, at length, and well. During training it attempts many problems with checkable answers (maths with a known result, code with tests), and it is rewarded when the final answer is right. Over time it learns to write long, useful working: trying an approach, checking it, noticing a mistake, and trying again.

For leadersTwo launches that changed the cost structure
  • OpenAI o1 (12 September 2024). OpenAI reported that o1's performance kept improving with more reinforcement learning and with more time spent thinking at test time. On AIME 2024 maths problems, GPT-4o solved 12% on average; o1 solved 74% with one sample and 83% with a consensus of 64 samples. OpenAI chose not to show the raw chain of thought, showing a model-written summary instead.
  • DeepSeek-R1 (January 2025). DeepSeek published how they did it: reinforcement learning against verifiable rewards, with reasoning behaviours such as self-reflection and verification emerging from training rather than from labelled reasoning traces. They released the weights. The method is covered in GRPO and RL with verifiable rewards.

The business consequence: quality became something you can buy per request with tokens, instead of only with a bigger model. Your cost model now has a variable term that depends on how hard each request is.

Under the hoodWhat RL changes

The policy samples a reasoning trace \(z\) and answer \(y\) for prompt \(x\). A verifier gives reward \(r(x, y) \in \{0, 1\}\). Training maximises expected reward, typically with a group baseline (GRPO) and a KL penalty to a reference model:

\[ \max_\theta\; \mathbb{E}_{x}\,\mathbb{E}_{(z, y) \sim \pi_\theta(\cdot \mid x)}\big[r(x, y)\big] - \beta\,\mathrm{KL}\big(\pi_\theta \,\|\, \pi_{\text{ref}}\big) \]Only the final answer is graded. The trace \(z\) is never supervised directly, so the model is free to discover whatever writing habits raise the chance of a correct \(y\), including longer traces.

Because longer traces often raise reward, average response length tends to grow during this training. That is the mechanism behind "thinking longer", and it is why labs add explicit length controls and budget training afterwards. Whether RL teaches new capabilities or mostly sharpens ones the base model already had is discussed in the GRPO chapter.

Thinking budgets, modes and routers

Plain wordsWho decides how hard to think

A thinking model needs to know how much effort to spend. There are three ways to decide. You pick (an "instant" or "thinking" button). You set a budget (think for up to this many tokens, or at this effort level). Or the system picks for you: a small, fast router reads the request and sends easy questions to the quick model and hard ones to the thinker.

Eval lens
A thinking setting is part of the system under test. Record the model, effort level, and routing mode with every result, and report accuracy alongside thinking tokens per item. A router is itself a classifier: when it sends a hard question to the fast path, that is a false negative with a measurable rate. Evaluate the router on labelled easy and hard requests, as in classification metrics.
Under the hoodRouting as expected-cost minimisation

A router predicts, for request \(x\), the chance \(q(x)\) that the fast path gets it wrong when the slow path would get it right. With cost \(c_{\text{err}}\) for a wrong answer and extra cost \(c_{\text{think}}\) for the slow path, routing to the slow path is worth it when

\[ q(x)\, c_{\text{err}} > c_{\text{think}} \quad\Longleftrightarrow\quad q(x) > \frac{c_{\text{think}}}{c_{\text{err}}} \]The threshold depends on what errors cost you, not on the router. A calibrated \(q(x)\) lets you move the threshold when prices change, without retraining.

This is the same threshold logic as any cost-sensitive classifier, and a router is often exactly the kind of fast, typed, probabilistic decision the next chapter is about. Budgets and effort levels apply the same idea within one model: training teaches the model to stop early on easy inputs and continue on hard ones.

Accuracy against thinking tokens

Plain wordsEach extra minute helps a little less

Give a student one minute on a hard problem and they might get 40% right. Five minutes, maybe 70%. An hour, maybe 82%. The first few minutes help most. Reasoning models behave the same way: accuracy climbs quickly with the first thousand thinking tokens, then flattens, while the cost and the wait keep growing in a straight line.

For leadersBuy the knee of the curve

Published charts, starting with OpenAI's o1 announcement, plot accuracy against thinking compute on a log axis and show a roughly straight line over a range. A straight line on a log axis means each doubling of tokens buys about the same gain, so each additional token buys less. Meanwhile latency and price grow linearly.

  • Measure your own curve. Run your eval at three to five effort levels and plot accuracy, p50 and p95 latency, and cost per 1,000 requests. Public curves come from maths contests, not your traffic.
  • Pick by target. Choose the cheapest setting that meets the accuracy target, and route only the requests that need more.
  • Watch the tail. Thinking length varies a lot per request. A p95 latency several times the median is normal, and it is what users feel.
Under the hoodA simple cost model
\[ \text{acc}(T) \approx a + b\log_2 T \;\;(\text{until it saturates}), \qquad \text{latency} \approx t_{\text{prefill}} + \frac{T + T_{\text{ans}}}{r}, \qquad \text{cost} \approx p_{\text{out}}\,(T + T_{\text{ans}}) \]\(T\) is thinking tokens, \(r\) the decode speed in tokens per second, \(p_{\text{out}}\) the output price per token. The log-linear form is an empirical fit over a range, not a law.

budgets  = [0, 1000, 2000, 4000, 8000, 16000]
accuracy = [0.42, 0.61, 0.70, 0.77, 0.81, 0.82]

PRICE_PER_M   = 10.0   # $ per 1M output tokens (illustrative); thinking tokens bill as output
TOKENS_PER_S  = 80.0   # decode speed: thinking is generated one token at a time
ANSWER_TOKENS = 200

def cost_and_latency(budget):
    toks = budget + ANSWER_TOKENS
    return toks * PRICE_PER_M / 1e6, toks / TOKENS_PER_S

def cheapest_budget(target):
    """Smallest budget that reaches the target accuracy, or None."""
    for b, a in zip(budgets, accuracy):
        if a >= target:
            return b
    return None

for b, a in zip(budgets, accuracy):
    c, t = cost_and_latency(b)
    print(f"{b:>6} tokens  acc {a:.2f}  ${c * 1000:6.2f} per 1k  {t:6.1f} s")
for i in range(1, len(budgets)):
    # accuracy bought per extra 1,000 thinking tokens: the curve flattens
    gain = (accuracy[i] - accuracy[i - 1]) / ((budgets[i] - budgets[i - 1]) / 1000)
    print(f"{budgets[i - 1]}->{budgets[i]}: +{gain:.3f} per 1k tokens")

const ACCURACY: [f64; 6] = [0.42, 0.61, 0.70, 0.77, 0.81, 0.82];

const PRICE_PER_M: f64 = 10.0;   // $ per 1M output tokens (illustrative); thinking tokens bill as output
const TOKENS_PER_S: f64 = 80.0;  // decode speed: thinking is generated one token at a time
const ANSWER_TOKENS: u32 = 200;

fn cost_and_latency(budget: u32) -> (f64, f64) {
    let toks = (budget + ANSWER_TOKENS) as f64;
    (toks * PRICE_PER_M / 1e6, toks / TOKENS_PER_S)
}

/// Smallest budget that reaches the target accuracy, or None.
fn cheapest_budget(target: f64) -> Option<u32> {
    BUDGETS.iter().zip(ACCURACY.iter()).find(|(_, &a)| a >= target).map(|(&b, _)| b)
}

fn main() {
    for (&b, &a) in BUDGETS.iter().zip(ACCURACY.iter()) {
        let (c, t) = cost_and_latency(b);
        println!("{:>6} tokens  acc {:.2}  ${:6.2} per 1k  {:6.1} s", b, a, c * 1000.0, t);

When reasoning does not help

Plain wordsOverthinking is real

Some tasks get worse when you think about them too hard. In a classic study (Wilson and Schooler, 1991), people asked to list their reasons for liking different jams ended up agreeing less with expert tasters than people who simply tasted them. Models show similar effects. Long reasoning can talk a model out of a good first instinct, get it lost in irrelevant details, or make it latch onto a pattern that is not really there.

For leadersThree published warnings
  • Most gains are symbolic. Sprague et al. (2024) found chain of thought helped mainly on maths and logic. For knowledge recall and many classification tasks, direct answers did about as well at a fraction of the cost.
  • Tasks where thinking hurts people. Liu et al. (2024) took six tasks from cognitive psychology where deliberation makes humans worse. On three, chain of thought caused large drops, up to 36.3 points of absolute accuracy for o1-preview compared with GPT-4o.
  • Inverse scaling with thinking length. Gema et al. (2025) built tasks where longer reasoning lowered accuracy: counting with distractors, regression with spurious features, and deduction with many constraints. Different model families failed differently, some getting distracted by irrelevant information, others overfitting to how the problem was framed.
Watch out
Defaulting every request to maximum thinking is a common and expensive mistake. It raises latency and cost on all traffic, and on some slices it lowers accuracy. Turn thinking up where your own eval shows a gain, and treat "more thinking is always safer" as a hypothesis to test.
Under the hoodWhy more tokens can hurt

Each step in a chain has some chance of introducing an error and some chance of fixing one. If the fix rate is low or the problem has no checkable structure, extra steps mostly add chances to go wrong. Long traces also dilute attention: by token 10,000 the original question is a small share of the context, and irrelevant details written along the way compete with it. Finally, RL rewards only the final answer on its training distribution. Habits that pay off on contest maths (always try another approach, always reconsider) can misfire on tasks where the first answer was best.

Reasoning is not temperature

Two knobs: the dice and the scratchpad

Temperature reshapes the odds of the next token. Reasoning changes the odds by doing more work first.

Asked for 17 × 24 with no working, one forward pass produces odds over candidate answers (illustrative). The wrong answer 398 is the favourite; the right one, 408, is second. Sampling picks one according to these odds.

Turn temperature down and the favourite takes almost everything. The ranking does not change, so here the model becomes reliably wrong. Low temperature buys consistency, never correctness.

Turn temperature up and the odds flatten. Answers vary more from run to run. The model did exactly the same amount of computation in all three pictures: one forward pass, then a reshaped draw.

Reasoning works on a different lever. The model writes its working first, so by the time it reaches the answer token it has done dozens of extra passes and can read "340 + 68" on the page. The odds themselves change: the right answer is now the clear favourite.

The two knobs combine. Self-consistency samples several reasoning chains at a temperature above zero, so they take different routes, and keeps the most common final answer. Temperature supplies variety; reasoning supplies quality per chain. See inference and test-time compute.

Plain wordsLoaded dice versus doing the sum

Temperature controls how adventurous the model is when it picks each next word. Low temperature: always take the favourite. High temperature: sometimes take an outsider. It changes how the odds are spread. It does not make the model think any harder.

Reasoning means the model writes working before the answer. That is more computation, so it can change which answer is the favourite in the first place.

They are separate dials. A model can reason at low or high temperature, and it can answer instantly at either.

Principal asks
If a team says "we lowered temperature and accuracy improved", ask whether they measured one sample per item at both settings. Lower temperature mostly reduces variance; a gain usually means the favourite was already right and sampling was throwing it away. If they say "we raised the thinking budget", ask for the accuracy and cost at each budget, and whether the comparison held temperature fixed.
Under the hoodThe two operations, side by side

Temperature acts after the forward pass, on the logits \(z\):

\[ p_i(\tau) = \frac{e^{z_i/\tau}}{\sum_j e^{z_j/\tau}} \]\(\tau \to 0\) approaches greedy argmax; \(\tau = 1\) is the model's own distribution; \(\tau > 1\) flattens it. The ordering of tokens never changes, and the logits \(z\) are computed once either way.

Reasoning changes the conditioning. The answer distribution becomes \(p(y \mid x, z_{1:T})\), where \(z_{1:T}\) are the thinking tokens. The logits at the answer position are different numbers, produced after \(T\) additional forward passes. Self-consistency then marginalises over sampled chains, \(\hat y = \arg\max_y \sum_{k=1}^{K} \mathbb{1}[y_k = y]\); Wang et al. (2022) reported gains over single-chain prompting of 17.9 points on GSM8K and 11.0 on SVAMP.

import math

def softmax_t(logits, T):
    # temperature divides the logits before softmax: it reshapes, it never adds computation
    z = [l / T for l in logits]
    m = max(z)
    e = [math.exp(v - m) for v in z]
    s = sum(e)
    return [v / s for v in e]

def entropy_bits(p):
    return -sum(q * math.log2(q) for q in p if q > 0)

answers = ["398", "408", "418", "388"]   # "408" is correct
logits  = [2.0, 1.7, 1.0, 0.8]           # one forward pass, answering 17 x 24 with no working
for T in (0.2, 1.0, 2.0):
    p = softmax_t(logits, T)
    top = answers[p.index(max(p))]
    print(f"T={T:.1f}", [round(q, 3) for q in p], f"H={entropy_bits(p):.2f} bits", "top:", top)
# the top answer is 398 (wrong) at every temperature; only the spread changes
fn softmax_t(logits: &[f64], t: f64) -> Vec<f64> {
    // temperature divides the logits before softmax: it reshapes, it never adds computation
    let z: Vec<f64> = logits.iter().map(|l| l / t).collect();
    let m = z.iter().cloned().fold(f64::NEG_INFINITY, f64::max);
    let e: Vec<f64> = z.iter().map(|v| (v - m).exp()).collect();
    let s: f64 = e.iter().sum();
    e.iter().map(|v| v / s).collect()
}

fn entropy_bits(p: &[f64]) -> f64 {
    -p.iter().filter(|&&q| q > 0.0).map(|q| q * q.log2()).sum::<f64>()
}

fn main() {
    let answers = ["398", "408", "418", "388"]; // "408" is correct
    let logits = [2.0, 1.7, 1.0, 0.8];          // one forward pass, answering 17 x 24 with no working
    for t in [0.2, 1.0, 2.0] {
        let p = softmax_t(&logits, t);
        let (mut best, mut top) = (0.0, 0);
        for (i, &q) in p.iter().enumerate() { if q > best { best = q; top = i; } }
        let r: Vec<f64> = p.iter().map(|q| (q * 1000.0).round() / 1000.0).collect();
        println!("T={:.1} {:?} H={:.2} bits top: {}", t, r, entropy_bits(&p), answers[top]);
    }
    // the top answer is 398 (wrong) at every temperature; only the spread changes
}

Eval lens
Reasoning changes what an eval measures. Report the thinking setting and mean thinking tokens per item next to every accuracy number, and compare models at matched cost, not only by name. Keep temperature and sample count fixed when comparing thinking budgets, or you are changing two knobs at once. And when a reasoning model's visible summary is used as evidence of how it solved a problem, remember the summary is generated text about hidden tokens.
