RLHF and PPO: optimizing against a learned reward

Reinforcement learning from human feedback takes the reward model from the previous chapter and trains the language model to produce responses that score well, while a leash keeps it close to where it started. PPO was the algorithm behind InstructGPT and the first wave of ChatGPT-style assistants. Newer methods (DPO, GRPO) are easiest to understand as fixes for PPO's costs, so this chapter is the foundation for both.

Treats, a coach, and a leash

RLHF and PPO, told as training a dog. Six pictures.

The model writes several answers to a prompt, like a dog trying tricks. The reward model hands out treats: one number per answer. Nobody shows the model the right answer. It learns from its own attempts.

After each batch, the model makes high-scoring answers a little more likely and low-scoring ones a little less likely. The faint bars are before the update, the solid bars after. Numbers are illustrative.

Three treats is great on a hard prompt and poor on an easy one. So a second network, the value model or critic, predicts what each attempt should earn. Only the surprise, called the advantage, drives learning: positive pushes that answer up, negative pushes it down.

Without limits the dog sprints toward whatever the reward model over-rates. The KL penalty is a leash to the starting model: every bit of drift costs reward. A short leash (large β) is safe but changes little. A long leash (small β) lets the model reach the shortcut.

Scoring answers is expensive, so PPO squeezes a few updates out of each batch. To stop one batch from wrecking the model, it clips: once a token's probability has moved 20 percent from where the batch started, pushing further earns nothing.

That is the whole loop: generate, score, compare with what the critic expected, take a small clipped step on a leash, repeat. The rest of this chapter is the math behind each arrow.

The RL vocabulary, mapped to language models

Plain wordsTraining with treats

You teach a dog a trick by letting it try things and giving a treat when it does well. Over time it does more of whatever earned treats. Nobody shows the dog exactly what to do; it learns from the results.

RLHF works the same way. The model writes an answer. The reward model gives it a score, the treat. The training nudges the model toward answers like the high-scoring ones. The difference from earlier training is that the model learns from its own attempts, not from copying examples a person wrote.

For leadersWhy use RL at all

Supervised fine-tuning imitates demonstrations, so its ceiling is the quality of the demonstrators and it never sees what a bad answer looks like. RL optimizes outcomes directly. It can learn from comparisons that are cheaper than writing ideal answers, it learns what to avoid as well as what to do, and in principle it can exceed the people who labeled the data, because judging is easier than producing. The cost is engineering complexity, compute, and a new failure mode: the model optimizes the reward you wrote, not the behavior you meant.

The reward is sparse: one number for the whole response, arriving after the last token. Working out which of hundreds of tokens deserved the credit is the central difficulty, and most of PPO's machinery exists to handle it.

The KL-regularised objective

Plain wordsA leash

Left alone, a model chasing treats will find strange tricks the reward model happens to like. So training adds a leash: every time the model drifts away from how it used to write, it pays a small penalty. It can move toward better answers, but not so far that it stops sounding like itself.

Under the hoodThe objective and its exact solution
\[ \max_{\theta}\; \mathbb{E}_{x \sim \mathcal{D},\, y \sim \pi_\theta(\cdot\mid x)}\big[r_\phi(x, y)\big] \;-\; \beta\, \mathbb{D}_{\mathrm{KL}}\big(\pi_\theta(\cdot\mid x)\,\|\,\pi_{\text{ref}}(\cdot\mid x)\big) \]\(r_\phi\) is the reward model, \(\beta\) sets the leash length. Small \(\beta\): the policy may move far. Large \(\beta\): it stays near the reference.

In practice the KL term is applied per token, folded into the reward the RL algorithm sees:

\[ r_t = -\beta \log \frac{\pi_\theta(y_t \mid s_t)}{\pi_{\text{ref}}(y_t \mid s_t)} \;+\; \mathbb{1}[t = T]\; r_\phi(x, y) \]Every token pays for how much more likely the policy made it than the reference did. Summed over the response, the penalty is a single-sample estimate of the sequence KL.

This objective has a closed-form optimum, which matters for the next chapter:

\[ \pi^*(y \mid x) = \frac{1}{Z(x)}\, \pi_{\text{ref}}(y \mid x)\, \exp\!\Big(\tfrac{1}{\beta}\, r_\phi(x, y)\Big), \qquad Z(x) = \sum_{y} \pi_{\text{ref}}(y \mid x)\, e^{r_\phi(x,y)/\beta} \]Reweight the reference policy by exponentiated reward. You cannot compute this directly for a language model because the sum over all responses is intractable, which is why we need RL (or DPO's trick).
For leadersβ is a risk dial

The KL coefficient is the single most consequential knob in RLHF. Set it too high and the run is expensive and changes nothing. Set it too low and the policy finds the reward model's blind spots: the widget below shows true quality rising at first and then falling as the length-biased answer takes over. Set the length bias to zero and the problem disappears, which is the point: the damage comes from the gap between the reward model and what you actually want, and β controls how hard the optimizer can exploit that gap.

Policy gradients: learning from your own samples

Plain wordsCredit and blame

After an answer gets a score, every word in it gets a little of the credit or blame. Words in high-scoring answers become a bit more likely next time; words in low-scoring answers become a bit less likely. To be fair, you compare each score to what you expected. A 7 out of 10 is good news on a hard question and bad news on an easy one.

Under the hoodREINFORCE, baselines, advantages

The policy gradient theorem gives an unbiased gradient of expected reward using only samples from the policy:

\[ \nabla_\theta J(\theta) = \mathbb{E}_{y \sim \pi_\theta}\Big[\sum_{t=1}^{T} \nabla_\theta \log \pi_\theta(y_t \mid s_t)\,\big(R(y) - b(s_t)\big)\Big] \]REINFORCE (Williams 1992). \(R\) is the return; \(b\) is any baseline that does not depend on the action. Subtracting it leaves the expectation unchanged and cuts variance.

The best simple baseline is the expected return from the current state, the value \(V(s_t)\). The difference is the advantage, \(A_t = R_t - V(s_t)\): how much better this continuation went than expected. Positive advantage raises the probability of the token taken; negative advantage lowers it. Without a good baseline, gradient estimates on long responses are so noisy that training stalls.

The critic and generalized advantage estimation

Under the hoodGAE

PPO for language models trains a value model (the critic), usually initialized from the reward model, to predict \(V(s_t)\) at every token. Advantages are then computed with generalized advantage estimation (Schulman et al. 2016):

\[ \delta_t = r_t + \gamma V(s_{t+1}) - V(s_t), \qquad \hat{A}_t = \sum_{l=0}^{T-t} (\gamma\lambda)^l\, \delta_{t+l} \]\(\delta_t\) is the one-step surprise. \(\lambda\) trades bias for variance: \(\lambda = 0\) trusts the critic completely, \(\lambda = 1\) uses the full Monte Carlo return. RLHF commonly uses \(\gamma = 1\) and \(\lambda\) around 0.95.
def gae(rewards, values, gamma=1.0, lam=0.95):
    """rewards[t]: per-token reward (KL penalty, plus the RM score on the last token).
       values[t]:  critic estimate V(s_t). Returns advantages and value targets."""
    T = len(rewards)
    adv, last = [0.0] * T, 0.0
    for t in reversed(range(T)):
        next_v = values[t + 1] if t + 1 < T else 0.0   # no value after the final token
        delta = rewards[t] + gamma * next_v - values[t]
        last = delta + gamma * lam * last                # discounted sum of future deltas
        adv[t] = last
    returns = [a + v for a, v in zip(adv, values)]       # regression targets for the critic
    return adv, returns

adv, ret = gae([-0.01, -0.02, 0.9], [0.5, 0.6, 0.7])
print([round(a, 3) for a in adv])   # [0.346, 0.27, 0.2]

PPO's clipped surrogate

Plain wordsSmall steps only

Collecting answers and scoring them is expensive, so you want to learn as much as possible from each batch. But if you change the model a lot based on one batch, you can wreck it, and the next batch of answers comes from a broken model. PPO's rule is simple: within one batch, no single answer's probability may move by more than a fixed fraction, say 20 percent. Once it has moved that far, PPO stops pushing on it.

Under the hoodThe objective

Proximal Policy Optimization (Schulman et al. 2017) reuses each batch of rollouts for a few gradient steps. Let \(\rho_t(\theta) = \pi_\theta(y_t \mid s_t) / \pi_{\theta_{\text{old}}}(y_t \mid s_t)\) be the probability ratio between the policy being updated and the one that generated the data.

\[ \mathcal{L}^{\text{CLIP}}(\theta) = \mathbb{E}_t\Big[\min\big(\rho_t(\theta)\,\hat{A}_t,\; \mathrm{clip}(\rho_t(\theta),\, 1-\epsilon,\, 1+\epsilon)\,\hat{A}_t\big)\Big] \]Maximized. For a good token (\(\hat A_t > 0\)) the gain stops once its probability has risen by a factor \(1+\epsilon\). For a bad token the gain stops once it has fallen to \(1-\epsilon\). The min makes the bound pessimistic: if an update already went the wrong way, the full penalty stays.

The full loss adds a value-function regression term and often an entropy bonus. The standard \(\epsilon\) is 0.2.

\[ \mathcal{L} = -\mathcal{L}^{\text{CLIP}} + c_v\,\mathbb{E}\big[(V_\theta(s_t) - \hat R_t)^2\big] - c_e\,\mathbb{E}\big[\mathcal{H}(\pi_\theta(\cdot \mid s_t))\big] \]

In code:

import torch

def ppo_loss(logp_new, logp_old, adv, values_new, returns, eps=0.2, vf_coef=0.5):
    """All tensors are per response token, already masked to exclude prompt and padding."""
    ratio = torch.exp(logp_new - logp_old)                 # pi_theta / pi_old, per token
    adv = (adv - adv.mean()) / (adv.std() + 1e-8)          # whitening is standard and stabilizes training
    unclipped = ratio * adv
    clipped = torch.clamp(ratio, 1 - eps, 1 + eps) * adv
    policy_loss = -torch.min(unclipped, clipped).mean()    # minus: optimizers minimize
    value_loss = 0.5 * (values_new - returns).pow(2).mean()
    return policy_loss + vf_coef * value_loss

# one token, eps = 0.2: the objective stops rewarding moves past the clip boundary
for r, a in [(1.5, 1.0), (0.6, -1.0), (1.1, 1.0)]:
    print(r, a, min(r * a, max(0.8, min(1.2, r)) * a))     # 1.2, -0.8, 1.1

Four models in memory: why PPO is expensive

Four people in the training room

Why PPO needs so much memory, one seat at a time.

The student, the policy, writes every answer. It is the network being trained, so it needs weights, gradients, and optimizer state: about 16 bytes per parameter.

The judge, the reward model, scores each answer. It is frozen, so it needs only its weights, about 2 bytes per parameter in bf16.

The coach, the value model, predicts what each answer should score so the policy can learn from surprises. It is trained too, and it is often as big as the policy.

The photo of the student on day one, the reference model, supplies the probabilities for the KL leash. Frozen, but always in memory.

Add it up with rough rules of thumb (16 bytes per trained parameter, 2 per frozen one) and a 70B run needs about 2.5 TB before counting activations or generation. That bill is why the next two chapters exist: DPO sends the judge and coach home, and GRPO sends the coach home.

For leadersThe bill

A PPO run for a language model keeps four networks alive at once:

Policy

Being trained. Weights, gradients, and optimizer state.

Value model

Being trained. Often the same size as the policy, so it doubles training memory.

Reward model

Frozen. Scores every rollout.

Reference model

Frozen. Provides log-probs for the KL penalty.

Rough arithmetic, labeled as such: mixed-precision Adam needs about 16 bytes per trainable parameter (weights, master copy, two moment estimates, gradients), and a frozen bf16 model needs about 2. With a 70B-parameter policy and value model, the two trainable networks need roughly 2.2 TB before activations, and the two frozen ones another 0.3 TB. On top of that, rollouts are generated token by token, and generation often dominates wall-clock time. This cost profile explains the next two chapters: DPO removes the RL loop and the reward and value models; GRPO keeps on-policy RL but removes the value model.

Principal asks
What fraction of wall-clock time is generation versus training? Is the value model the same size as the policy, and did you try sharing a backbone? How many rollouts per prompt, and how many PPO epochs per batch? What is the throughput in prompts per hour, and what does one additional point of win rate cost in GPU-hours?

The InstructGPT recipe

For leadersThe template everyone copied
  1. Supervised fine-tuning on labeler-written demonstrations (about 13,000 training prompts). See supervised fine-tuning.
  2. Reward model trained on labeler rankings of 4 to 9 model outputs per prompt (about 33,000 prompts).
  3. PPO against the reward model with a per-token KL penalty to the SFT model (about 31,000 prompts), optionally mixing in pretraining gradients ("PPO-ptx").

The headline result in Ouyang et al. (2022): labelers preferred outputs from a 1.3B-parameter InstructGPT model over the 175B GPT-3 base model. Alignment work moved the needle more than a hundred-fold increase in parameters on the thing users actually experienced. The paper also documented an alignment tax: PPO caused regressions on some public NLP benchmarks (such as SQuAD and DROP), which mixing pretraining updates back in largely recovered.

Reward hacking, the alignment tax, and calibration

Plain wordsFinding the loophole

If a cleaning robot is rewarded for not seeing mess, it may learn to cover its camera. RL finds whatever earns reward, including loopholes nobody intended. In language models the loopholes are usually boring: longer answers, more confident tone, telling the user what they want to hear.

Watch out
RLHF can make a model look better to raters while making its probabilities worse. The GPT-4 technical report (OpenAI 2023) showed the pretrained model was well calibrated on multiple-choice questions and the post-trained model much less so. If your product uses model confidence (to abstain, route, or show uncertainty), measure calibration after every post-training stage, not just before.
