Reading a shape: work out what you have before choosing a method

This is the practical skill: look at data and work out what you have. The checklist takes about two minutes and catches nearly everything that would otherwise invalidate the analysis that follows.

Plain wordsLook before you measure

A doctor looks at a patient before prescribing. A summary statistic is a prescription. Before you choose one, look at the data: draw a picture, check whether it leans, check whether there are two groups hiding in it, and check whether something chopped off the top. Most mistakes come from skipping the look.

The two-minute checklist

  1. Plot a histogram. Always. Before any number. Summary statistics hide bimodality, censoring and outliers with total reliability.
  2. Divide mean by median. Above 1.2 is right skewed, below 0.85 is left skewed.
  3. Divide variance by mean for counts. Near 1 means Poisson is plausible. Well above 1 means overdispersed.
  4. Check whether values are whole numbers, and whether they are bounded (at 0, at 1, at n).
  5. Try a log scale. If the shape turns symmetric, you have something lognormal.
  6. Look for a spike at a round number. That is censoring, not data.
  7. Look for two humps. That is two populations.
\[ \frac{s^2}{\bar{x}} \approx 1 \;\Rightarrow\; \text{Poisson}; \qquad\qquad \frac{s^2}{\bar{x}} \gg 1 \;\Rightarrow\; \text{overdispersed} \]The dispersion check for count data: variance divided by mean.
Under the hoodThe checklist as a function
import numpy as np
from scipy import stats

def diagnose(x):
    """Run this before choosing any method. Takes two seconds, catches most errors."""
    x = np.asarray(x, dtype=float)
    n = len(x)
    m, med = x.mean(), np.median(x)
    var = x.var(ddof=1)

    out = {
        "n": n,
        "mean": m,
        "median": med,
        "skew_ratio": m / med if med else float("inf"),   # >1.2 = right skewed
        "var_over_mean": var / m if m else float("inf"),  # ~1 = Poisson-like
        "whole_numbers": bool(np.all(np.abs(x - np.round(x)) < 1e-9)),
        "min": x.min(), "max": x.max(),
        "zeros": int((x == 0).sum()),                     # blocks a log transform
    }

    # Does a log transform fix the skew? Only meaningful for positive data.
    if x.min() > 0:
        out["skew_raw"] = float(stats.skew(x))
        out["skew_logged"] = float(stats.skew(np.log(x)))
        out["log_helps"] = abs(out["skew_logged"]) < abs(out["skew_raw"]) / 2

    # CENSORING CHECK: is there a pile-up at a single value near the max?
    top = x[x >= np.quantile(x, 0.98)]
    if len(top):
        common = stats.mode(np.round(top, 6), keepdims=False)
        out["censoring_suspect"] = bool(common.count > 0.01 * n)
        out["suspect_value"] = float(common.mode)

    return out


lat = stats.lognorm.rvs(0.9, scale=3, size=4000, random_state=0)
for k, v in diagnose(lat).items():
    print(k, v)
# skew_ratio 1.51 -> right skewed, do not report the mean
# log_helps True  -> it is lognormal, analyse on the log scale

The log scale check

QQ plots, once you want more than eyeballing

A QQ plot compares your data's quantiles against a reference distribution's. A straight line means a good match. The way it bends tells you how it differs.

Two humps means two populations

Eval lens
When you see this, stop reaching for statistics and go find the segmenting variable. Split by cache hit, retry status, repo size, task category. Two clean distributions beat one messy one every time, and the split is usually the actual finding.

Censoring: the one that hides

Watch out
Timeouts do not create outliers, they create censored data. Every value at the cap is really "at least this much". Your mean, p95 and p99 are all biased downward by an amount you cannot see from the data. Report the censoring rate next to any percentile, or use survival methods. Check for this before reaching for any outlier technique.
Name that distribution
A random sample with its diagnostics. Work through the checklist above, make a guess, and get the reasoning either way. Do ten of these and the skill sticks.
For leadersMake "show me the histogram" a habit

The cheapest review step in evaluation is asking to see the raw distribution before anyone argues about the summary. It surfaces three failure modes that no amount of downstream rigour can fix.

Hidden segments

Two humps mean two populations. A change that helps one and hurts the other can net to zero on average. The useful question becomes "which segment moved?"

Silent censoring

A spike at a timeout or token cap means the worst cases were truncated. A model that times out more often can look faster. Insist the censoring rate is reported beside every percentile.

Wrong scale

Latency, cost and token counts are multiplicative. Comparing them on a linear scale overweights the tail. Ratios and log-scale comparisons are usually the honest framing.

Principal asks
"What fraction of runs hit the timeout or the max-token limit, before and after?" If that fraction changed, every latency and length metric in the report is comparing different things, and the headline needs to be redone.
