Correlation is not causation: what else makes two numbers move together

Every dashboard is full of pairs of numbers that rise and fall together. Some of those pairs are cause and effect. Many are not, and acting on the wrong ones wastes quarters. This chapter covers what a correlation measures, the four ordinary ways a correlation appears without any causal link, Simpson's paradox, causal diagrams for deciding what to "control for", and the ladder of evidence that ends at a randomized experiment. Each idea gets an everyday example first and an AI example second.

Does ice cream cause drowning?

The classic confounder, then the same trap in a product dashboard. All numbers are illustrative.

A seaside town tracks two numbers every month. Ice cream sales and drownings climb together into summer and fall together into winter.

Plot one against the other, week by week, and the link is strong: a correlation of r = 0.89. Taken at face value, banning ice cream would save lives.

A third thing drives both. Hot weather sells ice cream and sends people into the sea. Temperature is a confounder: a common cause of the two things you measured.

Compare only weeks with similar weather and the link almost vanishes (within-band correlations 0.02, -0.10, 0.09). Comparing like with like on the confounder is what "controlling for" it means.

The same shape in a product review: feature users retain 29 points better. But people who were already engaged try more features. Within each engagement group the feature is worth 5 points. The 29 was mostly the confounder.

What a correlation is

Plain wordsTwo things that tend to move together

Two measurements are correlated when knowing one helps you guess the other. Taller parents tend to have taller children. Bigger houses tend to cost more. Days with more ice cream sold tend to have more drownings.

The usual summary is a single number, r, between −1 and +1. Near +1: when one is high, the other is almost always high. Near −1: when one is high, the other is almost always low. Near 0: no straight-line pattern. It says nothing about why the two move together. It is a description of the data, like an average.

For leadersGood for predicting, silent about intervening

A correlation answers "if I see X high, should I expect Y high?" A decision almost always asks a different question: "if I make X high, will Y go up?" The first is prediction and correlations are fine for it: a feature that correlates with churn is a useful churn predictor whatever the reason. The second is intervention, and a correlation alone cannot answer it.

  • Use correlations to find leads. They are cheap and they tell you where to look.
  • Do not fund an intervention on a correlation alone. Ask what else could produce it, then ask for the cheapest experiment that would tell the stories apart.
  • Strength is not evidence of causation. The ice cream correlation above is 0.9. Many real causal effects show up as weak correlations because other causes add noise.
Under the hoodPearson's r and its blind spots

Pearson's correlation is the covariance rescaled by both standard deviations (see the distributions chapter for SD and covariance):

\[ r = \frac{\operatorname{Cov}(X,Y)}{s_X\, s_Y} = \frac{\sum_i (x_i-\bar x)(y_i-\bar y)}{\sqrt{\sum_i (x_i-\bar x)^2}\,\sqrt{\sum_i (y_i-\bar y)^2}}, \qquad \hat\beta_{Y\sim X} = r\,\frac{s_Y}{s_X} \]r is unitless and lies in [−1, 1]. The least-squares slope of Y on X is r times the ratio of standard deviations, so slope and correlation always share a sign. \(r^2\) is the share of Y's variance a straight line in X accounts for.
  • Linear only. A perfect U-shape can have r = 0. Zero correlation does not mean independence.
  • Fragile to outliers and shapes. Anscombe's quartet (Anscombe 1973) is four datasets with the same means, variances, regression line and r ≈ 0.82, one of which is a curve and one a single outlier. Plot before you summarise.
  • Restricted range shrinks r. Correlate SAT scores with grades among admitted students only and r falls, because admission removed the low scorers. This is a selection effect, covered below.
  • r has sampling error. With n pairs, Fisher's transform \(z=\tanh^{-1} r\) has standard error about \(1/\sqrt{n-3}\). On 30 points, r = 0.40 has a 95% interval of roughly 0.05 to 0.66 (intervals).

Four ways to get a correlation without causation

Same pattern, five different worlds

Each picture is a causal diagram: boxes are things you could measure, arrows point from cause to effect.

Confounding. Temperature causes both ice cream sales and drownings. The two are correlated, and neither causes the other.

Reverse causation. The arrow runs the other way from the one you assumed. Wind turns windmills; fast windmills do not make wind. In products: unhappy users file more tickets, so "tickets cause churn" reads the arrow backwards.

Selection. You only look at cases that passed a filter. Restaurants survive if the food or the location is great, so among the survivors the two look like a trade-off even though they are unrelated across all restaurants that ever opened.

Chance. Slice the data 20 ways and test each at the 5% level, and one slice will usually show a "significant" pattern by luck alone: the chance of at least one false alarm is 1 − 0.9520 ≈ 64%.

Causation. Watering makes plants grow. The scatter can look identical in all five worlds. Only intervening, setting X yourself and watching Y, tells this world apart from the other four.

Plain wordsFour suspects to rule out first

Before you believe "X causes Y", line up four suspects and rule each one out.

  1. A hidden common cause. Something else pushes both up. Hot weather, wealthy neighbourhoods, users who were already keen.
  2. The arrow is backwards. Y causes X. People who feel ill see the doctor more; the doctor is not making them ill.
  3. You only saw some of the cases. A filter, often invisible, decided which cases reached your spreadsheet.
  4. Luck. You looked at enough comparisons that one was bound to look striking.

If all four are ruled out, causation is the explanation left standing. The cleanest way to rule out all four at once is an experiment where a coin flip decides who gets X.

Ranking them by how often they bite: confounding and selection cause most expensive mistakes, because they survive large samples. Chance shrinks with more data and replication; confounding and selection do not shrink at all.

Under the hoodHow big the spurious part is

Take a linear world with a confounder Z (variance 1), a treatment \(X = aZ + \varepsilon_X\) with \(\operatorname{Var}\varepsilon_X = 1\), and an outcome \(Y = bX + cZ + \varepsilon_Y\). The true causal effect of X on Y is b. The naive regression slope is

\[ \hat\beta_{\text{naive}} \;\to\; \frac{\operatorname{Cov}(X,Y)}{\operatorname{Var}(X)} = b + c\,\frac{\operatorname{Cov}(X,Z)}{\operatorname{Var}(X)} = b + \underbrace{\frac{c\,a}{a^2+1}}_{\text{confounding bias}} \]a is how strongly Z drives X, c how strongly Z drives Y. The bias has the sign of \(c\cdot a\): same-direction pushes inflate the estimate, opposite-direction pushes deflate it and can flip its sign. It does not depend on sample size.

The other suspects have their own signatures. Reverse causation gives a nonzero slope with b = 0 because X is a function of Y. Selection on a variable S that depends on both X and Y induces correlation inside the selected set even when X and Y are independent (Berkson 1946, below). Chance gives a nonzero estimate whose distribution is centred on zero; its rate is controlled by the significance threshold and the number of tests, \(1-(1-\alpha)^m\) for m independent tests.

Confounding, up close

Plain wordsCompare like with like

A confounder is a hidden third thing that pushes on both of the numbers you are comparing. The fix is to compare like with like: hot days with hot days, keen users with keen users, hard questions with hard questions. If the link survives inside each group, it was not just the confounder.

An eval example. Across a maths benchmark, problems where the model "thinks" longer have lower pass rates. It looks as if thinking hurts. But hard problems make the model think longer, and hard problems fail more. Group the problems by difficulty and, inside each group, more thinking goes with more correct answers. In the widget above, pick the thinking-tokens scenario and press stratify to see the sign flip.

For leadersWhen someone says "we controlled for it"

Adjusting for measured confounders is often the best you can do with logs, and it is far better than a raw comparison. Three limits decide how much to trust it:

  • Only measured confounders are removed. "Prior engagement" measured as last week's sessions is a proxy. Whatever it misses (motivation, team size, use case) stays in the estimate.
  • Coarse buckets leave confounding behind. Splitting difficulty into "easy" and "hard" removes some of it; the widget's five strata still leave a little. Finer strata or a model of the confounder do better, if there is enough data in each cell.
  • Adjusting for the wrong variable makes things worse. See the section on mediators and colliders below. More controls is not automatically safer.

When the decision is large and the intervention is under your control, skip the argument and randomize (randomization, A/B tests).

Under the hoodStratify, then re-weight

With a discrete confounder Z, estimate the effect inside each stratum, then average the stratum effects with the population's stratum weights. This is standardization, the simplest form of the back-door adjustment formula (Pearl 2009):

\[ \mathbb{E}[Y \mid do(X{=}x)] = \sum_z \mathbb{E}[Y \mid X{=}x, Z{=}z]\; P(Z{=}z), \qquad \text{ATE} = \sum_z \big(\bar y_{1z} - \bar y_{0z}\big)\, P(z) \]\(do(X{=}x)\) means "set X to x by intervention", as opposed to observing it. \(\bar y_{1z}\) and \(\bar y_{0z}\) are the outcome means for treated and untreated units in stratum z. Valid when Z blocks every back-door path and every stratum contains both treated and untreated units (positivity).

With continuous confounders, regression does the same job: include Z as a covariate and read off the coefficient on X. The Frisch–Waugh–Lovell theorem says that coefficient equals the slope of Y-residualised-on-Z against X-residualised-on-Z, which is exactly what the widget's "Adjusted for Z" card computes.

# Retention by engagement stratum and feature use. Illustrative counts.
# key: (stratum, used_feature) -> (users, retained)
TABLE = {
    ("engaged", True): (800, 640), ("engaged", False): (200, 150),
    ("casual", True):  (200, 80),  ("casual", False):  (800, 280),
}
STRATA = ["engaged", "casual"]

def rate(cells):
    users = sum(u for u, _ in cells)
    kept = sum(k for _, k in cells)
    return kept / users

def naive_lift(t):
    # pools the strata, so the stratum mix leaks into the comparison
    used = rate([t[(s, True)] for s in STRATA])
    unused = rate([t[(s, False)] for s in STRATA])
    return used - unused

def adjusted_lift(t):
    # compare within each stratum, then weight by the stratum's share of all users
    total = sum(u for u, _ in t.values())
    lift = 0.0
    for s in STRATA:
        n_s = t[(s, True)][0] + t[(s, False)][0]
        diff = rate([t[(s, True)]]) - rate([t[(s, False)]])
        lift += diff * n_s / total
    return lift

print(f"{naive_lift(TABLE):.3f}")     # 0.290  what the dashboard shows

];
const STRATA: [&str; 2] = ["engaged", "casual"];

fn cell(s: &str, used: bool) -> (f64, f64) {
    let c = TABLE.iter().find(|c| c.0 == s && c.1 == used).unwrap();
    (c.2, c.3)
}

fn rate(cells: &[(f64, f64)]) -> f64 {
    let users: f64 = cells.iter().map(|c| c.0).sum();
    let kept: f64 = cells.iter().map(|c| c.1).sum();
    kept / users
}

fn naive_lift() -> f64 {
    // pools the strata, so the stratum mix leaks into the comparison
    let used: Vec<_> = STRATA.iter().map(|s| cell(s, true)).collect();
    let unused: Vec<_> = STRATA.iter().map(|s| cell(s, false)).collect();
    rate(&used) - rate(&unused)
}

fn adjusted_lift() -> f64 {
    // compare within each stratum, then weight by the stratum's share of all users
    let total: f64 = TABLE.iter().map(|c| c.2).sum();
    let mut lift = 0.0;
    for s in STRATA {
        let n_s = cell(s, true).0 + cell(s, false).0;
        let diff = rate(&[cell(s, true)]) - rate(&[cell(s, false)]);
        lift += diff * n_s / total;
    }
    lift
}

fn main() {

    println!("{:.3}", adjusted_lift()); // 0.050  feature's effect within like users
}

Simpson's paradox: when the total disagrees with every part

Plain wordsTwo models, two task mixes

Two teams each report a pass rate for their model. Model A scores 54%. Model B scores 74%. B looks far better. Now split the tasks into easy and hard:

Easy tasksHard tasksAll tasks
Model A180 of 200 = 90%360 of 800 = 45%540 of 1,000 = 54%
Model B672 of 800 = 84%72 of 200 = 36%744 of 1,000 = 74%

A wins on easy tasks and on hard tasks, yet loses overall. Nothing is wrong with the arithmetic. A was tested mostly on hard tasks and B mostly on easy ones, so each total is mostly a measure of the task mix. This reversal is Simpson's paradox (named for Simpson 1951, described earlier by Yule and Pearson). Numbers are illustrative.

For leadersThe Berkeley case, told accurately

The famous real example is graduate admissions at the University of California, Berkeley, for fall 1973. Of 8,442 men who applied, 44% were admitted; of 4,321 women, 35%. Bickel, Hammel and O'Connell (1975, Science) looked department by department. Women had applied disproportionately to departments that admitted few applicants of either sex. Once that was accounted for, they found no overall bias against women, and a small but statistically significant bias in favour of women. They concluded that the aggregate gap was misleading and pointed to earlier screening in education, which steered women toward crowded fields, as the real issue.

Two leadership lessons follow:

  • A headline gap between groups with different mixes is not yet a finding. Ask for the per-segment numbers and a comparison on a common mix before any conclusion.
  • Which view is "right" depends on the causal story, not on the arithmetic. If the segment is a confounder (task difficulty, department), the per-segment comparison answers the causal question. If the segment is caused by the treatment (a mediator), the aggregate can be the right answer. The diagram section below makes this precise.
Under the hoodWeighted averages with different weights

An overall rate is a weighted average of segment rates, weighted by that model's own mix:

\[ \bar p_A = \sum_g w_{A,g}\, p_{A,g}, \qquad \bar p_B = \sum_g w_{B,g}\, p_{B,g}, \qquad \bar p^{\,\text{std}}_M = \sum_g w^{*}_g\, p_{M,g} \]\(w_{M,g}\) is the share of model M's tasks in segment g and \(p_{M,g}\) its pass rate there. A reversal needs the weights to differ between models and to be correlated with segment difficulty. Direct standardization replaces each model's own weights by one reference mix \(w^*\), such as production traffic.

The six largest Berkeley departments, as reproduced in R's UCBAdmissions dataset from the 1975 study, show the pattern. In four of the six, women were admitted at a higher rate. Aggregated, men lead by 14 points:

def overall(cells):
    # each group's own mix sets the weights
    return sum(k for _, k in cells.values()) / sum(n for n, _ in cells.values())

def standardized(cells, mix):
    # one shared mix for everyone: compares rates, not mixes
    return sum(mix[g] * k / n for g, (n, k) in cells.items())

def pooled_mix(groups):
    total = {}
    for cells in groups.values():
        for g, (n, _) in cells.items():
            total[g] = total.get(g, 0) + n
    s = sum(total.values())
    return {g: v / s for g, v in total.items()}

type Cells = Vec<(&'static str, f64, f64)>;

fn overall(cells: &Cells) -> f64 {
    // each group's own mix sets the weights
    let k: f64 = cells.iter().map(|c| c.2).sum();
    let n: f64 = cells.iter().map(|c| c.1).sum();
    k / n
}

fn standardized(cells: &Cells, mix: &[(&str, f64)]) -> f64 {
    // one shared mix for everyone: compares rates, not mixes
    cells.iter().map(|(g, n, k)| mix.iter().find(|m| m.0 == *g).unwrap().1 * k / n).sum()
}

fn pooled_mix(groups: &[(&str, Cells)]) -> Vec<(&'static str, f64)> {
    let mut total: Vec<(&'static str, f64)> = Vec::new();
    for (_, cells) in groups {
        for (g, n, _) in cells {
            match total.iter_mut().find(|t| t.0 == *g) {
                Some(t) => t.1 += n,
                None => total.push((g, *n)),
            }
        }
    }
    let s: f64 = total.iter().map(|t| t.1).sum();
    total.into_iter().map(|(g, v)| (g, v / s)).collect()
}

Selection and colliders: Berkson's paradox

Why good restaurants seem to have bad locations

A filter you never see can manufacture a trade-off. Simulated restaurants, illustrative.

Every restaurant that opened in a city over ten years. Food quality and location quality are unrelated: r = -0.02.

Restaurants survive if the food is great, or the location is great, or both are decent. Everything below the line closed within two years, so it never appears in a guide or on a map.

Among the survivors, food and location now look opposed: r = -0.45. A great restaurant in a great spot is rare not because the two clash but because the bad-food, bad-spot places are missing.

The same pattern appears whenever rows were kept because of one thing or another. The fix is to analyse the full pool before the filter, or to model the filter explicitly.

Plain wordsThe filter creates the pattern

Joseph Berkson noticed in 1946 that two diseases could look linked among hospital patients even when they were unrelated in the population, because having either one raised your chance of being in hospital. Looking only at hospital patients, you are looking at people who passed a filter.

Any filter that lets a case through for having "this or that" will make "this" and "that" look like opposites among the cases that got through. The people you dated were either kind or attractive, so among them the attractive ones seem less kind. The papers that got into a conference were either novel or rigorous, so among accepted papers novelty and rigour seem to trade off.

For leadersAsk what decided which rows exist

Selection bias is hard to see because the missing rows leave no trace in the table. Every analysis built on a filtered population deserves the question "what decided which rows are in here?"

  • Conversations flagged for review were flagged by a classifier or by angry users. Patterns inside the flagged set describe the flagger as much as the model.
  • Benchmarks that made it into the eval suite were kept because they separated models or because they were cheap. Correlations between benchmark properties inside the suite can be manufactured by that choice.
  • Models on a shortlist were kept for being strong or cheap. "Stronger models cost more" among the shortlist does not tell you about the market.

The companion problem, measuring only the units that survived, gets its own chapter: who is in your sample.

Under the hoodConditioning on a common effect

A collider is a variable with two arrows pointing into it, \(X \rightarrow S \leftarrow Y\). With X and Y independent, conditioning on S (by filtering, stratifying or adding it to a regression) makes them dependent. For the restaurant rule \(S = \mathbf{1}[X+Y>t]\) with X and Y uniform on [0, 1] and t = 1.05, the simulation above gives r = -0.45 among survivors from r ≈ 0 overall.

\[ X \perp\!\!\!\perp Y \quad\not\Rightarrow\quad X \perp\!\!\!\perp Y \mid S \qquad \text{when } X \rightarrow S \leftarrow Y \]Independence in the full population does not survive conditioning on a common effect. Explaining away: once you know a restaurant survived, learning its food is bad tells you its location must be good.

Selection does not need a sharp threshold. Any selection probability that depends on both X and Y, such as a response rate that depends on satisfaction and on usage, induces some dependence. The remedies are to observe the unselected units, to model the selection probability and reweight (inverse probability weighting, if the probability can be estimated), or to bound the effect with sensitivity analysis.

Causal diagrams: deciding what to control for

Back doors, mediators and colliders

A causal diagram (a DAG) tells you which variables to hold fixed and which to leave alone.

Does coffee cause heart disease? In older data, coffee drinkers also smoked more. There are two roads from coffee to heart disease: the direct one you care about, and a back door through smoking that starts with an arrow pointing into coffee.

Hold smoking fixed by comparing smokers with smokers and non-smokers with non-smokers. The back door closes, and whatever association remains flows through the front door.

A mediator sits on the causal road. Tutoring raises grades mostly by getting homework done. "Controlling for" homework compares tutored and untutored students who did the same homework, which hides most of what tutoring does.

A collider is caused by both. Skill and charisma both help you get hired. Analyse only hires and a fake trade-off appears, exactly like the restaurants. Controlling for a collider opens a path that was closed.

The rule of thumb: close back doors by controlling for confounders, leave mediators alone unless you want only the direct effect, and never control for colliders. Drawing the diagram first is how you tell which is which.

Plain wordsDraw the arrows before you do the maths

A causal diagram is a sketch with a box for each thing and an arrow from each cause to its effect. It has no loops: nothing causes itself, which is why it is called a directed acyclic graph, or DAG.

Any route between X and Y along the arrows (in either direction) can carry association. The route you want is the one that leaves X along its own arrows and arrives at Y. A back-door path is any route that starts with an arrow coming into X: it carries association that has nothing to do with X's effect. Your job is to block every back door without blocking the front door, and without opening new doors by holding a collider fixed.

For leadersMake the diagram part of the review

Most "we controlled for everything" analyses fail because nobody wrote down what causes what. A one-page diagram is cheap, and it turns a regression into a claim people can argue with.

  • Ask for the diagram before the coefficients. Every arrow is an assumption. Disagreements about arrows are the real disagreements, and they are domain questions, not statistics questions.
  • Ask for each control's role. Confounder, mediator, collider, or just a predictor of Y? Controls added "because they were available" are where bias creeps in.
  • Ask what is unmeasured. If a plausible confounder is missing from the data, no adjustment fixes it. That is the moment to fund an experiment instead.
Worked problem
A team claims a new system prompt raised user satisfaction "even after controlling for response length and refusal rate." The prompt changes length and refusals, and those change satisfaction: both are mediators. Controlling for them estimates only the part of the effect that does not go through length or refusals. If the question is "should we ship the prompt?", the total effect is the one that matters, so drop those controls or, better, run the A/B test.
Under the hoodd-separation and the back-door criterion

Three building blocks decide whether a path carries association (Pearl 2009):

ShapeNamePath open whenConditioning on the middle
\(A \rightarrow M \rightarrow B\)Chain (mediator)M not conditioned onblocks it
\(A \leftarrow Z \rightarrow B\)Fork (confounder)Z not conditioned onblocks it
\(A \rightarrow C \leftarrow B\)ColliderC or a descendant of C is conditioned onopens it

A path is blocked if any node on it blocks it. X and Y are d-separated given a set Z when every path between them is blocked; in a correctly drawn DAG this implies conditional independence.

Back-door criterion. A set Z is a valid adjustment set for the effect of X on Y if (1) no member of Z is a descendant of X, and (2) Z blocks every path between X and Y that begins with an arrow into X. Then the adjustment formula \(P(y\mid do(x)) = \sum_z P(y\mid x,z)\,P(z)\) identifies the causal effect from observational data. Condition (1) is what rules out mediators and colliders that sit downstream of X.

Known "bad controls" (Cinelli, Forney and Pearl 2022, "A crash course in good and bad controls"): mediators, which remove part of the effect; colliders and their descendants, which open spurious paths; and pre-treatment variables that only cause X (instruments), which add variance and can amplify bias from unmeasured confounders. The M-bias case shows that even a pre-treatment variable can be a collider: \(X \leftarrow U_1 \rightarrow M \leftarrow U_2 \rightarrow Y\).

The ladder of evidence

Plain wordsFrom "someone said" to "we flipped a coin"

Evidence for "X causes Y" comes in grades. A story from one user is the bottom rung. A chart of two numbers moving together is one rung up. Comparing like with like is higher. The top is an experiment where a coin flip decides who gets X, because the coin cannot be confounded, cannot be caused by Y, and cannot select who shows up.

Rung 2 is fine for picking what to investigate. Shipping decisions that are expensive to reverse deserve rung 5. Offline evals are unusual because you control the inputs: you can almost always run the experiment instead of arguing about observational data (randomization, A/B tests).

Under the hoodQuasi-experiments in one line each
  • Difference-in-differences. Effect \(= (\bar y^{T}_{\text{after}}-\bar y^{T}_{\text{before}}) - (\bar y^{C}_{\text{after}}-\bar y^{C}_{\text{before}})\). Assumes the treated and control groups would have moved in parallel without treatment. Check pre-period trends.
  • Regression discontinuity. When treatment switches on at a threshold (a safety classifier score of 0.8, a usage tier), compare units just above and just below. Assumes nothing else jumps at the threshold and units cannot precisely sort themselves across it.
  • Instrumental variables. Find something that shifts X but affects Y only through X, such as a randomized nudge to try a feature (an encouragement design). Effect \(=\operatorname{Cov}(Z,Y)/\operatorname{Cov}(Z,X)\), the Wald estimator. Weak instruments give wild estimates.
  • Matching and propensity scores. Pair treated and untreated units with similar covariates, or weight by the inverse probability of treatment. Same assumption as regression adjustment: no unmeasured confounders.

Where this bites in AI work

Feature usage and retention

Users of a new tool retain better. Prior engagement confounds it, retained users had more time to find the tool (reverse direction), and the dashboard only counts users still active. A randomized holdout answers it in weeks.

Longer answers, higher judge scores

A judge model may reward length itself, or harder questions may draw longer, better answers. Length-controlled AlpacaEval regresses out length and raised its Spearman correlation with Chatbot Arena from 0.94 to 0.98 (Dubois et al. 2024).

Benchmark gains and data overlap

Across models, the gap between GSM8K and the fresh GSM1k set rose with each model's probability of generating GSM8K examples (Spearman r² = 0.36; Zhang et al. 2024). That is a correlation across model families with different recipes.

Tool use and failure

Trajectories with more tool calls fail more often. Hard tasks cause both. Compare at fixed task, or randomize the tool budget per task.

Plain wordsSame four suspects, new costumes

Each tile above is a correlation someone will one day present as a cause. For each, ask the four questions: is there a hidden common cause, could the arrow run backwards, were some cases filtered out, and how many things did we look at? Then ask the best question of all: can we just try it, on purpose, and watch?

For leadersThe intervention that settles each case
  • Feature and retention: a randomized holdout, or a randomized prompt to try the feature, then compare by assignment, not by usage. Usage is chosen by users; assignment is chosen by the coin.
  • Length and judge scores: generate pairs that differ only in length (pad or trim the same content) and score both. That measures the judge's length effect directly. Use length control as a correction only after deciding whether some length is genuine quality: if thoroughness is part of what users want, length is partly a mediator and regressing it out removes real gains (judges).
  • Benchmark gains and overlap: evaluate on a fresh, private set built to the same spec, and compare the gap per model. Better still, decontaminate and retrain at small scale, or plant canary items at controlled rates, to measure the effect of overlap directly (contamination).
Under the hoodWriting each one as a DAG
  • Retention: \(\text{Engagement} \rightarrow \text{Usage}\), \(\text{Engagement} \rightarrow \text{Retention}\), \(\text{Usage} \rightarrow \text{Retention}\), plus \(\text{Tenure} \rightarrow \text{Usage}\) and selection on still-active users. The back door through engagement needs blocking; conditioning on "active this month" is conditioning on a collider of usage and retention.
  • Judge scores: \(\text{Question} \rightarrow \text{Length}\), \(\text{Question} \rightarrow \text{Quality}\), \(\text{Quality} \rightarrow \text{Length}\), \(\text{Length} \rightarrow \text{Score}\), \(\text{Quality} \rightarrow \text{Score}\). Regressing score on length alone mixes all four paths. The padded-pair experiment cuts every arrow into Length, which is what \(do(\text{Length})\) means.
  • Overlap: \(\text{Recipe} \rightarrow \text{Overlap}\), \(\text{Recipe} \rightarrow \text{Capability}\), \(\text{Overlap} \rightarrow \text{Score}\), \(\text{Capability} \rightarrow \text{Score}\). A cross-model correlation cannot separate the two paths into Score; a fresh set removes the \(\text{Overlap} \rightarrow \text{Score}\) path for every model at once.
Eval lens
Offline evaluation is the rare setting where you can intervene cheaply. Before mining logs for what correlates with a good score, ask whether you can set the variable directly: randomize the prompt template per item, pad or trim answers, swap tool budgets, assign items to checkpoints at random. A randomized comparison on 500 items usually beats a confounded analysis of 50,000 (eval statistics, measurement).
Principal asks
What would we expect to see if the cause ran the other way? What decided which rows are in this table? Which variables did you adjust for, and is any of them caused by the treatment? What plausible confounder is not in the data? How many comparisons were run before this one was chosen? What is the cheapest experiment that would settle it, and why have we not run it?
Common mistakes
  • Treating a strong correlation as stronger evidence of causation than a weak one.
  • Adding every available variable as a control, including mediators (which hide real effects) and colliders (which create fake ones).
  • Reading the aggregate when segment mixes differ, or reading segments when the segment is caused by the treatment.
  • Comparing users by what they chose to do (used the feature) instead of what they were assigned (saw the feature).
  • Analysing a filtered population (flagged, escalated, retained, shortlisted) and generalising to everyone.
  • Reporting the one striking slice out of dozens without saying how many were examined.
