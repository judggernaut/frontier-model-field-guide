How networks learn: gradient descent and backpropagation

The previous chapter built a network and set its weights by hand. Real networks start with random weights and learn them. Learning means: measure how wrong the network is, work out which way to nudge every weight to make it less wrong, nudge, repeat. This chapter covers the landscape the network walks on, the gradient that points the way, the step rule, the hills and pits and mountain passes along the route, why randomness helps, and backpropagation, the bookkeeping trick that makes it all affordable. Optimizers like Adam and learning-rate schedules build on this and live in optimisation.

Where does the raindrop end up?

Loss landscapes, minima, maxima and saddle points, told as rain falling on hills.

Imagine every possible setting of a model's weights laid out as a landscape. Each spot is one setting; its height is the loss, a number for how wrong the model is with those weights. Training is a search for low ground. This picture has one weight, so the landscape is a line; a real model has billions.

A raindrop lands on a hillside. It cannot see the landscape; it only feels which way the ground tilts right where it is. That local tilt is the gradient. Water flows the opposite way to uphill, and so does gradient descent.

The drop rolls into the first pond it meets and stops: every step from there goes uphill. That is a local minimum. The deepest lake, the global minimum, is elsewhere and the drop will never know. Hilltops are local maxima; the highest point on the map is the global maximum (here it sits at the edge). At every dip bottom and hilltop the ground is flat, so the gradient is zero.

Where a drop lands decides where it ends. Each pond drains its own patch of ground, called its basin, and the hilltops are the borders. For a network, "where it lands" is the random starting weights. In this one-dimensional picture, bad luck at the start means a worse final answer.

Add a second dimension and a new kind of flat spot appears: the mountain pass, or saddle point. It is the lowest point on the ridge going one way and the highest point on the trail going the other. A drop sitting exactly on it stays put, but the slightest nudge sends it on downhill. With millions of weights, a flat spot that curves up in every single direction is rare. Most flat spots are passes, which slow training down without trapping it.

The loss landscape

Plain wordsA score for wrongness, as ground to walk on

To learn, a network needs a single number that says how badly it is doing. That number is the loss. For a model predicting delivery times it might be the average squared error in minutes. For a language model it is how surprised the model is by the actual next word, averaged over a huge pile of text.

Every possible setting of the weights gives a different loss. Picture all those settings spread out as ground, with the loss as the height at each spot, and you have the loss landscape. Training is walking downhill on it. You never see the landscape as a whole; it has as many directions as the model has weights.

For leadersThe loss is what training optimizes, nothing more

The loss is a proxy chosen because it is smooth and cheap to compute on every example. Next-token cross-entropy for pretraining, a preference loss for reward models, a clipped objective for RL. The model gets better at whatever the loss rewards, which is why the choice of loss and data is a product decision: everything the loss does not measure, the model has no reason to learn.

Eval lens
What a training loss curve does and does not tell you. A smoothly falling training loss says the optimizer is working. It does not say the model is getting better at anything you care about. Four gaps to keep in mind:
  • Train versus held-out. Only loss on held-out data measures generalization; a falling training loss with a flat or rising validation loss is memorization.
  • Averages hide tasks. Loss is an average over billions of tokens, dominated by easy ones. A capability that lives in rare tokens (a correct final answer to a maths problem) can change a lot while the average barely moves. Wei et al. (2022) reported abilities that appear abruptly with scale; Schaeffer et al. (2023) showed much of that abruptness comes from all-or-nothing metrics, while smooth per-token measures improve gradually.
  • Not comparable across tokenizers. Loss per token depends on how text is split into tokens. Compare models with different tokenizers in bits per byte or on downstream evals, not raw loss.
  • Spikes and plateaus are diagnostics. A spike can be a bad batch or an unstable learning rate; a long plateau can be a saddle region. Both are reasons to look, not verdicts.
Under the hoodThe objective

With parameters \(\theta \in \mathbb{R}^P\), training examples \((x_i, y_i)\), and a per-example loss \(\ell\), the training objective is the empirical risk

\[ \mathcal{L}(\theta) = \frac{1}{N}\sum_{i=1}^{N} \ell\big(f_\theta(x_i),\, y_i\big) \]\(f_\theta\) is the network. For regression \(\ell\) is often squared error; for language models it is cross-entropy, \(-\log p_\theta(\text{next token})\), averaged over every token position (see pretraining). The landscape is the graph of \(\mathcal{L}\) over \(\mathbb{R}^P\), with \(P\) in the billions.

What we actually want is low expected loss on new data, \(\mathbb{E}_{(x,y)\sim\mathcal{D}}[\ell(f_\theta(x), y)]\). The empirical risk is an estimate of it; the gap between the two is the generalization gap, and held-out evaluation is how you measure it.

The gradient: which way is downhill

Plain wordsOne slope per knob

With one weight, the slope is a single number: turn the knob up a hair and see whether the loss goes up or down, and how fast. With a billion weights you need a billion slopes, one per knob, answering "if I turn just this knob up slightly, how much does the loss change?" That list of slopes is the gradient. Together the slopes point in the direction where the loss climbs fastest, so you step the opposite way.

For leadersGradients are cheap, which is the whole miracle

Measuring a billion slopes one knob at a time would take a billion extra forward passes per step. Backpropagation, at the end of this chapter, gets all of them for roughly the cost of two forward passes. That single fact is why training networks with billions of parameters is possible at all. It is also why training compute is about 6 floating-point operations per parameter per token: 2 for the forward pass and about 4 for the backward pass (Kaplan et al., 2020).

The size of the gradient (its norm) is one of the most watched training signals. Sudden growth often precedes a loss spike; that is why runs use gradient clipping, which caps the norm. See optimisation.

Under the hoodDefinition and the step rule

The gradient collects the partial derivatives:

\[ \nabla_\theta \mathcal{L} = \Big(\frac{\partial \mathcal{L}}{\partial \theta_1}, \ldots, \frac{\partial \mathcal{L}}{\partial \theta_P}\Big), \qquad \frac{\partial \mathcal{L}}{\partial \theta_j} \approx \frac{\mathcal{L}(\theta + \epsilon \mathbf{e}_j) - \mathcal{L}(\theta - \epsilon \mathbf{e}_j)}{2\epsilon} \]\(\mathbf{e}_j\) is the unit vector for parameter \(j\). The finite-difference form is how you check a gradient implementation, never how you compute gradients in training.

A first-order Taylor expansion shows why stepping against the gradient works:

\[ \mathcal{L}(\theta - \eta\,\nabla\mathcal{L}) \approx \mathcal{L}(\theta) - \eta\,\|\nabla\mathcal{L}\|^2 \]For a small enough step size \(\eta > 0\) the loss goes down by about \(\eta\) times the squared gradient norm. Among all directions of a given length, \(-\nabla\mathcal{L}\) gives the largest first-order decrease.

Hence gradient descent:

\[ \theta_{t+1} = \theta_t - \eta\, \nabla_\theta \mathcal{L}(\theta_t) \]\(\eta\) is the learning rate. Every optimizer used for LLMs is a variation that changes the direction (momentum) or the per-parameter step size (Adam).

Step size: the learning rate

Plain wordsStride length

The gradient says which way is downhill. The learning rate says how big a stride to take. Tiny strides are safe but slow; you may run out of time before reaching the bottom. Huge strides overshoot: you leap across the valley and land higher up the other side, and the next leap is even bigger. Somewhere in between you make fast, steady progress.

For leadersThe most expensive number to get wrong

A learning rate that is too high can waste a run outright; one that is too low wastes compute quietly by finishing at a worse loss. Large runs do not use one fixed value: they warm up, hold, and decay it, and they pick its scale from small-model sweeps. That material, plus Adam, momentum, and batch size, is in optimisation. The widget below shows a different face of the learning rate: on a bumpy landscape, a big step can jump out of a valley entirely.

Under the hoodWhy there is a speed limit

Near a minimum, the loss looks like a bowl \(\tfrac{\lambda}{2}(\theta - \theta^*)^2\) with curvature \(\lambda\). One gradient step gives

\[ \theta_{t+1} - \theta^* = (1 - \eta\lambda)\,(\theta_t - \theta^*) \]The error shrinks only if \(|1 - \eta\lambda| < 1\), that is \(0 < \eta < 2/\lambda\). For \(\eta < 1/\lambda\) it creeps in from one side; between \(1/\lambda\) and \(2/\lambda\) it zig-zags but converges; above \(2/\lambda\) every step overshoots further. In many dimensions the steepest direction sets the limit.

A 1D example with two valleys, \(\mathcal{L}(\theta) = \theta^4 - 3\theta^2 + \theta\): a deep valley near \(\theta = -1.30\) and a shallow one near \(1.13\). Same code, three settings:

def loss(theta):
    return theta**4 - 3 * theta**2 + theta     # two valleys: one deep, one shallow

def grad(theta):
    return 4 * theta**3 - 6 * theta + 1         # the slope, worked out by hand

def descend(theta, lr, steps):
    for step in range(steps):
        theta = theta - lr * grad(theta)        # step against the slope
        if abs(theta) > 1e6:                    # stepped clean off the map
            return None, step + 1
    return theta, steps

for start, lr in [(2.0, 0.01), (-0.5, 0.01), (2.0, 0.2)]:
    end, n = descend(start, lr, 200)
    if end is None:
        print(f"start {start:+.1f}  lr {lr:.2f}  diverged after {n} steps")
    else:
        print(f"start {start:+.1f}  lr {lr:.2f}  theta {end:+.3f}  loss {loss(end):+.3f}")
# start +2.0  lr 0.01  theta +1.131  loss -1.070   (shallow valley: a local minimum)
# start -0.5  lr 0.01  theta -1.301  loss -3.514   (deep valley: the global minimum)
# start +2.0  lr 0.20  diverged after 5 steps
fn loss(theta: f64) -> f64 {
    theta.powi(4) - 3.0 * theta.powi(2) + theta // two valleys: one deep, one shallow
}

fn grad(theta: f64) -> f64 {
    4.0 * theta.powi(3) - 6.0 * theta + 1.0 // the slope, worked out by hand
}

/// Returns (Some(final theta), steps) or (None, step it diverged at).
fn descend(mut theta: f64, lr: f64, steps: usize) -> (Option<f64>, usize) {
    for step in 0..steps {
        theta -= lr * grad(theta); // step against the slope
        if theta.abs() > 1e6 {
            return (None, step + 1); // stepped clean off the map
        }
    }
    (Some(theta), steps)
}

fn main() {
    for (start, lr) in [(2.0, 0.01), (-0.5, 0.01), (2.0, 0.2)] {
        match descend(start, lr, 200) {
            (None, n) => println!("start {:+.1}  lr {:.2}  diverged after {} steps", start, lr, n),
            (Some(end), _) => println!("start {:+.1}  lr {:.2}  theta {:+.3}  loss {:+.3}", start, lr, end, loss(end)),
        }
    }
    // start +2.0  lr 0.01  theta +1.131  loss -1.070   (shallow valley: a local minimum)
    // start -0.5  lr 0.01  theta -1.301  loss -3.514   (deep valley: the global minimum)
    // start +2.0  lr 0.20  diverged after 5 steps
}

Minima, maxima, and saddle points

Plain wordsFive kinds of flat ground

Anywhere the ground is flat, the gradient is zero and gradient descent stops pushing. There are five kinds of flat spot worth naming:

  • Local minimum: the bottom of a dip. Every small step from here goes uphill, but there may be deeper dips elsewhere.
  • Global minimum: the lowest point anywhere. The best possible weights for the training data.
  • Local maximum: the top of a hill. Every small step goes downhill.
  • Global maximum: the highest point anywhere. The worst weights; training never wants to be here.
  • Saddle point: a mountain pass. Downhill in some directions, uphill in others.

The worry in the raindrop story is getting stuck in a poor local minimum. For huge networks that worry turns out to be smaller than the one-dimensional picture suggests, for reasons the next two tabs explain.

For leadersWhy "stuck in a local minimum" is rarely the real problem

Intuition from one or two dimensions says training should constantly get trapped in bad valleys. In very large networks, that is mostly not what happens, for two reasons with good evidence behind them:

  • Most flat spots are saddles. To be a minimum, the ground must curve upward in every one of billions of directions at once. Dauphin et al. (2014) argued, from theory on random high-dimensional functions and experiments on neural networks, that critical points with high loss are overwhelmingly saddle points, and that saddles, with the flat plateaus around them, are the main thing that slows training.
  • The minima you reach are similarly good. Choromanska et al. (2015) gave a model in which local minima of large networks concentrate in a narrow band of loss close to the global minimum, and experiments agreed. Practitioners see the same: rerun with a different seed and the final loss is nearly identical.

Honest nuance: bad local minima do exist. Safran and Shamir (2018) showed they are common in small two-layer ReLU networks, and the theory behind the optimistic picture relies on simplifying assumptions. Being over-parameterized is what makes the landscape benign, which is one more argument for scale. The practical failure modes at frontier scale are plateaus, loss spikes, and divergence, not local minima.

The subtler question is which minimum, among many with equal training loss, generalizes best. Keskar et al. (2017) linked large-batch training to "sharp" minima that generalize worse; Dinh et al. (2017) showed sharpness can be changed by reparameterizing without changing the function, so it is not a clean measure. This is open research.

Principal asks
The loss sat flat for two thousand steps and then dropped: what changed, and could we have shortened the plateau? How much does final loss vary across seeds at small scale, and is our improvement bigger than that? Is the gradient norm stable, and how often does clipping trigger? When you say the model is "stuck", is that training loss, validation loss, or a downstream eval?
Under the hoodClassifying critical points with the Hessian

At a critical point \(\nabla\mathcal{L}(\theta^*) = 0\), the second-order expansion is governed by the Hessian \(H = \nabla^2\mathcal{L}(\theta^*)\):

\[ \mathcal{L}(\theta^* + \boldsymbol{\delta}) \approx \mathcal{L}(\theta^*) + \tfrac12\, \boldsymbol{\delta}^\top H\, \boldsymbol{\delta} = \mathcal{L}(\theta^*) + \tfrac12 \sum_{k} \lambda_k\, (\mathbf{u}_k^\top\boldsymbol{\delta})^2 \]\(\lambda_k, \mathbf{u}_k\) are the eigenvalues and eigenvectors of \(H\): the curvature along each principal direction.
Eigenvalues of \(H\)Critical pointWhat gradient descent does
All \(> 0\)Local minimumSettles there
All \(< 0\)Local maximumMoves away in every direction
Mixed signsSaddle point (index = number of negative \(\lambda_k\))Escapes along negative-curvature directions, slowly if they are shallow
Some \(= 0\)Degenerate: needs higher-order termsFlat valleys, common in over-parameterized nets

A heuristic, not a theorem: if each of \(P\) curvature signs were an independent coin flip, the chance that all are positive would be \(2^{-P}\). The signs are not independent, but the picture captures the scaling. The rigorous version comes from random-field theory (Bray and Dean, 2007): for Gaussian random functions in high dimension, the fraction of negative eigenvalues at a critical point grows with its loss, so low-index critical points sit at low loss. Dauphin et al. (2014) connected this to neural networks.

Gradient descent with a random start almost surely does not converge to a strict saddle (Lee et al., 2016), but it can spend a long time near one (Du et al., 2017, constructed cases needing exponential time). Near a saddle, the escape direction grows like \((1 + \eta|\lambda_-|)^t\) from its initial offset, so a start very close to the ridge produces a long plateau. Stochastic gradient descent: noise that helps

Plain wordsPoll a few, not everyone

Computing the exact gradient means running the network on every training example before taking a single step: like surveying an entire country before every decision. Stochastic gradient descent (SGD) instead looks at a small random handful of examples, a mini-batch, estimates the slope from them, and steps. The estimate is a bit off each time, but it is thousands of times cheaper, so you take thousands of times more steps.

The noise turns out to be useful. A ball that jiggles a little does not sit forever on a mountain pass, and can sometimes hop out of a shallow dip. Try the "Noisy steps" preset in the widget above.

For leadersBatch size is a speed and cost dial

Bigger batches give a cleaner gradient estimate and keep more GPUs busy in parallel, but past a point they stop reducing the number of steps needed, so extra hardware buys nothing. McCandlish et al. (2018) tied that point, the critical batch size, to a measurable gradient noise scale, which grows as training progresses. That is why large runs often increase the batch size over time. The practical settings live in optimisation.

Watch out
"Noise helps escape bad minima" is true in toy pictures and is easy to oversell. In a frontier run, gradient noise mostly matters for stability and for which of many equally good minima you land in. Treat claims that a batch-size or noise change "improved generalization" like any other: ask for seeds, held-out evals, and matched compute.
Under the hoodAn unbiased, noisy estimate

Sample a mini-batch \(\mathcal{B}\) of size \(B\) uniformly from the data:

\[ \mathbf{g}_{\mathcal{B}} = \frac{1}{B}\sum_{i\in\mathcal{B}} \nabla_\theta\, \ell\big(f_\theta(x_i), y_i\big), \qquad \mathbb{E}[\mathbf{g}_{\mathcal{B}}] = \nabla\mathcal{L}(\theta), \qquad \operatorname{Cov}[\mathbf{g}_{\mathcal{B}}] \approx \frac{\Sigma(\theta)}{B} \]\(\Sigma\) is the per-example gradient covariance. The noise shrinks like \(1/B\) in variance, the same \(1/\sqrt n\) story as the standard error in Part I.

The SGD update \(\theta \leftarrow \theta - \eta\,\mathbf{g}_{\mathcal{B}}\) is gradient descent plus zero-mean noise with covariance \(\eta^2\Sigma/B\). Smith and Le (2018) argued that the effective noise scale is set by the ratio \(\eta/B\), which is why learning rate and batch size are usually tuned together. Decaying the learning rate at the end of training shrinks the noise and lets the parameters settle; the widget's noise fades over the run for the same reason.

Blame flows backward

Backpropagation as a gear train, then with the numbers from a tiny network.

Turn gear A by one notch and B turns two; each notch of B turns C three. So C turns 2 × 3 = 6 notches per notch of A. You never need to watch A and C together: multiply the ratio of each link. Calculus calls this the chain rule, and the ratios are derivatives.

A network is a gear train too. A weight affects a weighted sum, which affects an activation, which affects the output, which affects the loss. Each link has a local slope that is easy to compute on its own. Here: one input x = 2, one hidden ReLU unit, one output, target 3. The forward pass gives a prediction of 2.0 and a loss of 0.5.

Now go backward. The loss's slope with respect to itself is 1. Multiply by each link's local slope as you move left: −1 at the output, −1.5 at the hidden unit, still −1.5 through the ReLU, and −3.0 at w₁. Negative means "increase w₁ and the loss goes down". That right-to-left sweep is backpropagation.

Every parameter gets its gradient from the same sweep. The −1 at the output is computed once and reused for w₂, b₂ and the hidden unit; the −1.5 at z is reused for w₁ and b₁. In a real network the reuse is massive, which is why one backward pass costs only about twice a forward pass, however many billions of parameters there are.

Step every parameter against its gradient with learning rate 0.1. The prediction moves from 2.0 to 3.4, and the loss drops from 0.50 to 0.08. It slightly overshoots the target of 3; the next step corrects it. Forward pass, backward pass, step, repeat: that loop, run trillions of times, is how every network in this guide was trained.

Backpropagation: the chain rule, layer by layer

Plain wordsPassing blame back down the line

A restaurant dish comes out too salty. The head chef asks the sauce cook, who asks the person who made the stock. Each person only needs to know how much their part contributed to the part after them; multiply those shares along the line and you know how much each person contributed to the final saltiness. Nobody has to taste every combination.

Backpropagation does that for a network. After a forward pass, it starts at the loss and walks backward through the layers, passing along "how much did you contribute to the error?" Each layer multiplies by its own local share and passes the result back. At the end every weight knows which way to move.

For leadersWhat it costs, and where it breaks

Nobody writes backprop by hand anymore: frameworks like PyTorch and JAX record the forward pass and generate the backward pass automatically (automatic differentiation). What leaders need is its cost profile:

  • Compute: about two forward passes' worth, so a training step costs about three forward passes.
  • Memory: the backward pass needs the intermediate values from the forward pass, so training must store them. For long contexts that storage, not the weights, often dominates memory. Activation checkpointing trades extra compute (recomputing them) for memory; see optimisation.
  • Failure modes: multiplying many slopes in a row can shrink gradients to nothing (vanishing) or blow them up (exploding). Residual connections, normalization layers and careful initialization exist largely to keep that product well behaved across a hundred layers; see architecture.
Note
When a training run produces NaNs, the cause is almost always in this chain: an exploding gradient, a division by a tiny number in a normalization layer, or a low-precision overflow. Good runs log gradient norms per layer so the first bad layer can be found.
Under the hoodThe worked example, then the general rule

The story's network: \(z = w_1 x + b_1\), \(h = \mathrm{ReLU}(z)\), \(y = w_2 h + b_2\), \(\mathcal{L} = \tfrac12 (y - t)^2\), with \(x = 2\), \(t = 3\), \(w_1 = 0.5\), \(b_1 = 0\), \(w_2 = 1.5\), \(b_2 = 0.5\).

const X: f64 = 2.0; // one input
const T: f64 = 3.0; // one target

/// Returns (z, h, y, loss).
fn forward(w1: f64, b1: f64, w2: f64, b2: f64) -> (f64, f64, f64, f64) {
    let z = w1 * X + b1; // hidden pre-activation
    let h = z.max(0.0); // ReLU
    let y = w2 * h + b2; // output
    let loss = 0.5 * (y - T).powi(2); // squared error
    (z, h, y, loss)
}

fn main() {
    let (mut w1, mut b1, mut w2, mut b2) = (0.5, 0.0, 1.5, 0.5);
    let lr = 0.1;

    let (z, h, y, loss) = forward(w1, b1, w2, b2);
    println!("forward: z={:.2} h={:.2} y={:.2} loss={:.3}", z, h, y, loss);

    // Backward: start at the loss and multiply local slopes going left.
    let dy = y - T; // dL/dy
    let (dw2, db2) = (dy * h, dy); // y = w2*h + b2
    let dh = dy * w2; // pass blame back through w2
    let dz = dh * if z > 0.0 { 1.0 } else { 0.0 }; // ReLU passes gradient only if it was on
    let (dw1, db1) = (dz * X, dz); // z = w1*x + b1
    println!("grads: dw1={:.2} db1={:.2} dw2={:.2} db2={:.2}", dw1, db1, dw2, db2);

    // Check one gradient by nudging the weight and re-measuring the loss.
    let eps = 1e-6;
    let num = (forward(w1 + eps, b1, w2, b2).3 - forward(w1 - eps, b1, w2, b2).3) / (2.0 * eps);
    println!("numeric dw1={:.2}", num);

    w1 -= lr * dw1;
    b1 -= lr * db1;
    w2 -= lr * dw2;
    b2 -= lr * db2;
    println!("after one step: loss={:.3}", forward(w1, b1, w2, b2).3);
    // forward: z=1.00 h=1.00 y=2.00 loss=0.500
    // grads: dw1=-3.00 db1=-1.50 dw2=-1.00 db2=-1.00
    // numeric dw1=-3.00
    // after one step: loss=0.080
}

The general rule. For a layer \(\mathbf{h}^{(\ell)} = \phi(\mathbf{z}^{(\ell)})\) with \(\mathbf{z}^{(\ell)} = W^{(\ell)}\mathbf{h}^{(\ell-1)} + \mathbf{b}^{(\ell)}\), write \(\boldsymbol{\delta}^{(\ell)} = \partial\mathcal{L}/\partial\mathbf{z}^{(\ell)}\). Backprop is three lines, applied from the last layer down:

\[ \frac{\partial\mathcal{L}}{\partial W^{(\ell)}} = \boldsymbol{\delta}^{(\ell)}\,\mathbf{h}^{(\ell-1)\top}, \qquad \frac{\partial\mathcal{L}}{\partial \mathbf{b}^{(\ell)}} = \boldsymbol{\delta}^{(\ell)}, \qquad \boldsymbol{\delta}^{(\ell-1)} = \big(W^{(\ell)\top}\boldsymbol{\delta}^{(\ell)}\big) \odot \phi'\big(\mathbf{z}^{(\ell-1)}\big) \]The first two give the gradients for this layer; the third passes blame one layer down. Each needs only values stored during the forward pass. Rumelhart, Hinton and Williams (1986) popularized the method for neural networks; it is reverse-mode automatic differentiation.

The third line also shows where gradients vanish or explode: \(\boldsymbol{\delta}\) is multiplied by \(W^\top\) and by \(\phi'\) at every layer. With sigmoid, \(\phi' \le 0.25\), so twenty layers can shrink it by \(0.25^{20} \approx 10^{-12}\). Residual connections add an identity path, \(\mathbf{h}^{(\ell)} = \mathbf{h}^{(\ell-1)} + F(\mathbf{h}^{(\ell-1)})\), whose derivative includes a plain "+1", so blame can travel the whole depth undiminished.

Key idea
Learning is a loop: forward pass to get the loss, backward pass to get every gradient at once, a small step against the gradient, repeat. Local minima matter less than one-dimensional pictures suggest because, in billions of dimensions, most flat spots are saddles and most minima are about equally good. What actually goes wrong in practice is step size, plateaus, and instability, which is why optimisation is its own chapter.
