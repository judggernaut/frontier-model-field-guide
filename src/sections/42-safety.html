Safety and alignment training

A capable model that does whatever it is asked is not a product anyone can ship. Safety and alignment training shapes what the model will and will not do, how honestly it reports what it knows, and how it behaves under pressure from adversarial users. This chapter covers the core trade-off, the main training methods, how safety is evaluated, and how it interacts with the capability training from earlier chapters. It is descriptive, not operational: no attack recipes.

The trade-off: helpful and harmless pull against each other

Plain wordsA pharmacist, not a vending machine

A good pharmacist answers almost every question, helps people understand their medicines, and refuses a tiny number of requests that would clearly hurt someone. A pharmacist who refused every question that mentioned a dangerous drug would be useless. One who handed out anything would be dangerous. Safety training is teaching a model to be the good pharmacist: helpful by default, careful in the few places that matter, and not fooled by someone dressing up a harmful request as an innocent one.

The two ways to fail are mirror images. Over-refusal: saying no to something harmless ("how do I kill a Python process?"). Unsafe compliance: saying yes to something genuinely harmful. Pushing one down tends to push the other up.

Helpfulness versus harmlessness frontier
A safety signal scores every request. Above the threshold the model refuses. Benign requests that score high are over-refusals; harmful requests that score low are unsafe completions. Move the threshold along the curve, then improve the model and watch the whole frontier move toward the corner.

For leadersTreat it as a classification problem with asymmetric costs

Refuse-or-answer is a binary decision, so everything from classification metrics applies. Over-refusal is a false positive, unsafe compliance a false negative, and harmful requests are rare, so precision on refusals is low even for a good model. Three consequences:

  • The threshold is a policy decision. Where you sit on the curve depends on the relative cost of the two errors, which differs by harm category. Severe, irreversible harms justify many over-refusals; minor ones do not. Mature policies set different operating points per category.
  • Real progress moves the curve, not the point. A release that cuts unsafe compliance by refusing more is a threshold move. A release that cuts both errors is better training. Ask which one you are looking at.
  • Over-refusal is a real cost. It erodes trust, pushes users to less careful tools, and is often concentrated on particular topics or groups. Track it with the same seriousness as unsafe compliance.
Principal asks
  • Did both error rates improve, or did one improve at the other's expense?
  • What are the operating points per harm category, and who signed off on the cost ratios?
  • How are the over-refusal prompts chosen? Are they hard, borderline cases, or easy ones that any model passes?
Under the hoodExpected cost and the operating point

With harmful share \(\pi\), refusal threshold \(t\), over-refusal rate \(\text{FPR}(t)\) on benign traffic, unsafe-compliance rate \(\text{FNR}(t)\) on harmful traffic, and cost ratio \(C\) (one unsafe completion costs \(C\) over-refusals):

\[ \mathbb{E}[\text{cost}](t) \;\propto\; (1-\pi)\,\text{FPR}(t) + \pi\, C\,\text{FNR}(t) \]The cost-minimising threshold is where the frontier's slope equals \(-(1-\pi)/(\pi C)\). Rarer harms or cheaper failures push the optimum toward refusing less.

In practice the "score" is not a single number. It is the model's learned propensity to refuse, plus any classifiers in front of or behind the model. The frame still holds: every component trades the two errors, and the system's frontier is what matters.

How models are trained to be safe

Plain wordsRules, examples, and practice

There are three main ways to teach good judgement. Show the model examples of good responses to tricky requests. Have people rate pairs of responses so it learns which is better. Or write down a set of principles and have an AI use them to critique and rate responses, which scales far better than asking people to read thousands of disturbing conversations.

Under the hoodConstitutional AI in two phases
  1. Supervised phase. Sample responses to red-team prompts from a helpful-only model. Ask the model to critique each response against a randomly drawn principle from the constitution, then revise it. Fine-tune on the revised responses.
  2. RL phase (RLAIF). Sample pairs of responses from the fine-tuned model. Ask a feedback model which response better follows a principle, giving preference labels. Train a preference model on those labels (often mixed with human helpfulness labels) and run RL against it.

The appeal is that the principles are explicit and editable, and the feedback scales with compute instead of human hours. The risk is that the feedback model's blind spots become the policy's blind spots, and that the policy learns to satisfy the letter of the principles. It also explains why constitutions are written as general judgements ("choose the response that is more honest") rather than narrow rules: general principles transfer better to unseen situations.

Failure modes beyond refusal

For leadersWhat safety teams worry about besides "will it help build something dangerous"
  • Jailbreaks. Inputs that get a model to do what its training says it should not. Broad classes include role-play and fictional framing, gradual escalation over many turns, encoding or translating the request, splitting a harmful task into innocent-looking pieces, flooding the context with fabricated examples of compliance, and instructions hidden in documents or tool outputs (prompt injection). Defences are layered: training on these patterns, classifiers on inputs and outputs, and limits on what tools an agent can use.
  • Sycophancy. Telling users what they want to hear: agreeing with stated opinions, caving when pushed back on a correct answer, praising weak work. Preference training rewards it, because people tend to rate agreement highly (Sharma et al., 2023).
  • Dishonesty and poor calibration. Stating guesses as facts, inventing citations, or claiming to have done work it did not do. For agents, misreporting what actions were taken.
  • Reward hacking that looks like misbehaviour. RL on capability tasks can teach a model to cut corners (see GRPO), and corner-cutting can generalise into broader patterns such as deceiving the grader.
  • Over-refusal and moralising. Unrequested lectures, hedging that buries the answer, and refusing legitimate professional questions in medicine, security, or law.
Watch out
Capability RL can quietly undo safety training. A long reasoning-RL run optimised only for correctness can raise compliance with harmful requests, increase sycophancy, or teach the model to game evaluations. Re-run the safety suite after every major capability stage, not only at the end, and include safety data or rewards in the RL mixture when possible.

How safety is evaluated

For leadersFour families of evaluation

Refusal and harm benchmarks

Sets of harmful requests scored for whether the model complies, for example HarmBench (Mazeika et al., 2024) and StrongREJECT (Souly et al., 2024), which also grades how useful a non-refusal actually was.

Over-refusal benchmarks

Benign prompts that look risky ("how do I kill a process"), for example XSTest (Röttger et al., 2024) and OR-Bench (Cui et al., 2024). Always report alongside harm benchmarks.

Red teaming

Humans and automated attackers search for failures. Automated red teaming uses another model to generate attacks at scale (Perez et al., 2022). Results are a lower bound on vulnerability, never a proof of safety.

Dangerous-capability evals

Tests of whether the model could meaningfully help with severe harms, such as advanced cyber operations or weapons development, or could act autonomously in risky ways. These feed frontier safety policies.

Frontier safety policies. Several labs publish frameworks that tie capability thresholds to required safeguards: Anthropic's Responsible Scaling Policy with its AI Safety Levels (first published 2023), OpenAI's Preparedness Framework (2023), and Google DeepMind's Frontier Safety Framework (2024). The common structure: define capability thresholds that would matter, evaluate for them before and during deployment, and commit to stronger security and deployment safeguards, or to pausing, when a threshold is crossed. Dangerous-capability evaluation therefore carries direct launch consequences, and its statistical quality matters accordingly.

Eval lens
Safety evals have every problem from Part II, often worse. Harmful prompts are rare and diverse, so sets are small and intervals are wide. Grading is usually done by an LLM judge, which must be validated against human labels (see LLM judges). Public benchmarks get trained on, so keep private held-out sets (see contamination). And a capability eval that a model fails because of refusals or poor prompting understates what it can do. Dangerous-capability evals should try hard to elicit the capability, which is the opposite of a normal product eval.
Under the hoodMeasuring robustness, not a single attack

A model's jailbreak resistance is better described by a curve than a number: attack success rate as a function of attacker effort (queries, attempts, or compute). Report attack success rate with an interval at several budgets, and for adaptive attacks report the budget needed to reach a given success rate. Because successes are rare at low budgets, use exact or Wilson intervals (see eval statistics), and treat zero observed successes as "fewer than about \(3/n\) at 95% confidence", the rule of three, not as zero risk.

\[ \text{If } 0 \text{ of } n \text{ attacks succeed:}\quad p_{\text{upper}} \approx \frac{3}{n} \]An approximate 95% upper bound. With 300 failed attacks you can only claim the success rate is probably below 1%.

Deployment: training is one layer of several

For leadersDefence in depth

No lab relies on model training alone. Deployed systems add input and output classifiers (for example Anthropic's constitutional classifiers, described in 2025), usage policies and account-level enforcement, monitoring for abuse patterns across many conversations, rate limits, and for agents, restrictions on tools and permissions. Each layer has its own false-positive and false-negative rates, and stacking classifiers compounds over-refusal as well as protection. Evaluate the full system on realistic traffic, not each layer alone.

Principal asks
If the model's refusal training failed completely on one category tomorrow, which other layer would catch it, and how would we know within a day?
