Measuring the right thing: data types and validity

A perfectly randomized experiment still fails if the number at the end is the wrong kind of number, analysed with the wrong formula, or only loosely connected to what you care about. This chapter covers the four levels of measurement and what each one allows, the long argument about averaging star ratings, the special data types that each need their own formula (binary, counts, censored durations, ratios, heavy tails, judge scores), the difference between a measurement that is consistent and one that is correct, how measurement error quietly shrinks effects, and the gap between a proxy and the target it stands in for.

Four numbers from one marathon

Nominal, ordinal, interval, ratio: what each kind of number lets you do.

Nominal. Bib numbers and club names are labels. Numbers on a bib could be swapped for colours without losing anything. You can count them and find the most common one; you cannot average them.

Ordinal. Finishing places have a real order, but the gaps between them mean nothing: second place was two seconds behind the winner, third was almost four and a half minutes behind. Medians and ranks are fine. "Average place" quietly treats every gap as equal.

Interval. Temperature in Celsius has equal steps, so "10 degrees warmer" means the same everywhere on the scale and averages make sense. Zero is just the freezing point of water, so ratios do not: in Fahrenheit the same two days are 50 and 68, nowhere near double.

Ratio. Finishing time has a true zero, so "twice as long" holds in any unit. Ratios, percentage changes, geometric means, and log scales all make sense. Latency, tokens, and cost per task are ratio data.

The four levels form a ladder. Each level allows everything the levels below allow, plus more. The level of your metric decides which summaries are honest and which tests answer the question you meant to ask.

Levels of measurement

Plain wordsNot every number can be averaged

Some numbers are names (a jersey number, a postcode). Some numbers are positions in a line (first, second, third; one to five stars). Some numbers measure in equal steps but start from an arbitrary zero (temperature in Celsius, a chess rating). And some measure amounts with a real zero (time, money, weight).

The kind of number decides what you can sensibly do with it. Averaging postcodes is nonsense. Averaging star ratings is debatable, because the step from 4 to 5 stars may not equal the step from 1 to 2. Averaging response times is fine, though often not the best summary, because a few slow ones drag the average.

For leadersWhy this shows up in reviews

Most eval dashboards mix all four levels and summarise them all with an average. That is where wrong calls come from: a model whose "average helpfulness" rose from 3.8 to 3.9 may have gained many 4s and also gained more 1s, which is a safety regression hiding inside an improvement. A leaderboard rank that moved from 5th to 3rd may reflect a 0.2-point gap or a 6-point gap.

Ask three questions of every headline metric: what kind of number is it, does the summary respect that, and does the comparison test what the decision needs (an average, a typical case, or the share of bad outcomes)? The answer often changes the metric from a mean to a share ("percent rated 4 or 5", "percent rated 1") that is both more honest and easier to act on.

Under the hoodPermissible transformations and what each level supports

Stevens (Science, 1946) defined the levels by the transformations that leave a scale's information unchanged. A statistic is meaningful on a scale if its conclusions survive every permissible transformation.

A quick test: apply a monotone transformation that preserves order (for example, recode 1–5 as 1, 2, 3, 4, 10) and see whether your conclusion survives. Medians and rank tests survive by construction. Means and t-tests can reverse, which is the formal content of "ordinal data has no meaningful mean". The reading a shape checklist covers how to recognise the level from the data itself; from shape to method maps shapes to summaries.

The Likert debate: can you average star ratings?

Plain wordsTwo restaurants, both 3.0 stars

Restaurant A gets three stars from everyone: fine, forgettable. Restaurant B gets five stars from half its diners and one star from the other half: some love it, some got food poisoning. Both average 3.0 stars. The average says they are the same. Anyone who has to pick a restaurant for their in-laws knows they are not.

The average can be fine for ratings. It throws away the shape, though, and the shape is often the story.

For leadersA practical policy

The argument has run for decades. One camp holds that ordinal data must never be averaged (Jamieson, Medical Education, 2004, is a widely cited statement). Another holds that t-tests and means are robust enough that averaging ratings rarely leads you astray in practice (Norman, Advances in Health Sciences Education, 2010). A third line of work shows concrete failure cases: Liddell and Kruschke (Journal of Experimental Social Psychology, 2018) demonstrate that treating ordinal ratings as metric can produce false alarms, missed effects, and even effects in the wrong direction, especially when groups differ in spread or pile up at the top or bottom of the scale.

A policy that holds up in review: report the full distribution and the share at the top and bottom of the scale as the primary numbers. Averages are acceptable as a secondary summary when the score is a sum or mean of many items (a multi-item scale behaves much more like interval data), the distributions are similar in shape, and nothing piles up at a floor or ceiling. For a single 1–5 rating where a tail matters (safety, harmful answers), never let the mean be the only number.

Under the hoodWhat each test actually tests

The t-test compares means and assumes the gaps between categories are equal. The Mann–Whitney U test compares whole distributions by ranks: it estimates \(P(B > A) + \tfrac12 P(B = A)\) for a random draw from each group, the probability of superiority, which equals the AUC of separating the groups. It is invariant to any order-preserving recoding. It is a test of medians only under the extra assumption that the two distributions have the same shape.

\[ U_B = \sum_{i \in B} R_i - \frac{n_B(n_B+1)}{2}, \qquad \hat\theta = \frac{U_B}{n_A n_B}, \qquad \sigma_U^2 = \frac{n_A n_B}{12}\Big[(N+1) - \frac{\sum_k (t_k^3 - t_k)}{N(N-1)}\Big] \]\(R_i\) are mid-ranks in the pooled sample of size \(N = n_A + n_B\); \(t_k\) is the number of tied values at category \(k\). With five categories the tie correction matters a lot. \(z = (U_B - n_A n_B/2)/\sigma_U\).

The model-based alternative is ordinal (cumulative logit or probit) regression: assume a latent continuous score cut into categories by thresholds, and estimate the shift in the latent score. It handles covariates, clustering (with random effects), and unequal spread, and it gives an effect on an interpretable scale. For pairwise preferences (A versus B on the same prompt), use a sign test or Bradley–Terry instead (see LLM as judge).

import math

def mann_whitney(a, b):
    """U for group b, tie-corrected z, two-sided normal p, and P(b beats a)."""
    pooled = sorted(a + b)
    rank = {}                       # mid-rank for each distinct value
    i = 0
    while i < len(pooled):
        j = i
        while j < len(pooled) and pooled[j] == pooled[i]:
            j += 1
        rank[pooled[i]] = (i + 1 + j) / 2     # average of positions i+1 .. j
        i = j
    na, nb, n = len(a), len(b), len(a) + len(b)
    u = sum(rank[v] for v in b) - nb * (nb + 1) / 2
    ties = sum(pooled.count(v) ** 3 - pooled.count(v) for v in set(pooled))
    var = na * nb / 12 * ((n + 1) - ties / (n * (n - 1)))   # ties shrink the variance
    z = (u - na * nb / 2) / math.sqrt(var)
    p = math.erfc(abs(z) / math.sqrt(2))
    return u, z, p, u / (na * nb)

// erfc via the Numerical Recipes rational approximation (relative error below 1.2e-7).
fn erfc(x: f64) -> f64 {
    let z = x.abs();
    let t = 1.0 / (1.0 + 0.5 * z);
    let r = t * (-z * z - 1.26551223 + t * (1.00002368 + t * (0.37409196 + t * (0.09678418
        + t * (-0.18628806 + t * (0.27886807 + t * (-1.13520398 + t * (1.48851587
        + t * (-0.82215223 + t * 0.17087277))))))))).exp();
    if x >= 0.0 { r } else { 2.0 - r }
}

/// U for group b, tie-corrected z, two-sided normal p, and P(b beats a).
fn mann_whitney(a: &[i32], b: &[i32]) -> (f64, f64, f64, f64) {
    let mut pooled: Vec<i32> = a.iter().chain(b.iter()).cloned().collect();
    pooled.sort();
    let mut rank = std::collections::HashMap::new(); // mid-rank for each distinct value
    let mut ties = 0.0;
    let mut i = 0;
    while i < pooled.len() {
        let mut j = i;
        while j < pooled.len() && pooled[j] == pooled[i] {
            j += 1;
        }
        rank.insert(pooled[i], (i + 1 + j) as f64 / 2.0); // average of positions i+1 .. j
        let t = (j - i) as f64;
        ties += t * t * t - t;
        i = j;
    }
    let (na, nb) = (a.len() as f64, b.len() as f64);
    let n = na + nb;
    let u = b.iter().map(|v| rank[v]).sum::<f64>() - nb * (nb + 1.0) / 2.0;
    let var = na * nb / 12.0 * ((n + 1.0) - ties / (n * (n - 1.0))); // ties shrink the variance
    let z = (u - na * nb / 2.0) / var.sqrt();
    let p = erfc(z.abs() / 2f64.sqrt());
    (u, z, p, u / (na * nb))
}

fn main() {
    let a = [3, 4, 2, 5, 3, 4, 3, 2, 4, 3]; // model A, 1-5 ratings (illustrative)
    let b = [4, 5, 4, 3, 5, 4, 5, 4, 3, 5]; // model B
    let (u, z, p, theta) = mann_whitney(&a, &b);
    println!("{:.1} {:.3} {:.4} {:.2}", u, z, p, theta); // 76.0 2.054 0.0400 0.76
}

Read \(\hat\theta = 0.76\) as: a random rating of model B beats a random rating of model A about three times in four, counting ties as half. With ten ratings each this is a borderline result, and the ratings would need to come from different prompts and raters for the independence assumption to hold.

Eval lens
Judge scores and human ratings in LLM evals are almost always ordinal, and the decision almost always cares about a tail: how often is the answer harmful, wrong, or useless? Report the distribution, the share at the bottom of the scale, and a rank-based comparison (Mann–Whitney for independent ratings, Wilcoxon signed-rank or a sign test when both models answered the same prompts). If a mean is on the slide, put the share of 1s next to it. A model can raise the average and the share of disasters at the same time.

Special data types and their right formulas

Plain wordsEach kind of data has its own ruler

A yes/no result, a count of things, a waiting time where some people are still waiting, a ratio of two totals, a cost where one customer spends a thousand times more than the rest, a grade from a judge: each looks like "a number per item", and each breaks the simple average-and-error-bar formula in its own way. Using the matching formula is the difference between an error bar you can trust and one that is off by a factor of three.

Under the hoodThe formulas

Binary. A proportion \(\hat p = x/n\) has variance \(p(1-p)/n\). Use the Wilson interval rather than the plus-or-minus normal interval, which misbehaves near 0 and 1 and at small \(n\); details and the paired McNemar test are in statistics for eval sets.

Counts. Poisson assumes variance equals mean. Real usage counts are usually overdispersed (variance far above mean), which the negative binomial models with \(\operatorname{Var}(Y) = \mu + \mu^2/k\). Compare groups with a negative binomial regression and report the rate ratio \(e^{\beta}\). See the distribution zoo.

Durations with censoring. If a run hit a time limit at 30 minutes, you know only that its true duration exceeds 30. Dropping it biases the average down; recording it as 30 also biases it down. The Kaplan–Meier estimator (Kaplan and Meier, JASA, 1958) uses each unit for as long as it was observed:

\[ \hat S(t) = \prod_{t_i \le t} \Big(1 - \frac{d_i}{n_i}\Big) \]\(t_i\) are the distinct event times, \(d_i\) the number of events at \(t_i\), and \(n_i\) the number still at risk (not yet failed and not yet censored) just before \(t_i\). Compare two curves with the log-rank test; model covariates with Cox regression. The key assumption is that censoring is unrelated to the outcome, given covariates.

Ratio metrics. For a metric like clicks per page view, randomized by user, the numerator \(X_u\) and denominator \(Y_u\) are per-user sums. The delta method gives the variance of the ratio of means \(\bar X/\bar Y\) over \(n\) users:

\[ \operatorname{Var}\!\Big(\frac{\bar X}{\bar Y}\Big) \approx \frac{1}{n\,\mu_Y^2}\Big(\sigma_X^2 - 2\frac{\mu_X}{\mu_Y}\sigma_{XY} + \frac{\mu_X^2}{\mu_Y^2}\sigma_Y^2\Big) \]\(\mu, \sigma^2\) are per-user means and variances, \(\sigma_{XY}\) the per-user covariance. Deng, Knoblich and Lu (KDD, 2018) give a practical guide for online experiments. A user-level bootstrap gives the same answer more slowly; treating page views as independent gives error bars that are too narrow.

Heavy tails. When a few values dominate the sum, the mean converges slowly and its interval is unreliable. Report percentiles, compare on a log scale (a ratio of geometric means), or trim or winsorize with the rule fixed in advance. See long tails and outliers.

Judge scores. Treat them as ordinal, check their reliability (below), and prefer paired designs: both models answer the same prompts and the judge scores both, ideally in a randomized order. Calibration, position bias, and agreement with humans are covered in LLM as judge.

Light bulbs and a test that ended early

Censoring, and how Kaplan–Meier uses every bulb for as long as it was watched.

A lab tests eight bulbs. The test ends at 1,000 hours, so bulbs 7 and 8 are still burning. Bulb 2's fixture broke at 400 hours and it was removed. We know when five bulbs burned out; for the other three we only know they lasted at least that long.

The two obvious shortcuts both understate how long bulbs last. Ignoring unfinished bulbs throws away the strongest ones. Counting them as failures at the cut-off pretends the test's end was their end.

Kaplan–Meier walks forward in time. At each burnout it asks: of the bulbs still being watched right now, what share failed? It multiplies those survival fractions together. Bulb 2 counts for its first 400 hours and then quietly leaves the "at risk" group, so it is neither thrown away nor counted as a failure.

The median lifetime is where the curve drops through 50 percent: about 780 hours, well above both shortcuts. If more bulbs had outlasted the test and the curve stayed above 50 percent, the honest answer would be "median above 1,000 hours", which is still more informative than a wrong number.

The same pattern appears whenever a clock stops before the story does: agent runs that hit a step cap, requests that time out, users who have not churned yet. Report how many were cut off, plot survival curves, and compare them with a log-rank test instead of comparing averages of the finished ones.

Under the hoodKaplan–Meier in a few lines

The light-bulb data from the story. Each record is a time and whether the bulb burned out (1) or was censored (0).

def kaplan_meier(times, events):
    """Survival curve as (time, S(t)) at each event time. Assumes distinct times or
    processes ties one record at a time, which gives the same product."""
    data = sorted(zip(times, events))
    at_risk, s, curve = len(data), 1.0, []
    for t, burned_out in data:
        if burned_out:
            s *= (at_risk - 1) / at_risk      # share of the at-risk group that survives this moment
            curve.append((t, s))
        at_risk -= 1                          # failed or censored, it leaves the risk set either way
    return curve

/// Survival curve as (time, S(t)) at each event time. Assumes distinct times or
/// processes ties one record at a time, which gives the same product.
fn kaplan_meier(times: &[u32], events: &[u8]) -> Vec<(u32, f64)> {
    let mut data: Vec<(u32, u8)> = times.iter().cloned().zip(events.iter().cloned()).collect();
    data.sort();
    let (mut at_risk, mut s, mut curve) = (data.len() as f64, 1.0, Vec::new());
    for (t, burned_out) in data {
        if burned_out == 1 {
            s *= (at_risk - 1.0) / at_risk; // share of the at-risk group that survives this moment
            curve.push((t, s));
        }
        at_risk -= 1.0; // failed or censored, it leaves the risk set either way
    }
    curve
}

Reliability versus validity

The bathroom scale and the dartboard

Consistent is not the same as correct.

A bathroom scale that reads 2 kg heavy gives nearly the same number every time. It is reliable: consistent from one reading to the next. It is not valid: it does not measure your true weight. Repeating it a hundred times only makes you more confident in the wrong number.

A cheap scale that jumps around by 3 kg is right on average but useless for any single reading. You need many readings to learn anything, and small real changes, like losing half a kilo, disappear in the noise.

Put together, there are four combinations. Only the top left is what you want. Reliability is necessary for validity, because a measure that disagrees with itself cannot agree with the truth, but it is not enough: the top right is perfectly consistent and wrong.

For an LLM judge, reliability means it gives the same verdict when asked again and agrees with other careful raters. Validity means its verdicts track what experts or users actually value. A judge that loves long answers is very consistent, and consistently wrong in a direction a model can learn to exploit.

Plain wordsConsistent and correct are two different checks

A measurement can fail in two ways. It can be shaky: ask twice, get two different answers. Or it can be steady but aimed at the wrong thing: a reading test that mostly measures how fast you read rather than how well you understand. The first problem is about reliability, the second about validity. You need to check both, and they need different tests.

For leadersWhat to ask of any new metric

Before a metric becomes a launch gate, it should pass both checks, with numbers.

  • Reliability. Run the same eval twice (same model, new seed or new judge sample): how much does the score move? Two raters on the same items: what is their agreement? Low reliability means you need far more items to see a real change, which is a direct cost.
  • Content validity. Does the eval cover the skill as the product uses it, or a convenient slice? A coding benchmark of single-function puzzles has weak content validity for repository-scale agent work.
  • Criterion validity. Does the metric predict something you trust: expert judgment today (concurrent), user retention or task success later (predictive)? This is the offline-to-online question.
  • Construct validity. Does it measure the concept in its name? A "reasoning" benchmark that a model passes by memorization measures recall. Check that it correlates with related measures and not with unrelated ones such as output length.
Principal asks
What is the test–retest correlation of this eval score across seeds? What is judge–human agreement, and how does it compare with human–human agreement on the same items? What external outcome has this metric been shown to predict, and how strongly? What would a model do to raise this number without getting better, and have we tried it?
Under the hoodKappa, ICC, and the validity types

Inter-rater agreement for categories is measured with Cohen's kappa (Cohen, 1960), which corrects raw agreement for the agreement expected by chance from each rater's base rates:

\[ \kappa = \frac{p_o - p_e}{1 - p_e}, \qquad p_e = \sum_k p_{1k}\,p_{2k} \]\(p_o\) is observed agreement, \(p_{jk}\) the share of items rater \(j\) put in category \(k\). Worked example: two raters label 100 answers; they agree on 40 "good" and 30 "bad". Rater 1 says "good" 60 times, rater 2 says it 50 times, so \(p_e = 0.6\times0.5 + 0.4\times0.5 = 0.50\), \(p_o = 0.70\), and \(\kappa = 0.40\). Seventy percent agreement sounds fine; kappa says it is only 40 percent of the way from chance to perfect.

Use weighted kappa for ordinal scales (a 4 against a 5 is a smaller disagreement than a 1 against a 5), Fleiss' kappa or Krippendorff's alpha for more than two raters or missing ratings, and the intraclass correlation (ICC) for continuous scores and test–retest designs (see statistics for eval sets). Landis and Koch (Biometrics, 1977) proposed verbal labels (0.41–0.60 "moderate", 0.61–0.80 "substantial"), which are widely quoted and were offered as arbitrary conventions; compare against the human–human ceiling on your own task instead.

Kappa has a known quirk: with very unbalanced categories (say 95 percent "safe"), kappa can be low even when raw agreement is very high, because chance agreement is already high. Report raw agreement, kappa, and the confusion table together. For the classic validity taxonomy (content, criterion, construct), the reference point in psychology is Cronbach and Meehl (1955) on construct validity; modern practice treats all three as evidence for one question: does this score support the decision we are using it for?

Measurement error: attenuation and inflated variance

Plain wordsNoise hides effects

Try to hear whether a friend's voice got louder in a quiet room, then try in a noisy bar. The change is the same; the bar makes it harder to detect. A noisy measurement does the same to an experiment: the effect is still there, but you need many more people to see it.

Noise does something sneakier when it sits in the thing you use to explain the outcome. If you measure study hours badly, the link between study hours and grades looks weaker than it is. The noise drags the relationship toward zero.

For leadersNoise is a budget line

Every bit of measurement noise is paid for in sample size. If an eval score's reliability is 0.6 (60 percent of its variance is real differences between items, 40 percent is noise from the judge or the seed), you need roughly 1/0.6, about 1.7 times as many items to detect the same effect as with a perfectly reliable measure. Improving the judge, averaging several judge samples, or using a paired design is often cheaper than collecting more data.

The more dangerous error is error that correlates with the treatment. If the new model writes longer answers and the judge rewards length, the measurement error is no longer noise: it is bias, and more data makes you more confident in it.

Under the hoodThe classical error model

Write the observed score as \(X^* = X + u\), with \(u\) independent noise. Define reliability \(\lambda = \sigma_X^2/(\sigma_X^2 + \sigma_u^2)\).

  • Error in the outcome. The treatment effect on \(Y^*\) is unbiased, but its variance is inflated by \(1/\lambda\). The standardised effect size shrinks by \(\sqrt{\lambda}\), so the sample size for fixed power scales by \(1/\lambda\).
  • Error in a predictor. Regressing \(Y\) on \(X^*\) gives a slope biased toward zero: \(\operatorname{plim}\hat\beta = \lambda\beta\). This is regression dilution or attenuation bias.
  • Correlation between two noisy measures. Spearman's (1904) attenuation formula: \(r_{X^*Y^*} = r_{XY}\sqrt{\lambda_X\lambda_Y}\).
\[ r_{\text{observed}} = r_{\text{true}}\sqrt{\lambda_X \lambda_Y} \]Illustrative: if a judge's true correlation with user satisfaction is 0.6, the judge's reliability is 0.7 and the satisfaction survey's is 0.8, you will observe about \(0.6\times\sqrt{0.56} \approx 0.45\). A low judge–human correlation may be partly the humans' noise.

Non-classical error breaks all of these. Error that correlates with the true value (scores compressed near the top of a scale), with the treatment (a judge with length bias evaluating a model that got longer), or with the outcome (users more likely to rate when angry) can bias effects in either direction. Probe for it with counterfactual items, such as the same answer padded and unpadded, before trusting a judge on a treatment that changes style.

Proxies versus targets

Plain wordsSteps are not fitness

A step counter measures steps, and you want fitness. Steps are a proxy: easy to count and usually related to the target. Shake the phone while watching television and the steps go up while fitness does not. Most things we measure in AI are proxies like this: thumbs-up instead of satisfaction, benchmark score instead of real usefulness, a judge's grade instead of an expert's.

For leadersUsing proxies without being used by them

Proxies are unavoidable because the target is slow, expensive, or rare. The risk is that optimizing the proxy finds the places where it and the target come apart (Goodhart's law; the reward-model version is in preferences and reward models).

  1. Validate on the variation you will create. A proxy that correlates with the target across users may not move with it under your treatment. The proxy has to track the target across past launches, not only across people.
  2. Keep a slow, expensive check alive. A sample of expert reviews, a holdout of users, a long-term retention read. Use it to recalibrate the proxy on a schedule.
  3. Pair every proxy with a guardrail that would move if the proxy were being gamed: length with helpfulness, thumbs-up with task completion, refusal rate with harmful-compliance rate.
Under the hoodSurrogate criteria

Medicine calls proxies surrogate endpoints. Prentice (Statistics in Medicine, 1989) proposed a strict criterion: the surrogate must capture the treatment's full effect on the true endpoint, so that the true endpoint is independent of treatment given the surrogate. That is rarely met. Weaker practical standards ask that, across many past trials, the treatment effect on the surrogate predicts the treatment effect on the target (trial-level validation). The cautionary result is that a treatment can improve the surrogate, the surrogate can be positively correlated with the target, and the treatment can still harm the target, because the treatment moves the surrogate through a path that does not lead to the target. The same logic applies to an eval: validate it on historical model pairs, where you know which model users preferred, not only on item-level correlations.

Common mistakes
  • Averaging ordinal ratings as the only summary. Report the distribution and the bottom-box share; a mean can rise while disasters also rise.
  • Reading Mann–Whitney as a test of medians. It tests whether one group tends to rank higher; it is a median test only if the shapes match.
  • Dropping or capping censored durations. Both bias the average down. Use Kaplan–Meier and report the censoring rate.
  • Computing a ratio metric's variance per event when randomization was per user. Use the delta method or a user-level bootstrap.
  • Poisson error bars on overdispersed counts. Check variance against mean first.
  • Calling a judge validated because it is consistent. Reliability is necessary, not sufficient. Check agreement with humans and probe its biases.
  • Optimizing a proxy that was validated only across users, never across launches.
Principal asks
  • What level of measurement is this metric, and does the summary on the slide respect it?
  • For this rating, what happened to the share at the bottom of the scale?
  • How many runs were censored by a step or time limit, and how were they handled?
  • Is the variance computed at the randomization unit, and for ratio metrics, by the delta method or bootstrap?
  • What is the eval's test–retest reliability, and how many items does that imply we need?
  • What evidence ties this proxy to the outcome we care about, across past launches?
