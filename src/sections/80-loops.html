What frontier-lab interview loops actually test

The rest of this guide teaches the material. This chapter maps it onto the interview loops at Anthropic, OpenAI, Meta and Netflix for applied research, research engineering, software engineering and forward-deployed roles, as candidates reported them in 2025–2026. Every pattern below comes from a named public report; treat it as a sample of self-selected posts, not a rubric (the survivor problem from Who is in your sample? applies in full).

Plain wordsFour kinds of question, in every loop

Almost every loop asks you four things in different costumes. Can you build a small working thing quickly and cleanly? (the coding rounds). Can you design a big system and justify it with arithmetic? (system design). Can you explain something you did, including what went wrong? (the project deep dive). Do your values and judgment hold up when someone pushes? (the culture or values round).

The surprise for most candidates is the weighting. At Anthropic the culture round is the one people most often report failing, and the technical rounds look less like puzzle-solving and more like a day of real work: extend a messy codebase, make a crawler concurrent, move a 500 GB model to a thousand machines.

For leadersWhere to spend preparation time
  • Practical, progressive coding beats LeetCode volume. Anthropic's screens are multi-level CodeSignal tasks (a bank, an in-memory database, a vLLM-like server in an unfamiliar codebase) or a single problem that grows: single-threaded, then concurrent, then fault-tolerant. OpenAI's are work queues with retries and a dead-letter queue, versioned data structures, and debugging an existing scheduler. Chapter Coding rounds works the recurring ones.
  • System design is infrastructure with numbers. The reported prompts are model-checkpoint distribution, GPU scheduling for mixed model sizes, inference serving with batching and cache hit rates, and sandboxed execution. A failed candidate's lesson: "the math actually drives design decisions." Chapters the method through reported prompts cover this.
  • The project deep dive is a presentation with cross-examination. Expect "was it worth $20M?", "what would you do differently?", and pushback on whether the evidence supports the conclusion. Part II and Part VI give you the vocabulary to defend an evaluation.
  • The values round is not a STAR exercise. Prepare real opinions, including where you disagree with the company, and real examples with ethical weight.

Watch out
Many of these posts come from interview-prep vendors summarising candidate submissions. They are useful for question shapes and recurring themes; they are weak evidence about pass rates or rubrics. Anthropic's own guidance prohibits AI use in live interviews and take-homes unless the instructions say otherwise, and some loops now include an explicit "code with Claude" round instead.

Recurring question shapes

Progressive build

Bank or in-memory database in four levels; a vLLM-like server extended in five parts. Each level adds rules; earlier tests must keep passing. Signal: modular code that bends without breaking.

Make it concurrent

Web crawler, image pipeline, Monte Carlo simulation. Signal: identify shared state, protect it, terminate correctly, choose threads versus processes for I/O versus CPU work.

Make it survive

An LRU that recovers after a crash; a work queue with timeouts and a DLQ. Signal: state machines, write-ahead logs, idempotency.

Read before you write

Debug a scheduler, review a PR whose tests pass, fix a NumPy model. Signal: you find production risks the tests miss.

The non-coding rounds

Recruiter screen (Anthropic): what gets asked

Reported questions: What does AI safety mean to you? What could happen if advanced AI is misused (they wanted both accidents and deliberate misuse: misinformation, fraud, privacy, insecure code, harmful automation, premature deployment)? Have you faced a situation that conflicted with your values? Have you followed Anthropic's recent decisions, and is there anything you disagreed with? "What's on your desk Monday morning?" For managers moving to IC: are you comfortable going back to an IC role? The recruiter can end the process here.

Culture or values round: what gets asked

What do you think Anthropic is getting wrong? What kind of work do you dislike most, and how would you convince someone else to do it? Have you ever done something morally wrong? When have you disagreed with your company's direction? What happens when speed and safety conflict? What would you do if a major breakthrough looked possible but carried unknown risks? Follow-ups go to how you felt then and now. Reports agree that rehearsed, emotionally flat, framework-shaped answers land badly, and that thoughtful disagreement lands better than agreement. Read Core Views on AI Safety, Machines of Loving Grace and The Adolescence of Technology, then form your own view.

Project deep dive: what gets pushed on

Motivation, your personal contribution, technical decisions, trade-offs, surprises, metrics, ROI ("50 engineer-years at $400K: was it worth $20M?"), and what you would do differently. Candidates recommend building the deck a day ahead, sticking to the time cap, and rehearsing for someone outside the domain. For research roles, expect "does the experiment support the claim?": the confounding, power and multiple-comparison chapters are your defence.

Forward Deployed Engineer: the customer case

Example prompts: a bank wants to automate analyst workflows, what do you build first? A customer says their Claude pilot "doesn't work", how do you diagnose it? Executives want an agent that acts in production systems and security is sceptical. Strong answers start with discovery (workflow, users, data, failure modes, regulation, success metric, what can be piloted safely), then design with evals, golden datasets, human review, audit logs, RBAC and a staged rollout. Part II (judges, offline–online gap, harness) and Part VI (A/B tests, metrics) are the evaluation half of that answer.
