Who is in your sample? Coverage, self-selection and survivors

The sampling distribution and every error bar in this guide assume one thing: that the people, prompts or sessions you measured were drawn fairly from the ones you care about. When that fails, the error bar is still computed, still shrinks with more data, and is still wrong. This chapter is about the gap between who you meant to measure and who actually ended up in your data: how it opens, why a bigger sample does not close it, the probability designs that prevent it, and the weighting that partly repairs it.

The email poll

A town asks residents about a new transit plan by email. Each stage loses people, and not at random. Illustrative numbers.

The population: everyone in town, in four age bands. Younger residents like the plan more. Overall, 52% support it. This is the number the poll is trying to find.

The sampling frame: the list you can actually reach. Only people on the council's email list can be asked. Nearly all under-30s are on it; most over-65s are not. Anyone off the list has zero chance of being heard. That is coverage bias.

The respondents: of those emailed, about one in eight opens it and answers, and supporters are a little keener. People who never read the email are silent. That is nonresponse bias.

The poll reports 61% support, with a tidy-looking interval of ±3 points. The truth, 52%, is nowhere near it. The interval only describes the luck of the draw among people who answered.

Email ten times as many people and the interval shrinks to ±1 point, still centred on 61%. More of the same people makes the wrong answer look more certain. Fixing who gets asked is the only real fix.

Each arrow loses people. If the people lost at each step are a fair cross-section, you just have fewer answers and wider error bars. If the people lost are different from the people kept (older, busier, less happy, less online), your answer drifts away from the truth, and no amount of extra data fixes the drift.

Under the hoodInclusion probabilities

Give every unit i in the population an inclusion probability \(\pi_i\): the chance it ends up among the respondents, the product of being in the frame, being selected and responding. A design is probability sampling when every \(\pi_i\) is known and positive. Then the Horvitz–Thompson estimator is unbiased for the population total:

\[ \hat T_{HT} = \sum_{i \in \text{sample}} \frac{y_i}{\pi_i}, \qquad \mathbb{E}\big[\hat T_{HT}\big] = \sum_{i=1}^{N} y_i \]Each sampled unit stands in for \(1/\pi_i\) population units. Coverage bias means some \(\pi_i = 0\), and no weight can recover them. Nonresponse means the true \(\pi_i\) are unknown and must be modelled.

With equal probabilities this collapses to the plain sample mean. Everything in this chapter is a variation: either the \(\pi_i\) are known (probability designs), or they are unknown and correlated with the outcome (the biases), or they are estimated and inverted (weighting).

The ways a sample goes wrong

Plain wordsSix everyday traps

Coverage bias

Some people cannot be reached at all. An email poll misses everyone without email. A phone survey of landlines misses people with only mobiles.

Nonresponse bias

People are reached but do not answer, and the ones who answer differ. Busy parents skip the survey; retirees fill it in.

Self-selection

People choose to take part. App-store reviews come mostly from the delighted and the furious; opt-in polls from people who care a lot.

Survivorship

You only see the ones that made it through. Advice from successful founders ignores the identical choices of founders who failed.

Convenience sample

You ask whoever is nearby. A team testing its own model writes the kinds of prompts it already knows work.

Time-window bias

You sample a period that is not typical. Logs from launch week, a holiday, or only office hours in one time zone.

Under the hoodBias is a covariance

Let \(R_i\) be the probability that unit i ends up in your data (a response propensity). The respondent mean is a propensity-weighted mean, and its bias has a compact form:

\[ \mathbb{E}[\bar y_{\text{resp}}] - \bar Y = \frac{\operatorname{Cov}(R, Y)}{\bar R} = \frac{\rho_{R,Y}\,\sigma_R\,\sigma_Y}{\bar R} \]\(\bar R\) is the mean propensity (the response rate). Bias needs propensity to be correlated with the outcome. A low response rate is not biased by itself, but it magnifies any correlation, because \(\bar R\) sits in the denominator.

Two classic cases show both halves at work:

  • The 1936 Literary Digest poll mailed about 10 million ballots, drawn largely from telephone directories and car registrations, and got about 2.4 million back. It predicted that Alf Landon would beat Franklin Roosevelt; Roosevelt won with about 61% of the popular vote. The frame skewed wealthier (coverage), and Landon supporters were more likely to mail ballots back (nonresponse). Squire (1988) concluded from a 1937 survey that nonresponse contributed heavily; Lusinchi (2012) reviews the debate over which mattered more.
  • Online reviews. Hu, Pavlou and Zhang (2009) found Amazon ratings piled up at 5 stars with a bump at 1 star, a J shape. When they had people rate a randomly assigned CD, ratings were unimodal and the mean was significantly lower. They attribute the J to purchasing bias (only people who expect to like a product buy it) and under-reporting bias (people with extreme views are likelier to post).

Length-biased sampling is a time-window trap worth knowing by name. Sample "a random active conversation at noon" and a conversation's chance of being picked is proportional to its duration, so long sessions are over-represented. The same holds for "a random running job" or "a random open ticket". Sample by start event instead.

Survivorship bias: the bombers that did not come back

Armour where the holes are not

The Second World War version, told carefully, then the version in your logs. Simulated planes, illustrative counts.

Bombers come back from missions with bullet holes. Plot where the holes are and they cluster on the wings, fuselage and tail. The engines and cockpit look comparatively clean.

The obvious reading: those areas take the most fire, so armour them. Armour is heavy, so you can only add it in a few places.

The better question: if hits land roughly evenly, where are the missing holes? The engines show far fewer holes per square foot than their size predicts.

The missing holes are on the planes that never returned. A hit to an engine was often fatal, so planes hit there rarely came home to be counted. The clean areas on survivors are the dangerous ones.

Your production logs are the planes that came back. Users who left after bad answers stop generating logs, so the logs over-represent the cases where the model worked. Evaluate on cohorts from their first day, not on whoever is still around.

Plain wordsAsk what happened to the ones you cannot see

Survivorship bias is looking only at the things that made it through a filter and forgetting the filter. Old buildings seem better built than new ones because the badly built old ones fell down. Successful dropouts seem to show that leaving university works because unsuccessful dropouts do not give talks.

The habit to build: whenever you look at a group, ask "what had to happen for something to end up in this group, and what does the group look like that didn't make it?"

For leadersThe story is true in outline, embellished in detail

The core is documented. Around 1943 Abraham Wald, a mathematician with the Statistical Research Group at Columbia University, wrote a series of memoranda on estimating aircraft vulnerability from the damage on planes that returned. His key move was to treat the missing planes as data: under the assumption that hits are spread across the aircraft, a section with fewer hits on survivors than its share of the surface implies hits there were more often fatal. Mangel and Samaniego's paper in the Journal of the American Statistical Association (1984) is the standard guide to the memoranda.

The vivid parts are less certain. A 2016 AMS Feature Column ("The Legend of Abraham Wald") notes that the memoranda do not say where the military should put armour, that the dramatic dialogue with officers is undocumented, and that the famous plane covered in red dots is a modern illustration, not Wald's drawing. Tell the reasoning, not the legend.

In AI work, survivorship hides in any dataset assembled after an outcome: logs of retained users, case studies of successful deployments, eval items that "made it" through filtering, and model checkpoints that were not discarded early.

Under the hoodEstimating lethality from survivors

Suppose hits land on section s with probability equal to its area share \(a_s\), and a hit there downs the plane with probability \(k_s\). Among hits on returning planes, the share in section s is proportional to \(a_s (1-k_s)\) times the chance the plane's other hits were survivable. For a single hit per plane:

\[ \frac{\text{returned holes in } s \,/\, a_s}{\text{returned holes overall}} = \frac{1-k_s}{\sum_t a_t (1-k_t)} \quad\Rightarrow\quad \frac{1-k_s}{1-k_t} = \frac{d_s}{d_t} \]\(d_s\) is holes per unit area on returned planes. Ratios of observed densities identify ratios of survival probabilities. A section whose density is far below the plane average is where hits are most lethal. Wald's memoranda worked out versions of this with multiple hits per plane.

The assumption doing the work is that hits are evenly spread. If gunners aimed at engines, low engine density on survivors would mean even higher lethality; if engines were hard to hit, it would mean less. The same is true in product data: survivorship arguments need a model of what the unfiltered population looked like.

Why a bigger biased sample does not help

Plain wordsA bigger bucket from the wrong well

Imagine tasting soup to check the salt, but you only ever dip the spoon near the top where the salt has not mixed in. Tasting ten spoonfuls instead of one makes you very sure of the wrong answer. More data reduces the wobble of your estimate. It does nothing about which part of the pot you are dipping into.

For leadersSmall and random beats huge and skewed

Xiao-Li Meng (2018, Annals of Applied Statistics) put numbers on this. Using the 2016 US presidential election, he estimated that whether a person reported a vote for Trump in survey data was correlated with actually voting for him at only about ρ ≈ −0.005. With a self-reported sample of about 2.3 million people, roughly 1% of eligible voters, that tiny correlation gives the same mean squared error as a genuine simple random sample of about 400 people: a 99.98% reduction in effective sample size.

  • Prefer a small probability sample to a large convenience sample when the decision depends on a population average. 400 random users can beat 2 million self-selected ones.
  • Treat a huge n as a warning sign for overconfidence, not as reassurance. The error bars from big logs are tiny and exclude the truth.
  • Spend on representativeness first, size second. Once the design is sound, use power analysis to choose n.

The opposite case is instructive too: Wang, Rothschild, Goel and Gelman (2015) polled 345,858 Xbox users before the 2012 election, a sample that was 93% male against 47% of the electorate. Raw, it was useless. After heavy statistical adjustment it forecast well. The data were biased in ways the adjustment variables could see; that is the case weighting can rescue, discussed below.

Under the hoodMeng's identity

For any dataset of n units from a population of N, random or not, with \(R_i = 1\) if unit i is recorded and \(f = n/N\), the error of the sample mean factors exactly into three terms (Meng 2018):

\[ \bar G_n - \bar G_N \;=\; \underbrace{\rho_{R,G}}_{\text{data quality}} \times \underbrace{\sqrt{\frac{1-f}{f}}}_{\text{data quantity}} \times \underbrace{\sigma_G}_{\text{problem difficulty}} \]\(\rho_{R,G}\) is the data defect correlation between being recorded and the value. Under simple random sampling its typical size is about \(1/\sqrt{N}\), which makes the error shrink with n. A non-random process can fix \(\rho\) at a value that does not depend on N, and then the error depends on the fraction f, not on n.

Equating mean squared errors with a simple random sample of size \(n_{\text{eff}}\) gives \(n_{\text{eff}} \approx \frac{f}{1-f}\,\frac{1}{\rho^2}\). Doubling a big biased sample barely helps; a tiny change in \(\rho\) helps enormously.

import math

def meng_error(rho, f, sigma):
    # error of the sample mean = data quality x data quantity x difficulty
    return rho * math.sqrt((1 - f) / f) * sigma

def effective_n(rho, f):
    # size of a simple random sample with the same mean squared error
    return f / (1 - f) / rho ** 2

rho, sigma = -0.005, 0.5        # binary outcome near 50/50 has sigma 0.5
for f in (0.01, 0.02, 0.05, 0.5):
    print(f, f"{meng_error(rho, f, sigma):+.4f}", round(effective_n(rho, f)))
# 0.01 -0.0249 404     1% of voters (about 2.3 million) is worth ~400 random ones
# 0.02 -0.0175 816     doubling the data cuts the error by only 30%
# 0.05 -0.0109 2105
# 0.5 -0.0025 40000    even half the population is worth 40,000 random draws
print(f"{sigma / math.sqrt(404):.4f}")   # 0.0249 the same error, from n = 404 at random
fn meng_error(rho: f64, f: f64, sigma: f64) -> f64 {
    // error of the sample mean = data quality x data quantity x difficulty
    rho * ((1.0 - f) / f).sqrt() * sigma
}

fn effective_n(rho: f64, f: f64) -> f64 {
    // size of a simple random sample with the same mean squared error
    f / (1.0 - f) / rho.powi(2)
}

fn main() {
    let (rho, sigma) = (-0.005, 0.5); // binary outcome near 50/50 has sigma 0.5
    for f in [0.01, 0.02, 0.05, 0.5] {
        println!("{} {:+.4} {}", f, meng_error(rho, f, sigma), effective_n(rho, f).round());
    }
    // 0.01 -0.0249 404     1% of voters (about 2.3 million) is worth ~400 random ones
    // 0.02 -0.0175 816     doubling the data cuts the error by only 30%
    // 0.05 -0.0109 2105
    // 0.5 -0.0025 40000    even half the population is worth 40,000 random draws
    println!("{:.4}", sigma / 404f64.sqrt()); // 0.0249 the same error, from n = 404 at random
}

Probability sampling: four designs

Four fair ways to pick 12 houses

A town of 120 houses in four neighbourhoods. Each design gives every house a known chance of being picked.

Simple random sampling: every house, and every set of 12 houses, is equally likely. The baseline. By luck it can under-sample a neighbourhood: here West gets one house while North gets five.

Stratified sampling: split the town into neighbourhoods (strata) first, then sample at random inside each. No neighbourhood can be missed, and if neighbourhoods differ, the estimate is more precise than simple random sampling.

Cluster sampling: pick whole streets at random and visit every house on them. Much cheaper to run, but neighbours resemble each other, so 12 houses on 2 streets carry less information than 12 scattered houses.

Systematic sampling: from a random start, take every 10th house on the list. Easy and spreads the sample out, but dangerous if the list has a rhythm, say every 10th house is a corner plot.

The designs differ in cost and precision, but all four share the property that matters: the chance of selection is known and set by the design, not by who chooses to answer. That is what makes the error bar honest.

Plain wordsLet a fair lottery do the choosing

The way to avoid every trap above is to let a lottery, not convenience or volunteering, decide who is measured. A lottery cannot prefer the keen, the nearby or the survivors. The four designs in the diagram are four ways to run that lottery: plain random, random within groups, random groups, and every k-th from a random start.

Stratifying is the one to reach for most often. If you know the groups that matter (language, task type, customer tier), make sure each gets its fair share instead of hoping luck provides it.

Under the hoodVariances of the four designs

For a population mean with strata h of population share \(W_h\), within-stratum SD \(S_h\) and sample size \(n_h\):

\[ \operatorname{Var}(\bar y_{\text{SRS}}) \approx \frac{S^2}{n}, \qquad \operatorname{Var}(\bar y_{\text{st}}) = \sum_h W_h^2\,\frac{S_h^2}{n_h}, \qquad n_h^{\text{Neyman}} \propto W_h S_h \]Finite-population corrections omitted. With proportional allocation \(n_h = nW_h\), stratified variance is \(\sum_h W_h S_h^2/n\), which is never larger than SRS and smaller when stratum means differ. Neyman allocation puts more sample where strata are large or variable.

Cluster sampling with m units per cluster and intra-cluster correlation \(\rho\) inflates variance by the design effect \(1+(m-1)\rho\) (standard errors, clustered intervals). Ten turns per conversation with \(\rho = 0.3\) gives a design effect of 3.7: 1,000 turns are worth about 270 independent ones. Systematic sampling behaves like SRS when the list order is unrelated to the outcome, better than SRS when the order is a smooth trend (it acts like implicit stratification), and badly when the order has a period that divides the step.

Weighting and post-stratification

Plain wordsCount the under-represented more

If your poll has too many young people and too few old people, you can count each older respondent a bit more and each younger respondent a bit less, so the age mix matches the town's. That is post-stratification: after the fact, give each group the weight it has in the population.

It fixes the part of the bias that comes from having the wrong mix of groups. It cannot fix bias inside a group: if the older people who answered are unlike the older people who did not, giving them more weight just amplifies the unusual ones. Try it in the widget above: the email poll improves but does not reach the truth.

For leadersWhat weighting can and cannot rescue
  • It works when you can see the cause of the skew. The Xbox poll was rescued because its biases ran mostly through age, sex, party and past vote, all of which were measured and adjusted for with multilevel regression and post-stratification (MRP). Its final national estimate was within about 0.6 points of the result (Wang et al. 2015).
  • It fails when the skew runs through something unmeasured. Satisfaction surveys skew toward engaged users in ways that tenure and plan type only partly capture.
  • Weights cost precision. A few heavily weighted respondents make the estimate jumpy. Always report the effective sample size next to the raw count.
  • Weights need accurate population totals and non-empty cells. If your poll has no respondents over 80, no weight can conjure them.

For eval sets the same logic applies: if your set is 70% English and production is 40% English, report per-language scores and a production-weighted total, and say which languages had too few items to trust.

Under the hoodWeights, effective n, raking

Post-stratification over cells g with known population shares \(W_g\) and respondent counts \(n_g\):

\[ \hat{\bar y}_{\text{ps}} = \sum_g W_g\, \bar y_g, \qquad w_i = \frac{W_g}{n_g/n} \;\; (i \in g), \qquad n_{\text{eff}} = \frac{\big(\sum_i w_i\big)^2}{\sum_i w_i^2} \]\(n_{\text{eff}}\) is Kish's effective sample size: how many unweighted, random respondents would give the same precision. Unbiased only if, within each cell, respondents resemble non-respondents on the outcome (missing at random given the cells).

With several weighting variables whose joint distribution is unknown, raking (iterative proportional fitting) adjusts weights to match each margin in turn until all match. MRP replaces raw cell means with a multilevel model's predictions, which keeps small cells from producing wild estimates.

# Email poll respondents by age band (illustrative), and the town's age mix.
BANDS      = ["18-29", "30-44", "45-64", "65+"]
POP_SHARE  = [0.20, 0.25, 0.33, 0.22]
RESPONDED  = [261, 286, 259, 84]
SUPPORTERS = [197, 184, 128, 33]

n = sum(RESPONDED)
raw = sum(SUPPORTERS) / n

# weight per respondent in each band: population share / sample share
weights = [p / (r / n) for p, r in zip(POP_SHARE, RESPONDED)]
post_strat = sum(p * s / r for p, s, r in zip(POP_SHARE, SUPPORTERS, RESPONDED))

# Kish effective sample size: the price of uneven weights
sum_w = sum(w * r for w, r in zip(weights, RESPONDED))
sum_w2 = sum(w * w * r for w, r in zip(weights, RESPONDED))
n_eff = sum_w ** 2 / sum_w2

print(f"{raw:.3f}")                              # 0.609 raw poll
print(" ".join(f"{w:.2f}" for w in weights))     # 0.68 0.78 1.13 2.33 over-65s count 2.3x
print(f"{post_strat:.3f}")                       # 0.561 age mix fixed; truth is 0.520
print(f"{n_eff:.0f}")                            # 731 of 890 respondents' worth of precision
// Email poll respondents by age band (illustrative), and the town's age mix.
const POP_SHARE: [f64; 4] = [0.20, 0.25, 0.33, 0.22];
const RESPONDED: [f64; 4] = [261.0, 286.0, 259.0, 84.0];
const SUPPORTERS: [f64; 4] = [197.0, 184.0, 128.0, 33.0];

fn main() {
    let n: f64 = RESPONDED.iter().sum();
    let raw = SUPPORTERS.iter().sum::<f64>() / n;

    // weight per respondent in each band: population share / sample share
    let weights: Vec<f64> = (0..4).map(|b| POP_SHARE[b] / (RESPONDED[b] / n)).collect();
    let post_strat: f64 = (0..4).map(|b| POP_SHARE[b] * SUPPORTERS[b] / RESPONDED[b]).sum();

    // Kish effective sample size: the price of uneven weights
    let sum_w: f64 = (0..4).map(|b| weights[b] * RESPONDED[b]).sum();
    let sum_w2: f64 = (0..4).map(|b| weights[b] * weights[b] * RESPONDED[b]).sum();
    let n_eff = sum_w.powi(2) / sum_w2;

    println!("{:.3}", raw); // 0.609 raw poll
    let ws: Vec<String> = weights.iter().map(|w| format!("{:.2}", w)).collect();
    println!("{}", ws.join(" ")); // 0.68 0.78 1.13 2.33 over-65s count 2.3x
    println!("{:.3}", post_strat); // 0.561 age mix fixed; truth is 0.520
    println!("{:.0}", n_eff); // 731 of 890 respondents' worth of precision
}

Sample size is not representativeness

Plain wordsTwo different questions

"Is my sample big enough?" and "is my sample the right people?" are separate questions. Size controls how much your answer wobbles. Representativeness controls whether it is aimed at the right target. A small fair sample is blurry but centred; a large unfair one is sharp and off-centre.

For leadersAnswer them in order

First settle the frame and the design: who could be in the data, and how they are chosen. Only then size the sample with power analysis for the smallest difference that would change the decision. A review that starts with "we have 50,000 examples" and never reaches "drawn how, from what" has answered the easy question.

Under the hoodMean squared error has two parts
\[ \operatorname{MSE}(\hat\theta) = \underbrace{\operatorname{Bias}(\hat\theta)^2}_{\text{does not shrink with } n} + \underbrace{\operatorname{Var}(\hat\theta)}_{\approx\, \sigma^2/n} \]Past the point where variance falls below squared bias, extra data buys almost nothing. With 9 points of bias, as in the email poll, that point arrives at a few dozen respondents.

The coverage of a nominal 95% interval also collapses as n grows when bias is fixed: the interval half-width shrinks like \(1/\sqrt n\) while the distance to the truth stays put, so coverage tends to zero. A biased design with a huge n produces confident, wrong intervals almost every time.

In AI evals

Plain wordsYour test set is a poll of prompts

An eval set is a sample of prompts standing in for all the prompts the model will face. Every trap in this chapter applies. If the set was built from whatever was easy to collect (English, short, clearly answerable), the score describes that slice and not the product.

For leadersFour common sampling failures in model evaluation
  • Easy and English-heavy eval sets. Items get written or kept because they are gradeable and unambiguous, and authors write in the languages they know. Scores then over-state performance on the hard, messy, multilingual tail. Stratify by language and difficulty and report each stratum (classification metrics by slice).
  • Production logs that only include users who stayed. Survivorship. Build eval sets from first-session cohorts, or weight by acquisition cohort, so that the users the model failed are represented.
  • Red-team pools. Findings reflect who was recruited: security researchers find jailbreaks; they are less likely to find harms that show up for non-experts or in other languages. Treat red-team counts as a lower bound on a frame you chose, and vary the pool.
  • Feedback signals. Thumbs, ratings and escalations come from a self-selected minority. Use them to find failures, not to estimate rates. Estimate rates on a random sample with paid, blind grading (judges, metrics).
Under the hoodBuilding a representative eval sample
  1. Define the population as a traffic distribution over a future window, by surface, customer tier, language and task type.
  2. List the frame and its gaps: which of those cells can you sample from logs, and which need synthetic or commissioned prompts? Record each gap.
  3. Stratify on the cells, over-sampling small but important strata so each has enough items for an interval, and cluster-sample conversations, with the design effect in the error bars.
  4. Track item loss from sampling to graded result. Items dropped as "ambiguous" or "ungradeable" are nonresponse; compare their features with kept items.
  5. Report per-stratum scores, a production-weighted total with its effective n, and the stated gaps. Refresh the sample as traffic drifts (offline to online gap, contamination).
Eval lens
Before comparing two models on an eval set, check that the set's mix matches the decision. A model can win on a set that is 80% easy English prompts and lose on production traffic that is 40% hard multilingual ones: that is Simpson's paradox caused by a sampling choice (correlation is not causation). Report per-stratum results and a total weighted to the population the decision is about.
Principal asks
What is the population, and what is the frame? Who can never appear in this data? What fraction of the sampled items or people made it to a result, and how do the dropped ones differ? Was selection by lottery or by convenience? If you weighted, on what variables, and what is the effective sample size? Would a small random sample have given a different answer?
Common mistakes
  • Believing a large n cancels out a biased frame. It only shrinks the error bar around the wrong value.
  • Treating a low response rate as fine because "the rest would answer the same way", without checking how responders differ.
  • Estimating rates from opt-in signals (thumbs, reviews, escalations) instead of a random sample.
  • Building eval sets from retained users' logs and calling them representative of new users.
  • Weighting on variables that do not drive the skew, then reporting the weighted number as unbiased.
  • Reporting the raw count as the sample size after heavy weighting or clustered sampling.
  • Telling the Wald bomber story with the invented details instead of the documented reasoning.
