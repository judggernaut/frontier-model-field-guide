Distillation, synthetic data, and merging

Most training data for modern post-training is written by models, not people. A large teacher model generates examples, a filter keeps the good ones, and a smaller or newer student learns from them. This chapter covers the three ways models learn from other models: distillation, synthetic data with filtering, and merging weights directly.

The chef's tasting notes

Why learning from a teacher's probabilities beats learning from an answer key.

An answer key gives one right answer and nothing else. Training on it tells the student that salt is correct, and says nothing about whether soy sauce was a near miss or sugar was absurd. This is a hard label.

A master chef thinking out loud gives far more: a full set of probabilities. The runner-ups carry the "almost right" information Hinton and colleagues called dark knowledge. Distillation trains the small model on these soft labels.

Raising the temperature divides the teacher's logits before the softmax, flattening the bars. The ranking stays the same, but the small differences between wrong answers become big enough for the student to learn from.

The student is trained to match the whole shape, usually by minimising the KL divergence between the two distributions. Each example now teaches a ranking, not just a winner, which is why small models distilled from big ones beat small models trained from scratch.

The catch: the student copies mistakes too, confidence included. If the teacher leans the wrong way (the answer is Canberra), so does the student. A distilled model is capped by its teacher and inherits its blind spots.

Knowledge distillation

Plain wordsLearning from the teacher's hesitation

A student can learn from an answer key: "the answer is Paris." Or they can learn from a teacher who thinks out loud: "Paris, almost certainly. Lyon is a distant second. It's definitely not banana." The second version carries more information. It tells the student which wrong answers are nearly right and which are absurd. Distillation trains a small model on the big model's full set of probabilities, not just its single best answer. Hinton and colleagues called that extra information "dark knowledge."

For leadersWhy every lab distils

Distillation is how a lab turns one expensive frontier model into a family of cheaper ones. Small models trained from a strong teacher usually beat small models trained from scratch on the same compute. Gemma 2 (Google, 2024) trained its 2B and 9B models with distillation from a larger model in place of plain next-token prediction. DeepSeek-R1 (2025) fine-tuned smaller Qwen and Llama models on about 800K curated samples (roughly 600K R1 reasoning traces plus 200K non-reasoning examples) and reported that this beat running RL directly on the small models.

Logit distillation

Match the teacher's full probability distribution at each token. Needs the teacher's logits and a shared tokenizer, so it is mostly done inside one lab.

Sequence distillation

Train on text the teacher generated (Kim & Rush, 2016). Works through an API and across tokenizers. This is what "training on model outputs" usually means.

On-policy distillation

The student writes the text and the teacher grades every token (Agarwal et al., 2024). Fixes the student's own mistakes instead of only copying perfect examples.

Reasoning-trace distillation

Train on the teacher's step-by-step solutions. Fast way to give a small model long-form reasoning; inherits the teacher's errors and verbosity.

Watch out
A distilled student is capped by its teacher and copies its blind spots. If the teacher hallucinates on a topic, the student will too, with the same confidence. Distilling from a model you don't control also raises terms-of-service and licensing questions: many providers' terms restrict using outputs to build competing models, and open-weight licenses carry their own conditions. Get legal review before a data pipeline depends on another company's model.
Under the hoodSoft targets, temperature, and which KL

With teacher logits \(z^{T}\) and student logits \(z^{S}\), soften both with temperature \(\tau\):

\[ p_i^{(\tau)} = \frac{\exp(z_i/\tau)}{\sum_j \exp(z_j/\tau)}, \qquad \mathcal{L} = (1-\lambda)\,\mathrm{CE}\big(y,\, p^{S,(1)}\big) + \lambda\,\tau^{2}\,\mathrm{KL}\big(p^{T,(\tau)} \,\big\|\, p^{S,(\tau)}\big) \]Hinton, Vinyals & Dean (2015). The \(\tau^2\) factor keeps the soft-target gradients on the same scale as the hard-label term when \(\tau\) changes. \(\lambda\) balances the two.

The direction of the KL matters for generative models:

  • Forward KL, \(\mathrm{KL}(p^T \| p^S) = \sum_i p_i^T \log \frac{p_i^T}{p_i^S}\), punishes the student for putting low probability anywhere the teacher puts mass. It is mode covering: a small student spreads itself thin and can produce blends of answers.
  • Reverse KL, \(\mathrm{KL}(p^S \| p^T)\), punishes the student for putting mass where the teacher doesn't. It is mode seeking: the student commits to the teacher's main modes. MiniLLM (Gu et al., 2024) argued reverse KL suits small generative students better. On-policy distillation scores the student's own samples against the teacher, most often with a per-token reverse KL (GKD, Agarwal et al. 2024, allows other divergences).
import torch, torch.nn.functional as F

def kd_loss(student_logits, teacher_logits, labels, tau=2.0, lam=0.5):
    """Hinton-style distillation for a batch of next-token predictions.
    logits: [batch, vocab]; labels: [batch] token ids (-100 to ignore)."""
    hard = F.cross_entropy(student_logits, labels, ignore_index=-100)
    log_ps = F.log_softmax(student_logits / tau, dim=-1)
    log_pt = F.log_softmax(teacher_logits / tau, dim=-1)
    # forward KL(teacher || student); kl_div expects log-probs for the input
    soft = F.kl_div(log_ps, log_pt, log_target=True, reduction="batchmean")
    return (1 - lam) * hard + lam * tau**2 * soft

s, t = torch.randn(4, 10), torch.randn(4, 10)
print(kd_loss(s, t, torch.tensor([1, 2, 3, -100])).item() > 0)  # True

Synthetic data and rejection sampling

Twenty shots, keep the sharp ones

How models make their own training data, and the bias hiding in the filter.

Making training data is easier when checking is easier than creating. Ask the model to solve the same problem many times, like a photographer firing off 20 shots of a moving bird.

A checker throws away every shot that is not sharp. For math it compares the final answer; for code it runs tests. Only the passing answers become training examples. This is rejection sampling.

Train on the keepers, and the improved model takes better shots next round. Repeating this loop is the idea behind STaR (Zelikman et al., 2022) and rejection-sampling fine-tuning, and it is a simple offline cousin of RL.

The hidden bias: easy prompts produce many keepers and hard prompts produce almost none. Expected keepers per prompt are 20 × pass rate, so the training set quietly over-represents problems the model can already solve.

Two fixes keep the album balanced: cap how many keepers any one prompt contributes, and spend more attempts on hard prompts. Watch the pass rate on the hardest prompts across rounds, not just the average.

Plain wordsWrite ten drafts, keep the ones that check out

If you can check an answer more easily than you can write it, you can make training data cheaply. Ask a model to solve a math problem ten times. Check each answer against the known result. Keep the correct solutions and train on them. Repeat with the improved model. This is the engine behind most reasoning data today.

For leadersThe filter is the product

Generating text is cheap. The value of a synthetic data pipeline sits in its filter: unit tests for code, exact-match answers for math, a reward model or LLM judge for open-ended tasks, and human spot checks throughout. The main methods:

  • Self-Instruct (Wang et al., 2023) and Evol-Instruct (Xu et al., 2023): a model writes new instructions from seed examples, or rewrites existing ones to be harder or more varied.
  • STaR (Zelikman et al., 2022): generate reasoning, keep traces that reach the right answer, fine-tune, repeat. For failures, give the answer as a hint and ask for a rationale ("rationalisation").
  • Rejection sampling fine-tuning (Yuan et al., 2023; also used in Llama 2): sample many responses, keep the best by verifier or reward model, and fine-tune on them. It is a simple offline relative of RL.
Watch out
Rejection sampling quietly reshapes your data toward problems the model can already solve. Prompts with a 1% pass rate rarely produce a kept example, so the training set over-represents easy problems. The model gets better at what it already does well. Watch coverage of hard prompts, cap examples per prompt, and spend extra samples on hard prompts.
Under the hoodYield, coverage, and model collapse

For a prompt with pass rate \(p\) and \(k\) samples, the expected number of correct samples is \(kp\), and the chance of at least one is \(1-(1-p)^k\). Averaging over prompts:

\[ \text{coverage} = \mathbb{E}_p\!\left[1-(1-p)^k\right], \qquad \text{kept share from prompt } i \propto p_i \]Without a cap, each prompt contributes in proportion to its pass rate, so easy prompts dominate. Capping at one kept example per prompt changes the weight to \(1-(1-p_i)^k\), which flattens toward uniform as \(k\) grows.

Model collapse. Shumailov et al. (2024, Nature) showed that models trained recursively on their own outputs, generation after generation, lose the tails of the original distribution first and then degrade. Rare facts, unusual styles, and minority languages vanish. Later work (Gerstgrasser et al., 2024) found collapse is avoided when synthetic data is added to real data rather than replacing it. In practice: keep real human data in the mix, filter synthetic data hard, track diversity metrics across rounds, and never let a pipeline train only on its own previous generation.

Model merging

Averaging two edited maps

Why model merging works, and when it cannot.

Start with one city map: the pretrained base model. Two teams will each make their own edit to a copy of it.

Each copy is fine-tuned for a different skill. The changes are small edits on top of the same base, so the two maps still line up street for street.

Because both edits start from the same map, you can combine them number by number. Task arithmetic adds each change to the base; a plain average keeps half of each. Often the merged model is decent at both skills, at no training cost. It still needs the full regression suite.

Merging only works for models with a shared parent. Two models trained from different random starts are like maps of different cities: their weights do not correspond, and averaging produces garbage.

Plain wordsAveraging two specialists

Take two copies of the same model. Teach one math and the other coding. Surprisingly, you can often average their weights, number by number, and get one model that is decent at both. It only works when both started from the same parent, like two edits of the same document.

For leadersCheap gains, unclear guarantees

Merging costs almost no compute and is now a routine post-training step: averaging checkpoints from different data mixes or seeds, combining a reasoning specialist with a chat model, or folding several fine-tunes into one release. It is also unpredictable. A merge can lift one benchmark and quietly break safety behaviour or formatting, so a merged model needs the full regression suite like any other release candidate.

Under the hoodSoups, task vectors, TIES
  • Model soups (Wortsman et al., 2022): average the weights of several fine-tunes of one base, trained with different hyperparameters. Often beats the best single model, at no inference cost.
  • Task arithmetic (Ilharco et al., 2023): define a task vector \(\tau = \theta_{\text{ft}} - \theta_{\text{base}}\). Add vectors to combine skills, subtract one to remove a behaviour: \(\theta = \theta_{\text{base}} + \sum_i \lambda_i \tau_i\).
  • TIES-Merging (Yadav et al., 2023): trim small entries of each task vector, resolve sign conflicts by majority, then average only agreeing entries. Reduces interference between tasks. DARE (Yu et al., 2024) randomly drops most delta entries and rescales the rest before merging.

Merging works because fine-tunes of a shared base tend to stay in one low-loss basin (linear mode connectivity). Models trained from different initialisations are not connected this way, and averaging them produces garbage.

Eval lens

Models learning from models create eval traps you won't see unless you look:

  • Teacher-induced contamination. If the teacher memorised a benchmark, its synthetic data can contain paraphrases of test items. The student then scores well without the skill. Run overlap checks against every eval set (see contamination), including paraphrase detection, not just exact n-grams.
  • Judge self-preference. An LLM judge from the same family as the teacher tends to prefer outputs written in its own style. A distilled student can win judge-based comparisons by sounding like the judge. Use a judge from a different family, and validate against human labels.
  • Style over substance. Distillation transfers length, formatting, and confident tone faster than correctness. Control for length in win rates and keep verifiable tasks in the suite.
  • Diversity loss. Track output diversity (distinct n-grams, embedding spread, pass@k versus pass@1) across synthetic-data rounds. Falling diversity is an early warning of collapse.
Principal asks
  • What fraction of our post-training data is synthetic, which models generated it, and are we allowed to use their outputs this way?
  • What is the filter, how was it validated, and what is its false-accept rate?
  • How does pass rate on the hardest decile of prompts change across rounds, not just the average?
  • Did we check the synthetic data for overlap with every benchmark we report?
  • For a merged model: which components, which method, and what did the full regression suite show compared with each parent?
