Two big ideas: the sampling distribution and the standard error

If you take only two things from Part I, take these. Nearly every statistical mistake in eval work is a misunderstanding of one of them, and every confidence interval and p value later in the guide is built on them.

Polling a neighbourhood about a new park

The sampling distribution and the standard error, with dots you can count.

A town of 10,000 people (each dot stands for 40). Exactly 60% like the plan for a new park. In real life you never see this picture; you only get to ask a few people.

You stop 50 people at random. 31 say yes, so your poll says 62%. Close to the truth, but not equal to it, and you have no way to know how close.

Imagine 36 different pollsters, each asking 50 different people. Their answers pile up around 60%, some as low as 48%, some as high as 72%. This pile is the sampling distribution.

Ask 200 people per poll and the pile tightens to about half the width. Four times the people buys half the uncertainty. The width of the pile is the standard error.

An eval score is one poll of tasks. A 62% pass rate on 200 tasks is one dot from a pile about ±7 points wide. That is why "62% vs 60%" on 200 tasks is not a result yet.

Plain wordsYour score is one draw from a lottery of possible scores

You ran 200 tasks and got 62%. If you had run a different 200 tasks you would have got something else, maybe 59%, maybe 65%. The spread of all those hypothetical alternative answers is the sampling distribution. Every confidence interval and every p value is a statement about it, not about your data.

The standard error is how wide that lottery is. It shrinks as you test more tasks, but slowly: to cut the uncertainty in half you need four times as many tasks.

The sampling distribution, and why the bell curve is everywhere

Here is the part that surprises people. The raw data can be any shape at all. But the average of many draws is always roughly a bell, and it gets more bell-like as you average more. That is the central limit theorem.

\[ \bar{X}_n \;\longrightarrow\; \mathcal{N}\!\left(\mu,\ \frac{\sigma^2}{n}\right) \quad \text{as } n \text{ grows} \]The average of n draws tends toward a normal shape centred on the true mean, with variance shrinking as one over n.
Key idea
This is why so much of statistics assumes normality even though almost no real data is normal. The assumption is not about your measurements, it is about their average. Your latency data is lognormal and always will be; the mean of 500 latencies is close to normal.
Watch the central limit theorem happen
Pick any starting shape, however ugly. The top panel is a single measurement. The bottom is the average of n measurements. Push n up and watch the bottom become a bell regardless of what the top looks like.
Try it
Set the starting shape to Pareto and push n to 200. It becomes a bell far more slowly than the others. That is what a genuinely heavy tail does: the central limit theorem still applies, but it needs much more data before you can lean on it.

Standard deviation is not standard error

This is the single most common confusion in applied statistics, and the two get swapped in dashboards constantly.

Standard deviationStandard error
DescribesHow spread out the individual measurements areHow precisely you know the average
Formula\(s\)\(s/\sqrt{n}\)
As n growsStays put. More data does not make users more alikeShrinks, but slowly
Use forDescribing variability, setting SLOsError bars on a mean, confidence intervals
Key idea
Because standard error shrinks as one over the square root of n, quadrupling your sample halves your error bar. Not doubling, quadrupling. That single fact explains why every sample size answer feels so unsatisfying and why chasing small effects gets expensive so fast.
Plain wordsScattered arrows, wobbling averages

An archer shoots a volley of 20 arrows. The arrows land all over the target: that scatter is the standard deviation, and practising more volleys will not make a single arrow land closer to the others.

Now mark the average landing spot of each volley. Shoot another volley and the average lands somewhere slightly different. How much that average wobbles from volley to volley is the standard error. Shoot 80 arrows per volley instead of 20 and the average wobbles half as much, even though each arrow is just as scattered as before.

Variance is the standard deviation squared. It describes the arrows. The standard error describes your estimate built from the arrows.

For leadersReading error bars correctly
  • Ask which one the bars are. SD bars show how varied the users or tasks are and never shrink. SE bars (or a confidence interval, about ±2 SE) show how sure we are of the average and shrink with more data. Decks swap them constantly.
  • Precision is bought with the square root. Halving an error bar costs four times the data. That is why small effects get expensive and why reducing variance (paired designs, cleaner tasks) is often cheaper than collecting more.
  • Standard errors assume independent items. Multi-turn conversations, several calls per agent session, or many tasks from one repository are correlated. The honest SE is larger, sometimes several times larger.
Principal asks
Are those SD or SE bars? What is n, and are the items independent? If we doubled the eval set, how much would the interval shrink, and is that cheaper than a paired design?
Under the hoodStandard error, formally, and the square-root law in code

Definition. An estimator \(\hat\theta\) (a mean, a pass rate, a median, a difference between two models) is computed from a random sample, so it is itself a random variable with its own distribution across hypothetical repeats of the study: its sampling distribution. The standard error is the standard deviation of that sampling distribution:

\[ \operatorname{SE}(\hat\theta) \;=\; \sqrt{\operatorname{Var}(\hat\theta)} \;=\; \sqrt{\mathbb{E}\Big[\big(\hat\theta - \mathbb{E}[\hat\theta]\big)^2\Big]} \]Same formula as a standard deviation, applied to the estimate instead of to individual observations. In practice it is unknown and estimated from the data, written \(\widehat{\operatorname{SE}}\).

Derivation for the mean. Let \(X_1,\dots,X_n\) be independent with variance \(\sigma^2\). Two variance rules from chapter 2 do all the work: variances of independent terms add, and scaling by \(a\) multiplies variance by \(a^2\).

\[ \operatorname{Var}(\bar X) = \operatorname{Var}\Big(\tfrac{1}{n}\sum_{i=1}^n X_i\Big) = \tfrac{1}{n^2}\sum_{i=1}^n \operatorname{Var}(X_i) = \tfrac{1}{n^2}\cdot n\sigma^2 = \frac{\sigma^2}{n} \qquad\Longrightarrow\qquad \operatorname{SE}(\bar X)=\frac{\sigma}{\sqrt n},\quad \widehat{\operatorname{SE}}=\frac{s}{\sqrt n} \]The \(1/n^2\) from averaging beats the \(n\) from adding, leaving \(1/n\) for the variance and \(1/\sqrt n\) for the standard error. Independence is used in the second step; drop it and covariance terms appear.

Four quantities that get confused

QuantitySymbolDescribesUnitsAs n grows
Population variance\(\sigma^2\)How much individual values vary in the processSquaredFixed: a property of the world
Sample variance\(s^2\)Your estimate of \(\sigma^2\)SquaredSettles toward \(\sigma^2\)

So the difference between variance and standard error is not just a square root. Variance (and SD) describe the data; standard error describes an estimate, and it depends on three things the data's variance does not: the sample size, the estimator, and the dependence between observations.

import math

def se_mean(sd, n):
    return sd / math.sqrt(n)

def se_proportion(p, n):
    return math.sqrt(p * (1 - p) / n)

def se_diff_independent(s1, n1, s2, n2):
    # variances add for independent estimates; standard errors do not
    return math.sqrt(s1 ** 2 / n1 + s2 ** 2 / n2)

def se_clustered(se_naive, m, icc):
    # design effect: m items per cluster, intra-cluster correlation icc
    return se_naive * math.sqrt(1 + (m - 1) * icc)

print(round(se_mean(12.0, 400), 3))                          # 0.6 seconds
print(round(se_proportion(124 / 200, 200), 4))               # 0.0343 -> about +/- 6.7 points at 95%
print(round(se_diff_independent(0.49, 200, 0.48, 200), 4))   # 0.0485 two models on different task sets
print(round(se_clustered(0.0343, 12, 0.6), 4))               # 0.0946 same pass rate, 12 calls per session
fn se_mean(sd: f64, n: f64) -> f64 {
    sd / n.sqrt()
}

fn se_proportion(p: f64, n: f64) -> f64 {
    (p * (1.0 - p) / n).sqrt()
}

fn se_diff_independent(s1: f64, n1: f64, s2: f64, n2: f64) -> f64 {
    // variances add for independent estimates; standard errors do not
    (s1.powi(2) / n1 + s2.powi(2) / n2).sqrt()
}

fn se_clustered(se_naive: f64, m: f64, icc: f64) -> f64 {
    // design effect: m items per cluster, intra-cluster correlation icc
    se_naive * (1.0 + (m - 1.0) * icc).sqrt()
}

fn round(x: f64, digits: i32) -> f64 {
    let f = 10f64.powi(digits);
    (x * f).round() / f
}

fn main() {
    println!("{}", round(se_mean(12.0, 400.0), 3));                       // 0.6 seconds
    println!("{}", round(se_proportion(124.0 / 200.0, 200.0), 4));        // 0.0343 -> about +/- 6.7 points at 95%
    println!("{}", round(se_diff_independent(0.49, 200.0, 0.48, 200.0), 4)); // 0.0485 two models on different task sets
    println!("{}", round(se_clustered(0.0343, 12.0, 0.6), 4));            // 0.0946 same pass rate, 12 calls per session
}

The square-root law, simulated

\[ \text{SE} = \frac{s}{\sqrt{n}} \qquad\qquad \text{SE} \propto n^{-1/2} \;\Rightarrow\; 4n \text{ gives half the error bar} \]
import numpy as np

rng = np.random.default_rng(0)
population_sd = 12.0

for n in (25, 100, 400, 1600):
    sample = rng.normal(60, population_sd, n)

    sd = sample.std(ddof=1)          # spread of the MEASUREMENTS
    se = sd / np.sqrt(n)             # spread of the ESTIMATE of their mean

    print(n, "sd:", round(sd, 2), " se:", round(se, 3),
          " 95% CI half width:", round(1.96 * se, 3))

// A tiny inline PRNG (SplitMix64) so the snippet needs no crates.
// Its draws differ from numpy's, so the random numbers below differ from the Python's.
struct Rng(u64);

impl Rng {
    fn new(seed: u64) -> Self {
        Rng(seed)
    }
    fn next_u64(&mut self) -> u64 {
        self.0 = self.0.wrapping_add(0x9E37_79B9_7F4A_7C15);
        let mut z = self.0;
        z = (z ^ (z >> 30)).wrapping_mul(0xBF58_476D_1CE4_E5B9);
        z = (z ^ (z >> 27)).wrapping_mul(0x94D0_49BB_1331_11EB);
        z ^ (z >> 31)
    }
    /// Uniform on [0, 1).
    fn random(&mut self) -> f64 {
        (self.next_u64() >> 11) as f64 / (1u64 << 53) as f64
    }
    /// Normal draw by Box-Muller.
    fn normal(&mut self, mean: f64, sd: f64) -> f64 {
        let u1 = 1.0 - self.random(); // in (0, 1], safe for ln
        let u2 = self.random();
        mean + sd * (-2.0 * u1.ln()).sqrt() * (std::f64::consts::TAU * u2).cos()
    }
}

fn round(x: f64, digits: i32) -> f64 {
    let f = 10f64.powi(digits);
    (x * f).round() / f
}

/// Sample standard deviation (ddof = 1).
fn sample_sd(x: &[f64]) -> f64 {
    let n = x.len() as f64;
    let m = x.iter().sum::<f64>() / n;
    (x.iter().map(|v| (v - m).powi(2)).sum::<f64>() / (n - 1.0)).sqrt()
}

fn main() {
    let mut rng = Rng::new(0);
    let population_sd = 12.0;

    for n in [25, 100, 400, 1600] {

        let sd = sample_sd(&sample);    // spread of the MEASUREMENTS

        println!("{} sd: {}  se: {}  95% CI half width: {}",
                 n, round(sd, 2), round(se, 3), round(1.96 * se, 3));
    }

    // n=25   sd 11.56  se 2.313  CI half width 4.533

What a confidence interval actually means

What "95% confident" really means

Twenty surveys, twenty nets thrown at a fish you cannot see.

The true pass rate is 60%, but you never get to see it. You run one survey of 200 tasks and build an interval around your result. Think of the interval as a net thrown at a hidden fish.

Now repeat the whole survey 20 times with fresh samples. Each one throws its own net. Most land across the truth. One, in red, misses.

That is all "95%" means: the method catches the truth in about 19 of 20 throws. It does not mean a 95% chance the truth is inside your particular net; that net either caught it or did not.

To make the net smaller without missing more often, you need more data. Quadrupling the tasks halves the width, the same square-root law as the polls.

In plain words
A 95% confidence interval is produced by a procedure that captures the true value 95 times out of 100. It is not a 95% chance the truth is inside the one interval in front of you. The truth is a fixed number; the interval is the thing that moves from experiment to experiment.
Under the hoodThe textbook interval and the Wilson interval
\[ \bar{x} \;\pm\; 1.96 \cdot \frac{s}{\sqrt{n}} \qquad\qquad \frac{\hat{p} + \frac{z^2}{2n} \;\pm\; z\sqrt{\frac{\hat{p}(1-\hat{p})}{n} + \frac{z^2}{4n^2}}}{1 + \frac{z^2}{n}} \]Left: the textbook interval for a mean. Right: Wilson, for a proportion \(\hat p = k/n\). Use Wilson for pass rates: the textbook version breaks near 0 and 1, and can hand you an upper bound above 100 percent.
import math

def wilson(k: int, n: int, z: float = 1.96):
    """Interval for a pass rate. k passed out of n."""
    if n == 0:
        return (0.0, 1.0)
    p = k / n
    den = 1 + z * z / n                       # shrink factor, -> 1 as n grows

    # The centre is NOT p. It is pulled toward 0.5 by z^2/(2n), and that pull
    # is what keeps the interval inside [0, 1] at extreme rates.
    centre = (p + z * z / (2 * n)) / den

    half = z * math.sqrt(p * (1 - p) / n + z * z / (4 * n * n)) / den
    return (max(0.0, centre - half), min(1.0, centre + half))


def naive(k, n, z=1.96):
    p = k / n
    h = z * math.sqrt(p * (1 - p) / n)
    return (p - h, p + h)                     # note: no clamping

print(wilson(49, 50))    # (0.8950, 0.9965)  stays inside [0, 1]
print(naive(49, 50))     # (0.9412, 1.0188)  claims a rate above 100%
/// Interval for a pass rate. k passed out of n.
fn wilson(k: u32, n: u32, z: f64) -> (f64, f64) {
    if n == 0 {
        return (0.0, 1.0);
    }
    let (k, n) = (k as f64, n as f64);
    let p = k / n;
    let den = 1.0 + z * z / n; // shrink factor, -> 1 as n grows

    // The centre is NOT p. It is pulled toward 0.5 by z^2/(2n), and that pull
    // is what keeps the interval inside [0, 1] at extreme rates.
    let centre = (p + z * z / (2.0 * n)) / den;

    let half = z * (p * (1.0 - p) / n + z * z / (4.0 * n * n)).sqrt() / den;
    ((centre - half).max(0.0), (centre + half).min(1.0))
}

fn naive(k: u32, n: u32, z: f64) -> (f64, f64) {
    let p = k as f64 / n as f64;
    let h = z * (p * (1.0 - p) / n as f64).sqrt();
    (p - h, p + h) // note: no clamping
}

fn main() {
    println!("{:?}", wilson(49, 50, 1.96)); // (0.8950, 0.9965)  stays inside [0, 1]
    println!("{:?}", naive(49, 50, 1.96));  // (0.9412, 1.0188)  claims a rate above 100%
}

What a p value is, and the four things it is not

A p value answers exactly one question: if there were genuinely no difference, how often would I see a result at least this extreme by chance alone? Small p means your result would be surprising in a world with no real effect.

\[ p = P\big(T \ge t_{\text{obs}} \;\big|\; H_0 \text{ true}\big) \]The probability of a result at least this extreme, assuming there is no real effect. Note which side of the bar the assumption sits on.
  • It is not the probability that the null hypothesis is true.
  • It is not the probability your result was a fluke.
  • It is not a measure of effect size. A tiny, useless difference gets a tiny p value with enough data.
  • p = 0.06 is not "no effect". It is slightly weaker evidence than p = 0.04. The 0.05 line is a convention, not a law of nature.
Key idea
Always report the effect size next to the p value. "Model B is better, p = 0.003" is nearly useless on its own. "Model B passes 4.2 points more, 95% CI [1.1, 7.3], p = 0.003" tells someone whether to ship.
Under the hoodA useless effect becomes "significant" with enough data

/// ln Gamma(x) by the Lanczos approximation (g = 7, 9 terms).
fn ln_gamma(x: f64) -> f64 {
    const G: [f64; 9] = [
        0.999_999_999_999_809_93, 676.520_368_121_885_1, -1259.139_216_722_402_8,
        771.323_428_777_653_13, -176.615_029_162_140_59, 12.507_343_278_686_905,
        -0.138_571_095_265_720_12, 9.984_369_578_019_571_6e-6, 1.505_632_735_149_311_6e-7,
    ];
    let pi = std::f64::consts::PI;
    if x < 0.5 {
        return (pi / (pi * x).sin()).ln() - ln_gamma(1.0 - x); // reflection
    }
    let x = x - 1.0;
    let t = x + 7.5;
    let a = G[0] + (1..9).map(|i| G[i] / (x + i as f64)).sum::<f64>();
    0.5 * (2.0 * pi).ln() + (x + 0.5) * t.ln() - t + a.ln()
}

fn ln_choose(n: u64, k: u64) -> f64 {
    ln_gamma(n as f64 + 1.0) - ln_gamma(k as f64 + 1.0) - ln_gamma((n - k) as f64 + 1.0)
}

/// Two-sided Fisher exact test on [[a, b], [c, d]]: add up every table with the
/// same margins that is no more likely than the one observed.
fn fisher_exact(t: [[u64; 2]; 2]) -> f64 {
    let [[a, b], [c, d]] = t;
    let (row1, col1, n) = (a + b, a + c, a + b + c + d);
    let ln_pmf = |x: u64| ln_choose(row1, x) + ln_choose(n - row1, col1 - x) - ln_choose(n, col1);
    let cutoff = ln_pmf(a) + 1e-7_f64.ln_1p(); // small tolerance for rounding
    let lo = col1.saturating_sub(n - row1);
    let hi = row1.min(col1);
    let p: f64 = (lo..=hi).map(ln_pmf).filter(|&l| l <= cutoff).map(f64::exp).sum();
    p.min(1.0)
}

fn round(x: f64, digits: i32) -> f64 {
    let f = 10f64.powi(digits);
    (x * f).round() / f
}

// A tiny, useless difference. 0.5 percentage points.
fn experiment(rng: &mut Rng, n: u64) -> f64 {
    let a = (0..n).filter(|_| rng.random() < 0.605).count() as u64;
    let b = (0..n).filter(|_| rng.random() < 0.600).count() as u64;
    fisher_exact([[a, n - a], [b, n - b]])
}

For leadersWhat these two ideas let you demand

The sampling distribution is the reason an eval number is never just a number. It comes with a margin, and that margin decides whether a result can carry a decision.

  • No interval, no decision. A pass rate without a confidence interval cannot support a ship call. On 200 tasks at around 60%, the 95% interval is roughly ±7 points. Two models 3 points apart on that suite are usually indistinguishable unless the comparison is paired (Part II covers how pairing shrinks the margin).
  • Budget with the square-root law. Halving the error bar costs four times the eval compute and four times the labelling. Detecting a 1 point change typically needs thousands of tasks. Put that in planning documents before someone promises "we'll know by Friday".
  • Separate "real" from "matters". A p value says whether a difference is likely real. It says nothing about whether it is big enough to care about. Ask for the effect size and its interval, and agree in advance on the smallest difference worth acting on.
  • Watch for SD and SE swaps. Error bars drawn with the standard deviation look alarmingly wide; error bars drawn with the standard error look reassuringly tight. Both are legitimate for different questions, and dashboards frequently mix them up.
Principal asks
"If we reran this eval on a fresh draw of tasks, how much would the headline move?" A team that can answer with a number, and knows where it came from, understands its own result. A team that cannot has a point estimate and a hope.
