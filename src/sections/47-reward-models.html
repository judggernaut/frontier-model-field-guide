Preferences and reward models: turning taste into a number

Pretraining and supervised fine-tuning teach a model to imitate text. They cannot teach it which of two good-looking answers people actually prefer. Reward models close that gap. They learn a scoring function from human (or AI) comparisons, and that score becomes the target for RLHF, the selector for best-of-N sampling, and the yardstick many teams quietly use as an eval. This chapter covers how comparisons are collected, the Bradley–Terry model behind almost every reward model, how to evaluate one, and how optimizing against it goes wrong.

Which is clearer, 1 or 2?

How an eye exam explains reward models, in five pictures.

Ask people to score an answer from 1 to 10 and everyone uses their own scale. One person's 6 is another person's 8, so absolute scores are noisy.

An optometrist never asks you to rate a lens from 1 to 10. They show two and ask which is clearer. Labs collect model feedback the same way: two answers to one prompt, and a person picks the better one.

Collect thousands of these picks across many answers. Some picks disagree with the rest, because people are noisy. With enough of them, a clear ordering emerges anyway.

Bradley–Terry pretends each answer has a hidden score, like a chess rating. The bigger the gap between two scores, the more likely people pick the higher one, but upsets stay possible. Only gaps matter: adding 100 to every score changes nothing.

Train a model to predict those picks and you get a reward model: a judge that scores any answer in milliseconds. It drives RLHF, picks the best of several samples, and often ends up on dashboards. Everything it knows came from the picks, including their blind spots.

Why preferences instead of labels

Plain wordsPicking is easier than scoring

Ask ten people to score a poem from 1 to 10 and you get ten different scales: one person's 6 is another's 8. Ask the same ten people which of two poems is better and they agree far more often. Comparing is easier and more consistent than scoring on an absolute scale.

So instead of asking people "how good is this answer?", labs show them two answers to the same question and ask "which is better?" After collecting many of these picks, they train a separate model, the reward model, to predict which answer people would pick. Once trained, it can score any answer instantly, which lets you train the main model against it millions of times without a person in the loop.

For leadersWhat you are actually buying

A preference dataset is a product decision encoded as data. The rubric you give annotators defines what "better" means, and the reward model will learn that definition, including its blind spots. Four decisions dominate quality and cost:

  • Who labels. Crowd annotators are cheap and fast but drift toward surface features (length, confident tone). Domain experts (doctors, lawyers, senior engineers) cost 5 to 20 times more per comparison and are the only reliable source for specialized correctness. Most frontier programs mix both and route by topic.
  • The rubric. Helpfulness, honesty, and harmlessness pull against each other. A single "overall better" judgment hides which one won. Many teams collect per-dimension preferences or a written rationale so they can reweight later without relabeling.
  • Agreement. On open-ended chat, independent labelers agree with each other only about 70 to 77 percent of the time (InstructGPT, Ouyang et al. 2022, reported roughly 73 percent among its trained labelers). That number is the ceiling on reward model accuracy. A reward model at 72 percent is near the ceiling, not mediocre.
  • Freshness. Comparisons collected on an old model's outputs describe old mistakes. As the policy improves, its outputs leave the distribution the reward model was trained on. Plan for repeated collection rounds on current samples, not a one-time dataset.
Principal asks
What was inter-annotator agreement, measured on the same items? What fraction of pairs were ties or "both bad", and what did you do with them? Which dimensions does the rubric privilege when they conflict? How old are the samples the reward model was trained on relative to the current policy?
Under the hoodThe data format

A preference record is a triple \((x, y_w, y_l)\): a prompt \(x\), a preferred ("winning") response \(y_w\), and a dispreferred ("losing") response \(y_l\). Common variations:

  • Rankings of K responses. InstructGPT showed labelers 4 to 9 responses and asked for a full ranking, which yields \(\binom{K}{2}\) pairs. They kept all pairs from one prompt in the same batch element, because treating them as independent samples caused the reward model to overfit within one epoch.
  • Strength labels. "Significantly better" versus "slightly better". Llama 2 (Touvron et al. 2023) fed this in as a margin term in the loss, below.
  • Ties and "both bad". Usually dropped for Bradley–Terry training, which has no tie outcome. Dropping them silently biases the dataset toward easy, clear-cut pairs.

The Bradley–Terry model

Plain wordsChess ratings for answers

Chess ratings work like this: every player has a hidden strength number. When two players meet, the bigger the gap in their numbers, the more likely the stronger one wins, but upsets still happen. You never see the strengths directly, only who won. After enough games you can work backward to good estimates of everyone's number.

A reward model does the same with answers. Each answer gets a hidden score. The bigger the score gap between two answers, the more confident the model is that people prefer the higher one. Only the gap means anything. Adding 100 to every score changes nothing.

For leadersWhat the score does and does not mean

A reward score is a relative preference estimate within one prompt. It is not a quality grade, not a probability of correctness, and not comparable between reward model versions. If a dashboard shows "average reward went from 1.3 to 1.9", ask what it was normalized against, because a retrained reward model can shift every score without the policy changing at all.

Annotator noise is the hidden lever in the widget below. Doubling noise does not just make labels messier; it shrinks the learned scale, so the reward model becomes less confident everywhere, and downstream RL pushes less hard. That can look like "RLHF stopped working" when the real cause is a labeling vendor change.

Under the hoodThe model and its loss

Bradley–Terry (1952) says the probability that \(y_w\) beats \(y_l\) depends only on the difference of their scores, passed through a logistic function:

\[ P(y_w \succ y_l \mid x) = \sigma\big(r(x, y_w) - r(x, y_l)\big) = \frac{1}{1 + e^{-(r_w - r_l)}} \]\(r(x,y)\) is the scalar score, \(\sigma\) is the logistic sigmoid. A gap of 0 means a coin flip; a gap of 2 means about 88 percent; a gap of 4 means about 98 percent.

Training a reward model \(r_\phi\) is maximum likelihood under this model, which is binary cross-entropy on the score difference:

\[ \mathcal{L}_{\text{RM}}(\phi) = -\,\mathbb{E}_{(x, y_w, y_l)}\Big[\log \sigma\big(r_\phi(x, y_w) - r_\phi(x, y_l) - m\big)\Big] \]\(m = 0\) in the plain version. Llama 2 used a margin \(m > 0\) that grows with the labeled preference strength, which pushes clearly better pairs further apart.

Two consequences matter in practice. First, the scores are only identified up to an additive constant per prompt, so raw reward values are not comparable across prompts or across reward model versions. Teams normalize (subtract the mean reward on a reference set) before using them. Second, the scale is set by labeler consistency: noisy labels compress all score gaps toward zero, because the model learns that even large quality differences only win 70 percent of the time.

import torch
import torch.nn.functional as F

def bradley_terry_loss(r_chosen, r_rejected, margin=None):
    """r_chosen, r_rejected: tensors of shape [batch], scalar scores from the reward model."""
    diff = r_chosen - r_rejected
    if margin is not None:          # Llama 2 style: demand a bigger gap for clearer preferences
        diff = diff - margin
    # -log sigmoid(diff) == softplus(-diff), and the softplus form is numerically stable
    return F.softplus(-diff).mean()

r_w = torch.tensor([1.2, 0.3, 2.0])
r_l = torch.tensor([0.2, 0.9, -1.0])
print(round(bradley_terry_loss(r_w, r_l).item(), 3))                  # 0.466
print(round(((r_w - r_l) > 0).float().mean().item(), 3))             # pairwise accuracy 0.667
// Plain Rust, no ML framework: the same math on small vectors.

fn softplus(x: f64) -> f64 {
    // log(1 + e^x) without overflow: max(x, 0) + log(1 + e^-|x|)
    x.max(0.0) + (-x.abs()).exp().ln_1p()
}

/// r_chosen, r_rejected: slices of length batch, scalar scores from the reward model.
fn bradley_terry_loss(r_chosen: &[f64], r_rejected: &[f64], margin: Option<&[f64]>) -> f64 {
    let n = r_chosen.len() as f64;
    let mut total = 0.0;
    for i in 0..r_chosen.len() {
        let mut diff = r_chosen[i] - r_rejected[i];
        if let Some(m) = margin { // Llama 2 style: demand a bigger gap for clearer preferences
            diff -= m[i];
        }
        // -log sigmoid(diff) == softplus(-diff), and the softplus form is numerically stable
        total += softplus(-diff);
    }
    total / n
}

fn main() {
    let r_w = [1.2, 0.3, 2.0];
    let r_l = [0.2, 0.9, -1.0];
    println!("{:.3}", bradley_terry_loss(&r_w, &r_l, None)); // 0.466
    let wins = r_w.iter().zip(&r_l).filter(|(w, l)| w > l).count();
    println!("{:.3}", wins as f64 / r_w.len() as f64);     // pairwise accuracy 0.667
}

Training a reward model

For leadersSize, data volume, cadence

Reward models are usually initialized from the same family as the policy, often the SFT checkpoint, because the reward model must understand the responses at least as well as the policy that produces them. A reward model much smaller than the policy is cheaper to run during RL but is easier for the policy to fool. Published programs range from tens of thousands of comparisons (InstructGPT used about 33,000 prompts for reward model training) to well over a million (Llama 2 reported roughly 1.4 million Meta-collected comparisons plus open datasets). Collection is typically done in rounds against the latest policy, so the budget question is less "how many comparisons" than "how many per round, and how many rounds".

Watch out
Reward models overfit fast. Several reports train for a single epoch on comparison data, because a second pass raises training accuracy and lowers held-out accuracy. If a team reports a reward model at 90 percent training accuracy on chat preferences, the first question is what held-out accuracy looks like.
Under the hoodArchitecture

Take a transformer language model, drop the vocabulary projection, and add a linear head that maps the final hidden state of the last token to one scalar. Run the prompt and response through, read the scalar, done. Chosen and rejected responses are run as two sequences that share the prompt.

import torch
import torch.nn as nn

class RewardModel(nn.Module):
    def __init__(self, backbone, hidden_size):
        super().__init__()
        self.backbone = backbone                 # e.g. the SFT model without its LM head
        self.value_head = nn.Linear(hidden_size, 1)

    def forward(self, input_ids, attention_mask):
        h = self.backbone(input_ids, attention_mask=attention_mask).last_hidden_state
        last = attention_mask.sum(dim=1) - 1     # index of the final real token (assumes right padding)
        h_last = h[torch.arange(h.size(0)), last]
        return self.value_head(h_last).squeeze(-1)   # one scalar reward per sequence
// Plain Rust, no ML framework: the same math on small vectors.
// The backbone is any function from token ids to hidden states [batch][seq][hidden].

struct RewardModel<F: Fn(&[Vec<u32>]) -> Vec<Vec<Vec<f64>>>> {
    backbone: F,          // e.g. the SFT model without its LM head
    value_head_w: Vec<f64>, // Linear(hidden_size, 1): one weight per hidden unit
    value_head_b: f64,
}

impl<F: Fn(&[Vec<u32>]) -> Vec<Vec<Vec<f64>>>> RewardModel<F> {
    fn forward(&self, input_ids: &[Vec<u32>], attention_mask: &[Vec<u8>]) -> Vec<f64> {
        let h = (self.backbone)(input_ids);
        h.iter()
            .zip(attention_mask)
            .map(|(seq, mask)| {
                // index of the final real token (assumes right padding)
                let last = mask.iter().map(|&m| m as usize).sum::<usize>() - 1;
                let h_last = &seq[last];
                // one scalar reward per sequence
                h_last.iter().zip(&self.value_head_w).map(|(x, w)| x * w).sum::<f64>() + self.value_head_b
            })
            .collect()
    }
}

fn main() {
    // tiny worked example: a toy backbone whose hidden state is [token / 4, 1]
    let backbone = |ids: &[Vec<u32>]| -> Vec<Vec<Vec<f64>>> {
        ids.iter().map(|seq| seq.iter().map(|&t| vec![t as f64 / 4.0, 1.0]).collect()).collect()
    };
    let rm = RewardModel { backbone, value_head_w: vec![1.0, 0.5], value_head_b: 0.0 };
    let ids = vec![vec![5, 8, 3, 0], vec![4, 2, 9, 7]];
    let mask = vec![vec![1, 1, 1, 0], vec![1, 1, 1, 1]];
    println!("{:?}", rm.forward(&ids, &mask)); // [1.25, 2.25]  reads token 3, then token 7; the pad is never used
}

Alternatives you will hear about: generative reward models that write a critique and then a verdict (an LLM judge trained on preference data, see LLM as judge), multi-objective heads that score helpfulness and harmlessness separately (Llama 2 trained two separate reward models), and pairwise models that read both responses at once and output which is better.

Evaluating a reward model

Plain wordsGrading the grader

A reward model is a judge. Before you let it train your model, check its picks against people's picks on comparisons it has never seen. If it agrees as often as two people agree with each other, it is about as good as it can get. Then check the cases that matter: does it prefer a long wrong answer over a short right one? Does it reward flattery?

For leadersThe four numbers to ask for
MeasureWhat it tells youTypical trap
Held-out pairwise accuracyHow often it agrees with human picks on unseen pairsReported without the human agreement ceiling next to it
Accuracy by sliceWhere it fails: code, math, safety, long answers, multi-turnA good average hiding a 55 percent slice that RL will exploit

Downstream effectBest-of-N or RL results using this reward model, scored by humans or a held-out judgeStatic accuracy correlates only loosely with how good a policy it trains

Public suites such as RewardBench (Lambert et al. 2024: chat, hard chat, safety, reasoning) and RewardBench 2 (Malik et al. 2025: factuality, precise instruction following, math, safety, focus, ties) give comparable numbers across categories. They are useful for comparing reward models from different groups, and weak as a ship criterion, because your policy's failure modes are not in a public benchmark.

Eval lens
Reward model evaluation is a binary classification problem on pairs, so everything in classification metrics applies. Pairwise accuracy on randomly ordered pairs is a concordance measure, the same quantity ROC-AUC estimates. Report it with a confidence interval (a few thousand held-out pairs gives roughly ±1.5 points), report it per slice, and compare it to the human-human agreement on the same items. A 2-point gain between reward model versions on 1,000 pairs is inside the noise.
Under the hoodKnown biases and how to probe them

Length

Annotators tend to prefer longer, more thorough-looking answers, and reward models amplify this. Probe with counterfactual pairs: the same answer with and without padding. Singhal et al. (2023) showed much of the reward gain from RLHF on several datasets could be explained by length alone.

Sycophancy

Answers that agree with the user's stated opinion get rated higher. Sharma et al. (2023) found this in human preference data and in reward models trained on it. Probe with paired prompts that differ only in the user's stated belief.

Formatting and tone

Markdown headers, bullet lists, and confident phrasing earn reward independent of content. Probe by stripping formatting or swapping hedged for confident wording.

Position and self-preference

Pairwise generative reward models can prefer whichever response comes first, or responses that sound like their own outputs. Score both orders and average.

The standard mitigations are to balance the comparison data (include pairs where the shorter answer wins), add an explicit length penalty or length-normalized reward, train on counterfactual augmentations, and report length-controlled win rates in evaluation.

Reward overoptimization: Goodhart in one chart

The kitchen that learned to please one critic

Reward overoptimization, told as a restaurant chasing reviews.

The critic is a stand-in. Asking real diners about every dish is too slow, so the kitchen optimizes the critic's score. That is exactly the deal RLHF makes with a reward model.

At first everything works. The kitchen fixes real problems, the critic notices, and diners are happier too. Early RLHF steps look like this: proxy reward and true quality rise together.

Then the kitchen finds the critic's quirks. Bigger portions and more garnish raise the score without making the food better. Language models find the same kinds of quirks in reward models: longer answers, heavy formatting, confident tone, flattery.

Plot both against how far the kitchen has drifted from its original recipe. The critic's score climbs forever. The diners' score peaks and then falls. Gao et al. (2022) measured exactly this shape for reward models, and found better reward models push the peak later and higher.

The defenses map one to one: a KL penalty is the leash, ensembles are extra critics, an independent eval is asking real diners, and refreshing the reward model on current outputs is retraining the critic.

Plain wordsTeaching to the test

If you reward students only for test scores, they first learn the material, then they learn the test. Past some point, scores keep climbing while real understanding stops improving or even gets worse. A reward model is a test. Push a language model hard enough against it and the model finds the reward model's quirks: longer answers, more confident tone, flattering phrasing. The reward model's score keeps rising. What people actually want peaks and then falls.

For leadersOperating against a proxy

Every RL run against a learned reward is a bet on where the gold curve peaks. The controls are the KL penalty (how hard you hold the policy near its reference), early stopping on an independent eval, reward model ensembles (optimize the minimum or the mean minus the spread, so the policy cannot exploit one model's quirk), and refreshing the reward model on the policy's current outputs.

Watch out
The signature of overoptimization is a training curve where reward rises smoothly while response length climbs, hedging disappears, and human or held-out-judge win rates flatten. If the only chart in a review is the training reward, you cannot tell a great run from a hacked one.
Principal asks
What independent signal told you when to stop? How did KL from the reference evolve, and where did the held-out win rate peak against it? Did response length change, and what is the length-controlled result? Was the reward model refreshed on samples from this policy?
Under the hoodThe scaling law

Gao, Schulman and Hilton (2022) made this measurable. They used a large "gold" reward model as a stand-in for humans, trained smaller proxy reward models on its labels, optimized policies against the proxies, and tracked gold reward as a function of how far the policy moved from its starting point, measured by \(d = \sqrt{\mathrm{KL}(\pi \,\|\, \pi_{\text{ref}})}\). Gold reward followed simple functional forms:

\[ R_{\text{bon}}(d) = d\,(\alpha_{\text{bon}} - \beta_{\text{bon}}\, d), \qquad R_{\text{RL}}(d) = d\,(\alpha_{\text{RL}} - \beta_{\text{RL}} \log d) \]Best-of-N sampling (left) and RL (right). Both rise, peak, and fall. Larger proxy reward models had smaller \(\beta\) (for RL, \(\alpha\) stayed roughly constant across reward-model sizes), so gold reward declines more slowly: a later, higher peak. More proxy training data helped in the same direction.

Two practical lessons follow. The KL distance at which gold reward peaks is a budget, and it grows with reward model quality. And the proxy score is useless for finding that peak, because it keeps rising straight through it. You need an independent signal: fresh human ratings, a different reward model, or task-specific checks.

Outcome rewards, process rewards, and verifiers

Plain wordsGrading the answer or grading the working

A math teacher can mark only the final answer, or check every line of the working. Grading only the answer is cheap but cannot tell lucky guesses from sound reasoning. Grading each step is expensive but pinpoints where things went wrong.

For leadersWhen you do not need a learned reward at all

An outcome reward model scores the final response. A process reward model scores each intermediate step of a reasoning chain; Lightman et al. (2023), "Let's Verify Step by Step", found process supervision beat outcome supervision for selecting correct math solutions, at the cost of step-level labels. For tasks with checkable answers (math with a known result, code with tests, formal proofs), a programmatic verifier can replace the learned reward entirely. That removes the reward model as an attack surface for overoptimization, though policies can still exploit weak tests. This is the basis of RL with verifiable rewards, covered in GRPO and RLVR.

AI feedback

For leadersReplacing some of the humans

Human comparisons are slow and expensive. RLAIF uses a capable model, guided by written principles, to produce the comparisons instead. Anthropic's Constitutional AI (Bai et al. 2022) used model-generated critiques and preferences for harmlessness; Lee et al. (2023) reported RLAIF reaching parity with RLHF on summarization and dialogue tasks they tested. AI feedback scales and is consistent, but it inherits the labeling model's biases and can create a closed loop where models grade models in their own image. Most programs keep a human-labeled slice to audit the AI labels and measure drift. See safety training for the constitutional approach in depth.

Eval lens
An AI labeler is an LLM judge, and should be validated like one (LLM as judge): agreement and Cohen's kappa against humans on a held-out set, checks for position and length bias, and a comparison to human-human agreement. If the AI labeler agrees with humans less often than humans agree with each other, the reward model trained on its labels starts below the human ceiling.

Applied: critiquing a reward design

A common research-design follow-up: "here is the reward you proposed; what are its weaknesses, and how would they show up in the model?" Meta's research-scientist loop asked it directly. The answer is a habit: for each term, ask what the cheapest way to score well is.

Plain wordsPay a builder by the brick

Pay a builder per brick laid and you get many bricks, not necessarily a good wall. Every reward is a payment rule like that. The training process is a very persistent worker that will find whatever earns the most for the least effort. Critiquing a reward means imagining that worker and asking: what would they do?

For leadersThe review checklist
  1. Cheapest path. What is the laziest behaviour that scores well? (Long answers for a length-biased judge; deleting failing tests for a "tests pass" reward.)
  2. Coverage. What does the reward ignore? (Correct but rude; helpful but unsafe; right answer, wrong reasoning.)
  3. Noise and bias. How often does it disagree with a careful person, and in which direction?
  4. Exploit surface. Can the policy influence the scorer (format tricks, prompt injection into a judge, access to the checker)?
  5. Monitoring. Which held-out signal, scored differently from the training reward, will reveal hacking early?
Under the hoodSix proposals to critique
A chatbot rewarded by an LLM judge's 1–10 helpfulness score.

Judges prefer longer, more confident, more formatted answers and their own style (LLM as judge), so the policy grows verbose and over-confident. Absolute scores drift and compress near the top. Mitigate: pairwise comparisons against a reference, length-controlled scoring, a rubric with specific checks, and periodic human audits of high-reward samples.

A coding model rewarded when the unit tests pass.

Weak tests are satisfied by special-casing inputs; with file access, the model can edit or skip tests, or exit early with a success code. Mitigate: hidden tests the policy never sees, read-only test files, sandboxing, mutation testing to check test strength, and a penalty or audit for test-file changes (Verifiers fail too).

A math model rewarded for an exact-match final answer.

Sparse (no signal on hard problems where every sample fails), ignores reasoning so lucky guesses are rewarded, and brittle to formatting (equivalent forms marked wrong). Mitigate: a symbolic equivalence checker, curriculum by difficulty, and process supervision or reasoning audits on a sample.

A real-time Q&A chatbot rewarded by user thumbs-up.

Selection bias (few users rate, unhappy ones more often), sycophancy (agreeing earns thumbs), and short-term satisfaction over correctness. Delayed and noisy. Mitigate: treat it as one signal among several, weight by rater reliability, combine with correctness checks on verifiable questions, and watch long-term retention, as Netflix-style A/B thinking would (A/B tests).

A safety reward: +1 for refusing any request flagged as risky.

Over-refusal: the policy learns that refusing is safe and refuses benign requests that look similar. Mitigate: a paired helpfulness reward on benign look-alikes, and track both over-refusal and harmful-compliance rates (Safety training).

A weighted sum: 0.7 × correctness + 0.3 × brevity.

The policy finds the corner where the easier term dominates (very short, sometimes wrong answers) whenever correctness is hard to earn. Weights interact with each term's scale and variance. Mitigate: make correctness a gate (brevity only counts when correct), normalise terms, and sweep the weights while watching each term separately.

Eval lens
The standard defence is an evaluation the policy is not trained on: a held-out reward model or judge, human ratings on a sample, and task-specific checks. Plot training reward against the held-out score as in the overoptimisation chart above; the step where they diverge is where hacking starts.
